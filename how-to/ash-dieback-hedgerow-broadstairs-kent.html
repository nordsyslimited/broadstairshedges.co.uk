Ash dieback in a Broadstairs hedgerow: how to identify it, assess what to retain and plan replacement planting for CT10.

Ash (Fraxinus excelsior) is one of the three most common hedgerow trees in Thanet and across east Kent, alongside oak and field maple. Since Hymenoscyphus fraxineus (also called Chalara dieback, from its earlier asexual-stage name Chalara fraxinea) arrived in the UK in 2012 and was confirmed in east Kent woodland within the following two years, it has worked progressively through the region's ash population. There is no cure. Forestry Research estimates that between 70 and 80 per cent of UK ash trees will eventually be killed by the disease; a proportion of the remainder appear to have some inherited tolerance and may survive in diminished form for decades. For anyone with ash growing in a hedge or field hedgerow in CT10, the question is no longer whether the disease will arrive but how to survey what is happening, identify which trees are salvageable, spot the ones that have become dangerous, and plan the gaps with species that will anchor the hedgerow structure for the next generation. This guide covers the full assessment and management cycle, in order.

Assessment and management steps

  1. Survey in the right window: late July to mid-September. Ash dieback symptoms are most visible and most reliably distinguishable from other problems during the main growing season, specifically from late July through to mid-September. By late July the current season's shoots are fully extended, so any that have wilted or died back from dieback infection stand out clearly against healthy green growth. The other diagnostic advantage of this window is timing relative to autumn leaf drop: ash naturally drops its leaves early compared with most UK deciduous trees, usually from late September onward, and the diseased leaves turn black and fall rather than the pale straw-yellow of normal autumn drop. If you survey too late, in October or November, the distinction between diseased and normal leaf fall disappears and you lose the main visual indicator. Walk the full hedgerow length in late July and repeat in September, noting the position of each ash and recording your observations against each one. A simple sketch map or numbered photograph sequence is enough; you do not need specialist equipment.
  2. Look for shoot dieback first: wilted black shoot tips, not browning ones. The clearest early symptom is the death of individual shoots from the tip inward, with the leaves turning black while still attached to the branch. This is an important distinction: leaves killed by drought stress or late frost damage tend to turn brown or russet; leaves killed by ash dieback turn black and may remain attached to the dead shoot for several weeks before falling. Look at the outer canopy from a slight distance first to get an overview of how much of the crown is affected, then inspect individual shoots closely. A dying shoot typically shows a sharp boundary between the dead, blackened tip section and the still-living stem below it; peeling back the bark just at and below that boundary often reveals an orange-brown to grey-brown discolouration of the wood and inner bark, which is the characteristic lesion that gives the disease its old alternative name, Chalara dieback. Early-stage trees may show only a few isolated dead shoot tips, often scattered through the outer canopy with no obvious pattern. Mid-stage trees show 20-50 per cent crown dieback, with entire outer limb-ends dying back while the main scaffold still carries some live growth.
  3. Check the base of the trunk for basal lesions. In trees where the disease has progressed beyond the outer shoots and into the main structural wood, characteristic lesions appear on the bark at the base of the trunk, typically within the lower 1 metre. These are elongated or diamond-shaped areas of darkened, olive-grey to black bark, sometimes weeping a dark fluid and sometimes accompanied by a slight sunken or puckered appearance of the bark surface. Basal lesions indicate that the fungus has established in the vascular tissue of the main stem and that the tree's ability to defend itself is significantly compromised. A tree showing both significant crown dieback and basal lesions is unlikely to survive, and the structural integrity of the main stem may already be affected: the internal wood beneath a basal lesion is often discoloured and softening even when the outer bark looks intact. Any ash showing basal lesions should be treated as a priority for safety assessment, particularly if the tree overhangs a path, road, or structure.
  4. Assess structural risk where any tree is near a path, road or building. Ash is structurally unpredictable once dieback is advanced. The wood may appear sound from the outside while being significantly colonised by secondary decay fungi that enter through the wounds created by the dieback. Ash failure under storm conditions is a known risk: in named storms in late autumn and winter, partially dead ash trees, particularly those where the main stem is weakened by basal lesions or where large dead limbs are present in the crown, have caused damage to property and road infrastructure. If any ash in the hedgerow is growing within falling distance of a road, the public highway, a property or a path regularly used by people, treat it as a potential safety liability rather than simply a diseased tree. Commission a specialist inspection (a Registered Arborist or someone with an NPTC/LANTRA trees qualification) for any tree showing 40 per cent or more crown dieback combined with its proximity to a target. Do not wait for the full decline cycle: a half-dead ash with a substantial dead limb overhead is a hazard now, not a future problem.
  5. Categorise each ash: monitor, watch, or plan for removal. After completing the survey, divide the ash trees in the hedgerow into three groups. Monitor: trees showing less than 20 per cent crown dieback, no basal lesions, and no structural concerns. These may be among the proportion of trees with some natural tolerance; they should be surveyed annually and not felled simply because the disease is confirmed. Some tolerant trees in heavily infected areas retain reasonable crown cover for ten or more years. Watch: trees with 20 to 40 per cent crown dieback, no basal lesions, and no immediate structural concern. These are likely declining but not yet dangerous; plan for phased management within two to three years, depending on rate of deterioration. Plan for removal: trees with more than 40 per cent crown dieback, any basal lesions, or structural concerns. These should be programmed for safe felling in the current or immediately following season, before winter storms increase the risk of uncontrolled failure.
  6. Carry out felling of prioritised trees, with a nesting check and legal clearance first. Before felling any ash tree from a hedgerow, carry out the nesting check below and satisfy yourself on the legal position (conservation area, Tree Preservation Order and hedgerow regulations: see the section later in this guide). Once clearance is confirmed, fell the tree at ground level or as a coppice stool if the tree is small enough that coppicing is realistic. Ash coppices readily in some circumstances, but a tree with significant dieback is not a good coppice candidate: the stool is likely to be infected and may not regenerate. For most infected hedgerow ash, a clean fell at ground level and a planned gap-fill with a replacement species is the right approach. Remove all felled material from site: do not leave cut ash stems and large branches in the hedgerow in a pile, as the material may carry fungal spores and damp piled wood encourages secondary decay fungi. Brash and smaller material can be retained on site in a dead-hedge if you are building one for wildlife, but keep it away from any living ash in the same hedgerow run. Confirm with the landowner or Thanet District Council whether removal of the stump is required before replanting into the same position.
  7. Plan the gap with a replacement native species suited to Margate Chalk. Each ash gap in a CT10 hedgerow is an opportunity to diversify the species mix. The chalk soils of Broadstairs, St Peter's and Reading Street are alkaline and free-draining: not every native hedgerow species performs well here. The following replacements are all well-suited to Margate Chalk conditions and provide good wildlife value as hedgerow components: field maple (Acer campestre), which is arguably the classic chalk-hedgerow tree and provides similar structural presence to ash in the hedgerow canopy; hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), excellent on chalk, dense structure, berry value; wild cherry (Prunus avium), fast-growing on chalk, good bird-food tree; spindle (Euonymus europaeus), a chalk specialist with striking autumn berries; hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), tolerates chalk once established, provides similar autumn colour to beech; dogwood (Cornus sanguinea), excellent for lower structure in the hedgerow gap, good chalk performer; hazel (Corylus avellana), fast gap-filler, chalk-tolerant, strong wildlife value. Use bare-root stock where possible, plant in autumn or early spring, and protect each plant with a spiral guard against rabbit and deer damage on sites where either is present. The gap-filling timing matters: a gap left through a second winter becomes increasingly difficult to close because the hedgerow edges grow toward each other. Plant in the first autumn or spring following felling.

Can you fell a hedgerow ash during the nesting season?

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. Hedgerow ash trees are used for nesting by blackbirds, song thrushes, garden warblers, dunnocks and long-tailed tits, among others. The practical nesting guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. Felling a hedgerow ash during this window without first checking for active nests risks committing an offence. If the tree poses an immediate and serious safety risk (a large dead limb over a road, a structurally failed stem), emergency felling can proceed but should be preceded by a visual check of the specific area of the tree to be worked: if no active nest is visible in the crown or in the immediate work zone, proceed carefully. For non-emergency felling, plan the work between 1 September and the end of February, when nesting risk is negligible. The penalty for intentionally destroying an active nest is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine per nest, or both on summary conviction.

Ash dieback in east Kent: scale and context

Ash dieback was first confirmed in the UK in 2012, initially in nursery stock and then in planted woodland in East Anglia. By 2013 it was confirmed in naturally regenerated woodland in England, and Kent was among the early affected counties: the county had confirmed infected sites in both managed woodland and hedgerow contexts within the first two years of the UK outbreak. Kent County Council produced a formal ash dieback action plan from 2013 onward, noting that ash was among the most numerous hedgerow trees in the county, with surveys from that period showing ash present in well over a third of Kentish hedgerows in some districts.

The Thanet area, including Broadstairs, St Peter's, Reading Street and Kingsgate, is predominantly arable chalk downland with fragmented hedgerow coverage compared with the more heavily hedged Weald or the North Kent marshes. The hedgerows that do exist tend to be mixed native field-boundary hedges along the rural margins of the town, particularly on the Reading Street and North Foreland fringes, in older garden boundaries, and along public footpaths. These hedgerows carry a mix of hawthorn, blackthorn, elder, field maple, hazel and ash. Where ash is present in these hedgerows, it is usually as either a layered hedgerow shrub (kept within the hedge by regular laying or cutting) or as a hedgerow tree that has been allowed to grow up through the hedge canopy. Both forms are susceptible.

Unlike box blight or fireblight, which can be contained or cut out, ash dieback cannot be treated or arrested. The fungus spreads via airborne spores released from apothecia (tiny white to cream disc-shaped fruiting bodies, 1-2 millimetres across) on dead ash leaf stalks on the ground in July through October. Spores travel on the wind and infect new growth directly. There is no registered fungicide treatment for the disease and no practical way to stop the spores reaching a tree in an open landscape. What can be managed is the timing of the response.

What tree tolerance means in practice

The Living Ash Project (livingashproject.org.uk), a research collaboration between Forest Research, the Woodland Trust and others, has been monitoring ash trees with apparent natural tolerance since 2013. Research confirms that a proportion of ash trees, possibly 1 to 5 per cent of the UK ash population, carry genetic variants that confer meaningful resistance to the disease. These trees continue to function in the landscape, may lose some crown cover but do not die, and are the basis for ongoing breeding work aimed at producing disease-resistant ash for future replanting. What this means for a Broadstairs hedgerow owner is practical: if you have an ash that is showing only mild dieback after several years of disease pressure and shows no sign of advancing decline, it may be one of the more tolerant individuals. Do not fell it pre-emptively. Survey it annually and retain it as long as it is not structurally compromised, since it represents the kind of genetic material that will seed the hedgerow's future ash component if any recovery is possible. By contrast, a tree with rapid progressive dieback from one season to the next is not demonstrating tolerance: it is demonstrating susceptibility. The rate of change is the most useful indicator.

Legal considerations before felling: conservation areas, TPOs and hedgerow regulations

Three separate legal frameworks can apply to hedgerow ash trees in Broadstairs, and all three need to be checked before felling anything of significant size.

Conservation areas. Broadstairs has four conservation areas: Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA and Kingsgate CA. Within any of these, any tree with a trunk diameter of 75 millimetres or more at 1.5 metres above ground level requires a section 211 notice to Thanet District Council at least six weeks before any work. This applies even to a diseased tree, and even if you intend to fell it on safety grounds. TDC routinely raises no objection to felling of confirmed diseased trees but the notice must be submitted; failing to do so carries a potential fine. The full conservation areas guide covers the s.211 process and the online TDC submission form. Submit the notice with a brief description of the disease symptoms and any safety concerns: TDC typically confirms no objection well within the six-week window in straightforward cases.

Tree Preservation Orders. Individual ash trees in CT10 may be subject to a TPO, which requires separate TDC consent before felling regardless of disease status. Check the TDC online mapping tool or contact TDC planning before work. As with conservation area notices, TDC will typically give consent quickly for a confirmed diseased tree, but the consent must be in place first.

Hedgerow Regulations 1997. If the hedgerow is a countryside boundary hedge on agricultural or equestrian land (as opposed to a garden hedge or urban boundary), the Hedgerow Regulations 1997 may protect it. Where the regulations apply and the hedgerow is classed as "important" under the criteria in the regulations (including age, species diversity and historical status), you need to submit a Hedgerow Removal Notice to TDC before removing it. Felling individual ash trees from within a hedgerow and replacing them with other species is generally considered management rather than removal, and does not trigger the notification requirement. However, if the volume of diseased ash in a hedgerow is such that the hedgerow character would be substantially changed by removing it all, the prudent approach is to discuss the position with TDC before proceeding. The hedgerow regulations guide covers the notification process and the importance criteria in detail.
