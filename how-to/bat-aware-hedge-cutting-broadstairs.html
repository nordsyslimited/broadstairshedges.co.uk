How to check a hedge for bats before cutting in Broadstairs: steps, timing and the Habitats Regulations for CT10.

Every UK bat species is a European Protected Species. That means disturbing a bat at its roost, or damaging a roost even when no bat is present, is a criminal offence under the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017, with no seasonal exemption and no de minimis threshold. Broadstairs hedges are higher-risk than most: old ivy-covered flint walls along clifftop gardens, dense mature evergreen screens on the Victorian terraces above Viking Bay, and the scrubby hedgerow edges of North Foreland and Kingsgate all provide exactly the shelter that pipistrelles and serotine bats use. This guide sets out the check steps, the signs to look for, and the safest timing window for CT10 hedge work.

Steps

Step 1: Decide whether the hedge is high-risk for a bat roost

Not every hedge in Broadstairs needs a full dusk check before cutting. The risk varies with what the hedge is made of and where it sits. Work through this quick assessment before booking a cut:

  • Ivy-covered sections. Dense ivy growing through or over a hedge, particularly over old flint walls or stone, is the single highest-risk feature. Ivy provides warm, sheltered cavities that pipistrelles and brown long-eared bats use as day roosts from April through October. Any hedge with significant ivy cover should be treated as high-risk.
  • Dense, mature evergreen hedges. Old leylandii, laurel, thuja and similar species with thick, uncut interiors create insulated cavities. The denser and less-managed the hedge, the higher the risk. A hedge that has not been cut for three or more years is more likely to harbour roosts than one on a regular annual schedule.
  • Mixed native hedges with old wood. Hawthorn, blackthorn or field maple hedges with thick basal growth, dead wood pockets or hollow stems provide access points that bats can exploit.
  • Proximity to known foraging routes. Bat foraging routes in Broadstairs run along the clifftop hedgerow belts from Stone Bay to Kingsgate, through the North Foreland scrub, and along the sheltered gardens behind Viking Bay. A hedge that sits on or directly adjacent to one of these corridors is more likely to support roosting bats than a heavily lit, exposed front garden hedge.
  • Hedges next to buildings with bat roosts. If the adjacent building already has known bat activity in the roof or soffits, the hedges in the same garden should be treated as high-risk regardless of their composition.

If none of these factors apply (a recently planted, regularly trimmed formal hedge with no ivy, no dead wood and no adjacent known roost), the risk is low and no dusk check is needed. If one or more applies, work through the remaining steps.

Step 2: Choose the right time of year before you do anything else

The safest cutting window for hedge work near potential bat roosts is November to February. During this period, UK bats are in hibernation or torpor: they roost in cool, stable places (often underground, in tree cavities or in buildings rather than surface hedges) and are far less likely to be present in an external hedge. Disturbance during hibernation is still an offence, but the probability of encountering an active roost in a surface hedge is significantly lower.

Avoid hedge cutting adjacent to any identified or suspected roost between late March and October. The maternity season, when female bats form nursery roosts to raise their pups, runs roughly from May to August. Cutting through a maternity roost in a hedge or ivy bank would be a serious criminal offence, with pups unable to escape. The BCT's practical guidance for contractors suggests avoiding all hedge cutting near potential roost features from April through October where there is any uncertainty.

What law applies to bats in hedges and ivy?

All UK bat species are European Protected Species under the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017. Regulation 43 makes it a criminal offence to deliberately capture, injure or kill a bat, to deliberately disturb a bat (including impairing its ability to survive, breed or rear young, or significantly affecting local distribution), or to deliberately damage or destroy a breeding site or resting place. Critically, the resting-place offence applies even when no bat is present at the time of the work: destroying an ivy bank known to be used as a roost is an offence regardless of whether a bat is in it on the day of cutting. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, s.9 adds a parallel domestic offence: it is unlawful to intentionally kill, injure or take a bat, or to intentionally disturb a bat occupying a structure or place used for shelter or protection. On summary conviction the penalty is an unlimited fine per offence. Prosecution is rare for accidental disturbance, but deliberate or reckless destruction of a known roost has resulted in substantial fines. If you are uncertain whether your hedge is used as a bat roost, the safest course is to get a check done before cutting.

Step 3: Carry out a dusk emergence check if the hedge is high-risk

A dusk emergence check is the standard method for identifying bat activity at a roost feature. It does not require any specialist equipment for a basic check, though a bat detector significantly increases confidence. The check takes roughly 30 to 45 minutes and must be carried out on a dry, calm evening with no strong wind, from about 20 minutes before official sunset until 30 to 40 minutes after. Cold nights (below about 8C) suppress bat activity and are not reliable for this check.

Stand at a point where you can see the full length of the hedge and any adjacent structure. Watch for bats emerging from the hedge itself (not just flying past overhead): a pipistrelle emerging from a roost will typically exit from a specific, consistent point, often a gap in dense ivy or a cavity at the junction of a hedge and a wall. Note the time of first emergence, the number of bats seen and the exit point. A single bat emerging from a consistent feature on two or more consecutive evenings is good evidence of a roost.

A bat detector, even a basic heterodyne model, helps confirm the species. Common pipistrelles call at around 45 kHz, soprano pipistrelles at around 55 kHz and serotines at around 25-30 kHz. Species identification is not required to trigger the legal protection: any bat at a roost gives rise to the same obligations.

Step 4: Look for physical evidence of roosting before cutting

On the day of a planned cut, carry out a physical inspection of any high-risk features identified in Step 1 before starting the machine:

  • Droppings. Bat droppings are small (3-7mm), dark brown or black, dry and crumbly (they do not smear like mouse droppings). They accumulate beneath a roost entrance point, often on a wall or on foliage beneath a cavity in ivy. Fresh droppings at the base of an ivy-covered hedge section are strong evidence of recent use.
  • Grease marks. Bats leave dark grease stains around the edges of a regularly used entry point. Look for a narrow, slightly stained opening in dense ivy or at a hedge-to-wall junction, particularly if the gap is 15-20mm wide (the minimum size a pipistrelle can pass through).
  • Insect activity and feeding remains. A pile of moth wings or insect fragments on a horizontal surface directly below a hedge feature is good evidence of a bat feeding roost in that spot.
  • Live bats. Do not physically search for bats inside the hedge. If a bat is encountered during the inspection, stop, do not disturb it, and contact the Bat Conservation Trust's National Bat Helpline (see Step 5).

Step 5: What to do if you find signs of bats

Stop the planned work. Do not cut the hedge or remove ivy that is in or directly adjacent to the suspected roost feature. Record the location, the type of evidence found and the date.

Contact the Bat Conservation Trust's National Bat Helpline on 0345 1300 228 (Mon-Fri, 9am-5pm). The BCT can advise on whether a licensed bat survey is needed before the work can proceed. For hedge cutting on private residential land, the BCT often advises that cutting can proceed in the winter months (November to February) provided the roost feature itself is not mechanically disturbed; always confirm this for your specific situation.

If the work involves more significant intervention (demolishing a wall that supports ivy used as a bat roost, or removing a large section of a hedge known to have a maternity roost), a formal survey by a licensed bat ecologist and a European Protected Species licence from Natural England may be required before work can proceed. This applies regardless of whether the hedge or wall sits inside a Broadstairs conservation area or not: the EPS protection is a separate layer from the s.211 conservation-area rules.

Step 6: Proceed with the cut, following the safest approach

If the inspection in Steps 1-4 found no evidence of bat use, or if the check was carried out in December to January and the hedge is a low-risk type, the cut can proceed. Work from outside the hedge canopy inward where possible: cutting and clearing with the machine rather than handling large sections of dense ivy by hand reduces the chance of disturbing any bat that might be inside.

Leave any section of ivy that covers an old flint wall or structural cavity untouched if there is any doubt. Cutting the hedge itself can proceed; the ivy section can be deferred to after a proper dusk check in spring, then addressed in September or October when pups are independent but bats are still active enough to relocate if disturbed. Never cut ivy against an old wall in May, June or July without a completed survey.

Bat species most likely in Broadstairs hedges

Kent supports 14 of the UK's 18 breeding bat species. The ones most likely to use hedges, ivy banks and wall-top scrub in CT10 are:

  • Common pipistrelle (Pipistrellus pipistrellus). The most widespread UK bat and the most frequently encountered in Broadstairs gardens. Roosts in crevices behind ivy, under bark and in wall cavities. Forages actively along hedgerow lines after dark. Active from about March through October.
  • Soprano pipistrelle (Pipistrellus pygmaeus). Similar size and habits to the common pipistrelle, with a slight preference for riparian and coastal scrub. Recorded around the North Foreland area and the scrubby hedgerow belts above Joss Bay and Kingsgate.
  • Serotine (Eptesicus serotinus). A large species strongly associated with the south-east of England. Serotines roost primarily in buildings (soffits, roof spaces) but forage along hedgerow corridors and can use dense hedges as day rest-stops during foraging excursions. Broadstairs and the wider Thanet peninsula sit within the core serotine range for England.
  • Brown long-eared bat (Plecotus auritus). Uses wooded hedgerow edges and the understorey of dense hedges to glean insects from foliage. Less likely to roost in a hedge itself but does use ivy-covered sections as night roost sites between foraging bouts.

Why Broadstairs hedges carry a higher bat risk than typical inland gardens

The Thanet Coast Site of Special Scientific Interest runs along the clifftop above Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate Bay and North Foreland. The SSSI designation reflects the habitat value of the chalk grassland, scrub and cliff-face communities: exactly the conditions that concentrate insect activity and therefore bat foraging. Clifftop hedges in CT10 sit at the edge of this SSSI, within or immediately adjacent to habitat that has been supporting bat populations since long before the town was built up.

The Victorian and Edwardian properties of Broadstairs and St Peter's CA also bring a building stock where bat roosts in soffit boards and roof spaces are common. When a building has an active bat colony, the garden hedges and ivy walls immediately adjacent are almost always used as foraging habitat and often as secondary shelter. If you have confirmed bats in your property, treat your garden hedges as a material consideration before any cutting work, not an afterthought.

Reading Street and Kingsgate CAs contain some of the most mature and least-cut hedgerow on the CT10 peninsula, which makes them the highest-risk areas for roost-bearing hedges in this part of Thanet. If you are working hedges in these areas, particularly thick, old, ivy-covered runs that have not been touched for several years, a dusk check is worth doing even in mid-November as a precaution.
