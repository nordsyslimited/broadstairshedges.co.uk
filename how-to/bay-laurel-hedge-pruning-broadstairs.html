How to prune a bay laurel hedge: secateurs-only technique, summer timing and the sucker rule for Broadstairs CT10.

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) is not related to cherry laurel or Portuguese laurel despite sharing the common name: it belongs to the Lauraceae family, not the Rosaceae, and comes from the Mediterranean. In Broadstairs and St Peter's it appears most often as a formally clipped hedge in older Victorian terraces, as a potted or planted standard trained into a pyramid or ball shape in front gardens and holiday-let entrances, or occasionally as a loose informal screen on a sheltered south-facing wall. The pruning rules are quite different from the other laurels: the most important is the tool rule. Bay laurel leaves are large enough that hedge trimmers and garden shears leave cut leaf edges that turn brown and remain visible for the rest of the season. Secateurs are not optional here; they are the correct tool for all routine work on a bay hedge. This guide covers the full process: choosing the cutting window, removing suckers, trimming each face with secateurs, managing renovation on a neglected hedge, and dealing with the bay sucker (the psyllid insect, not the root shoot) that causes leaf curling and yellowing across CT10.

Steps

  1. Choose the right cutting window. The main trimming period for bay laurel in CT10 runs from late May through late August. Within that range, June and July are the best months: the plant is in active growth, cuts callus quickly in warm weather, and the new growth pushed from cut stems has time to harden off before the first autumn frosts arrive on the east Kent coast, usually in October or November. A light tidy in late May (once the risk of a sharp overnight frost has passed for the season) is fine for removing winter-damaged tips and any frost-browned foliage; the main shaping cut is better left until June when the hedge is pushing strongly. Avoid cutting in August if the weather has been hot and dry without rain: bay under drought stress is more susceptible to tip scorch. Avoid any significant cutting from October through April in Broadstairs. Bay is rated RHS hardiness H3 to H4, meaning it can be damaged at temperatures below -5 °C to -10 °C depending on the form and provenance. In this part of Thanet, hard north-easterly frosts are uncommon but not unknown; a hedge cut in autumn or winter presents open wound surfaces to any frost that follows, and the combination can cause dieback well below the cut line. Spring renovation cuts are the exception: an April cut on a hedge that needs bringing back significantly is fine, as long as the forecast shows no frost returning.
  2. Walk the hedge and check for active nests before touching it between 1 March and 31 August. The nesting-season check applies to bay as much as it does to any densely-planted shrub. Bay laurel hedges in sheltered Broadstairs gardens provide good nesting cover for blackbirds, robins, dunnocks and house sparrows. Walk the full length of the hedge slowly before starting. Watch for adult birds repeatedly entering or leaving one section and listen for alarm calls. Part the outer foliage gently and look inside for a nest cup. If you find an occupied nest, leave that section uncut and return two to three weeks after the chicks have fledged. The legal position is in the box below.

Is it legal to trim a bay hedge in June?

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. The practical guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. The June to July main trimming window falls squarely inside this period, which is why the nest check above is not optional. Bay hedges in sheltered Broadstairs rear gardens are actively used for nesting from March onward. If you find any nest that could be in use, leave that section and return once the chicks have fledged. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine per nest, or both.

  1. Gather the correct tools: secateurs and bypass loppers only. This is the single most important tool rule for bay laurel, and it is different from the rules for cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel and most other hedging species. Hedge trimmers and garden shears slice through bay leaves at whatever angle the blade meets them, leaving a half-cut leaf edge that turns brown within a few days and remains visible and unsightly for the rest of the season. The RHS advises specifically against using hedge shears on bay because of this: the solution is secateurs, which cut individual stems cleanly at a node or junction, leaving no exposed leaf area. For stems up to roughly 1.5 centimetres across, a good pair of bypass secateurs handles the work without strain. For stems between 1.5 and 4 centimetres, bypass loppers are faster and reduce hand fatigue on a long run. Anything thicker than 4 centimetres requires a pruning saw: this is renovation territory rather than routine maintenance. Keep the blades clean and sharp; a bruised cut on bay causes more browning than a sharp, clean one. Wipe the blades with a clean cloth after each section if you are working in damp or high-humidity conditions.
  2. Remove sucker growth from the base before trimming the main hedge body. Bay laurel produces basal suckers readily: upright shoots that emerge from the root zone or from the base of the main stem at or just below ground level. On a formal clipped hedge, suckers left unmanaged widen the base of the hedge beyond its intended footprint and disrupt the uniform line of the face. On a standard (a lollipop or pyramid shape trained on a clear stem), suckers on the stem must be removed to maintain the shape. Pull suckers off by hand if they are small and newly emerged: a sharp pull at the base removes them with less regrowth potential than cutting, because pulling takes some of the base tissue with it. If a sucker is too thick to pull cleanly, cut it flush with the ground or flush with the main stem using secateurs. Do not leave stubs. Sucker removal is a year-round task on bay: check and remove as they appear rather than leaving them until the annual trim. Left for a full season, a sucker can develop into a substantial multi-stem shoot that requires loppers to remove and leaves a larger wound at the base.
  3. Trim the hedge faces using secateurs, working from the base upward on each face. Set a string line at the target height before starting, using canes driven at each end of the hedge run and a taut line run between them. Bay grows at a moderate rate on the sheltered chalk soils of Broadstairs and St Peter's: an established hedge typically extends 15 to 30 centimetres per year in a good season, so the amount to remove varies. Work systematically along the base of each face first, cutting individual stems back to just above a leaf node or a junction with a lateral branch. Move up in passes rather than cutting randomly across the face. The goal is to cut each protruding stem just inside the target line, leaving the last leaf or pair of leaves intact rather than removing the entire stem back to the main structural framework. Bay responds well to this approach: the nodes left on each retained stem push new growth within a few weeks in warm conditions. The finished face should look slightly rough immediately after trimming, with visible cut stem ends showing. It will green up and present a uniform surface as the new growth breaks from the retained nodes over the following four to six weeks.
  4. Trim the top last, following the string line. With the string line set at the finished height, work along the top of the hedge using secateurs in the same individual-stem method used on the faces. The top of a bay hedge is where the temptation to reach for hedge shears is strongest, particularly on a long run. Resist it. The sheared top produces the same browning leaf-edge problem as sheared faces, and it is the most visible surface of the hedge. For a formal flat top, work in short sections, standing back regularly to check the level against the string line. A slight batter (taper from base to top, with the top slightly narrower than the base) is correct for the same reason it is correct on all formal hedges: it allows light to reach the lower foliage and prevents the progressive thinning of the base that develops on hedges cut with vertical faces over many years.
  5. Clear clippings and check for bay sucker damage while doing so. Collect the clippings from the ground as you work rather than leaving them to pile up. Bay laurel clippings are aromatic and pleasant to handle; fresh clippings from a garden hedge that has not been sprayed can be used in the kitchen. Inspect the material you remove, particularly any leaves that show curled edges or cupped yellowing growth: this is the characteristic sign of bay sucker (Trioza alacris), a small psyllid insect that overwinters on the plant and lays its eggs in the new growth in spring. Infested shoots show tight, inward-curled leaf margins, often with a yellow or pale green discolouration and sometimes a waxy deposit on the underside of the leaf. The main trimming cut in June to July removes the worst-affected growth as a matter of course, since bay sucker damage concentrates on the current season's extension shoots that the trim removes. Collect and dispose of infested material in garden waste or household waste bins, not in a compost heap: bay sucker nymphs can survive in the material.

Why hedge trimmers ruin a bay laurel hedge

The case against using hedge trimmers on bay is not a matter of preference but of plant biology. Bay leaves are large (typically 6 to 10 centimetres long and 2 to 3 centimetres wide), slightly leathery, and contain volatile aromatic oils. When a trimmer blade or shear blade slices through the middle of a leaf, the exposed tissue at the cut edge desiccates and oxidises rapidly. The cell damage spreads slightly beyond the cut line within the first few days, producing a brown border several millimetres wide along every cut edge. On a hedge of reasonable size, every pass of the trimmer produces hundreds of these cut edges, and the finished surface looks brown and tattered rather than green and tidy. The browning is not a sign of disease or die-back; the rest of the leaf is unaffected. But it is permanent for that leaf: the brown edge does not green up again. The only remedy is waiting for enough new growth to cover the damaged leaves, which takes a full season.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) are also large-leaved species where the RHS advises secateurs over trimmers for the same reason. With bay the effect is more pronounced because the leaves are thinner and more aromatic than either Prunus laurel, and the oils released by a cut edge accelerate the browning. If you are managing a large bay hedge of 20 metres or more and the labour of secateur work is prohibitive, hand shears with long, fine blades are a compromise: they produce shorter cut edges than a powered trimmer and can be used more accurately to minimise leaf-cutting if you work carefully, cutting at angles that favour stem cuts over leaf cuts. This is not as good as secateurs but substantially better than a powered trimmer. For any hedge under about 15 metres, secateurs are the correct answer and the work is achievable in a half-day.

Bay sucker: what it is and how the pruning cycle interacts

Bay sucker (Trioza alacris) is one of the most commonly encountered problems on bay laurel in UK gardens. The adults overwinter on the plant and begin laying eggs in developing shoot tips as the new growth emerges in spring. The nymphs, which are small and flat, feed on the underside of young leaves and cause the characteristic tightly curled leaf margins and yellowing that appears from April through early summer. Severely infested hedges can look unhealthy and sparse through spring, with large amounts of distorted growth concentrated on the outer face.

The good news for CT10 bay hedge owners is that the main pruning window (June to July) aligns almost exactly with the point at which the worst of the bay sucker damage is visible and can be removed. The June cut removes the current season's extension growth, which is precisely where the nymph infestation is concentrated. This does not eliminate bay sucker from the garden (adults return and re-infest the following spring), but it removes the visible damage and the population living in it, reducing the pressure on the plant for the remainder of the season. No pesticide treatment is necessary or recommended on a hedge being managed with annual secateur pruning, since the pruning does the job. If the infestation is severe on a neglected hedge that has not been trimmed for several years, a targeted treatment with a systemic insecticide labelled for bay sucker (or a contact oil spray timed to the spring nymph emergence in April to May) is an option: check that the product is listed for bay sucker specifically, and do not apply any systemic insecticide to bay plants whose foliage you intend to use in cooking.

Bay laurel in Broadstairs: frost, chalk and coastal exposure

Bay laurel is a Mediterranean species that performs well in the generally mild south-east England climate but has clear limits. In a typical Broadstairs winter the temperatures rarely fall below -5 °C, which is within the plant's comfort zone on a sheltered site. In a hard winter with a sustained north-easterly or easterly air mass, temperatures in CT10 can drop to -8 °C or below, and unprotected bay plants in exposed positions will show significant die-back or complete top-kill. The standard response in Broadstairs gardens is to site bay hedges and standards on south or south-west-facing boundaries, against a wall that holds daytime warmth, or in the sheltered rear garden of a Victorian terrace where the building provides effective frost-buffering. Bay planted on a north or north-east-facing boundary at a property close to the clifftop bays (Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate, North Foreland) is exposed to both the full force of cold north-easterly winds and the desiccating effect of salt-laden air: this is too exposed a position for bay as a formal hedge. For clifftop north-east-facing boundaries, the coastal species guide lists the species that actually handle those conditions.

On the chalk soils of Broadstairs and St Peter's, bay performs well. It is a tolerant species on pH, managing alkaline conditions comfortably, and the free-draining character of Margate Chalk suits it: bay does not like waterlogged roots and the chalk's natural drainage prevents the winter saturation that kills more plants in this area than frost does. On newly prepared chalk ground, incorporate organic matter before planting to improve early water-retention, but once established the plant requires no amendment. Bay on chalk will grow at a moderate rather than fast pace compared with the same plant on deep loam, which makes the annual secateur trim more manageable.

Within the four Broadstairs-area conservation areas (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA and Kingsgate CA), routine annual pruning of a bay laurel hedge does not normally require a section 211 six-week notice under the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The s.211 rules apply to individual trees with a trunk over 75 millimetres in diameter at 1.5 metres above ground level. A formally clipped bay hedge is not caught by this rule. However, if a bay plant growing in a hedge position has been left unclipped for many years and has developed into a recognisable tree form with a substantial stem, check before cutting: the boundary between hedge shrub and tree is a matter of judgement and Thanet District Council can advise if you are uncertain. The full rules are in the conservation areas guide.

Renovation pruning on a neglected bay hedge

Bay laurel tolerates hard pruning well and will regenerate from old, bare wood, which puts it in the same useful category as cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, hawthorn and holly for renovation work. If a bay hedge has been left unclipped for three or more years and has grown well beyond its intended dimensions, the staged renovation approach works here as it does for other hard-prune-tolerant species. In late April or early May, before the main nesting season is fully under way (carry out a nest check first regardless of the date), cut one face of the hedge hard back to the main structural stems: remove the previous seasons' extension growth and cut back to within 10 to 15 centimetres of the structural frame. Leave the other face and the top at their existing dimensions. The cut face looks bare for three to four weeks, then begins to push new growth from dormant buds along the remaining stems. By the end of the first growing season, the face has reasonable new cover that can be trimmed to shape in the following June. In the second late April, cut the second face back. Top reduction, if the hedge has gained significant height, can be staged in the same way or taken in one cut if the height reduction required is not more than about a third of the total. If a bay has been left for many years and the main structural stems have become very large (over 15 centimetres across), the renovation is better done by a contractor who can judge whether the plant will recover reliably from the scale of cut proposed and can manage the significant volume of material produced.
