Box blight and box tree moth in Broadstairs: diagnosis and treatment steps for CT10 gardens.

Box (Buxus sempervirens) is in more Broadstairs gardens than almost any other formal hedge species: older terraced streets are full of it. Since around 2011 two separate problems have been working through the UK's box population: box tree moth, an invasive pest that strips foliage from inside the bush outward, and box blight, a fungal disease that turns stems black and leaves brown. In 2024 the RHS recorded nearly five times as many box tree moth reports in the first four months of the year as in the same period in 2023. Many owners are treating the wrong problem, or treating the right one at the wrong time. Here is how I approach it.

Steps

Step 1: Diagnose the problem before touching anything

Box blight and box tree moth produce superficially similar results, both cause brown patches and bare sections, but the causes and treatments are entirely different. Confusing them wastes time and can make the situation worse. Do the diagnosis first, not last.

Signs of box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis): Pull the outer foliage aside and look inside the bush. Caterpillars feed from the interior outward, so exterior damage is a late sign. Inside you will find: pale green and yellow-striped caterpillars up to 4cm long; white or cream webbing draped between branches; dark green or black frass (caterpillar droppings) collecting on stems and the soil below. The foliage is stripped and the stems left intact, at least initially. If you only see the outside, look for patches of foliage that are entirely missing or hanging as dried, dead material, with the stems beneath still green and structurally sound.

Signs of box blight (Calonectria pseudonaviculata, previously Cylindrocladium buxicola): Brown leaves that fall, leaving bare patches, with black streaks and dieback on young stems. In wet weather, turn affected leaves over and look for white spore masses on the underside. Blight typically spreads outward from a central point of infection, so the brown patches tend to have a rough circular or irregular perimeter rather than the stripped-clean interior pattern of moth. No webbing, no frass.

A box can have both at once. Diagnose each problem separately and address both.

Step 2: For box tree moth, deploy a pheromone trap to time spraying correctly

Btk (Bacillus thuringiensis var. kurstaki), the biological insecticide registered for amateur use against box tree moth in the UK, only kills young caterpillars when they are actively feeding. It has no residual effect against eggs, adults or pupae. Spraying at the wrong point in the generation cycle achieves nothing: if you spray when adults are flying but before eggs have hatched, the product degrades before the caterpillars emerge. If you spray after the caterpillars are fully grown, they are past the stage where Btk is lethal.

Pheromone traps, a sticky card inside a simple trap carrying a synthetic female box tree moth pheromone, catch the male moths as they fly and tell you when the adult generation is active. Buy at least one trap and hang it in or near the affected box in April. Check it weekly. When you first catch moths, start checking the box closely for small caterpillars, and time any Btk spray for when young caterpillars are present.

The RHS says there are at least two generations of box tree moth a year in the UK, with the possibility of an extra generation if autumn stays mild. A single trap deployed through the season gives you the timing information to catch each generation before the caterpillars establish.

Step 3: Apply Btk with a drench, not a surface spray

Because box tree moth caterpillars feed from inside the bush, applying Btk to the outside surface of the foliage treats the wrong surface. The product needs to reach the foliage inside the canopy where the caterpillars are actually eating.

Use a sprayer set to fine mist and physically push the wand into the bush to drench the interior branches. Work around the full circumference of the hedge or topiary piece. Follow the product label for the number of applications, the interval between them and what to do after rain. Store the concentrate as the label directs.

Read the product label for the specific Btk formulation you buy: concentration rates and spray interval guidance vary slightly between brands. Follow the MAPP (Ministerial Approval of Pesticide Products) number on the label to confirm the product is registered for this use in the UK.

Step 4: Remove caterpillars by hand from smaller topiary pieces

For a single box ball or small clipped piece rather than a run of hedge, hand-picking caterpillars is faster and more thorough than spraying. Wear gloves. Pull back sections of foliage and remove caterpillars directly into a bucket of soapy water. The webbing makes them easier to spot once you know what to look for. Check every three or four days through an active generation. For a large hedge run this is impractical, but for the box topiary that appears in many Broadstairs front gardens, hand-picking is often the more precise tool.

Step 5: For box blight, cut out affected material and clean tools between every cut

Box blight spreads via fungal spores, and cutting through infected material with an unclean blade transfers spores to every subsequent cut. This is why blight in a formally trimmed hedge often appears in a line or streak that matches the direction of a single trimmer pass: the trimmer was the vector.

Cut out affected parts in dry conditions, back to healthy tissue. The RHS advises that infected material should be bagged to avoid dropping debris around the garden and binned, not composted. Do not shred it on site or stack it in the garden: the fungus can survive on fallen leaves for years and will re-infect from the heap. Clean blades with a garden disinfectant or mild bleach solution between plants and between areas of the garden. This applies whether you are cutting by hand or with a trimmer. Feed plants that have been cut back with a general-purpose fertiliser to aid recovery.

Does blight removal count as trimming under the nesting-season rules?

Yes. Cutting into box foliage and stems, whether for cosmetic trimming or blight removal, is covered by section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 if the bush contains an active nest. Box is dense enough to attract nesting from blackbirds, robins, sparrows and dunnocks, particularly in established topiary pieces and thick garden hedging. Before any cutting work between 1 March and 31 August, visually check the interior of the box for an active nest: a compact cup of grass and mud, usually containing eggs or chicks. If you find one, leave that section until the brood has fledged. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest. A blight-removal job is not exempt from this check.

Step 6: Be cautious about fungicides, and clear fallen leaves

The RHS recommends that you do not use fungicides on box blight: they may reduce biodiversity and affect soil health, and they are unlikely to work unless combined with good hygiene. If you do decide to use one, use only a product that is legally available to home gardeners in the UK and follow the label. The practical control is hygiene: remove fallen leaves from the centre and around the base of affected plants, strip off the surface of the topsoil where infected leaves have fallen, and bin that material rather than composting it.

For a badly affected plant or a less important planting, the RHS suggests digging it up and destroying it, especially if you are trying to safeguard prized plantings nearby.

Step 7: Reduce the conditions that allow blight to establish

Box blight (Calonectria pseudonaviculata) is a wet-weather fungal disease. It needs high humidity, free moisture on leaf surfaces, and poor air movement to establish and spread rapidly. Several routine care changes reduce the risk:

  • Water at the base, never overhead. Wet foliage from overhead irrigation is one of the primary vectors in sheltered city gardens. Summer overhead watering is rarely necessary for established box and should stop entirely.
  • Avoid trimming in wet weather. Trimming opens cut surfaces on every stem in the hedge, and the RHS advises pruning box only in dry conditions.
  • Improve air circulation. The RHS suggests clipping less often, because regular clipping makes dense foliage with little air movement, and pruning a hedge with a convex top rather than a flat one.
  • Clear fallen leaves from inside and around box hedging in autumn. Fallen box leaves can carry the fungus for years and re-infect the plant.

Is box still the right plant for your Broadstairs garden?

Box blight was first recognised in the UK in the mid 1990s, and box tree moth was found in private gardens from 2011. Both are now widespread across England. The honest assessment is that box in a sheltered, humid, shaded position, particularly under other foliage, in an enclosed Victorian terrace courtyard, or against a north-facing wall, is now a very high-maintenance plant compared to the same spot in 1990. In a position with reasonable air movement and reliable drainage, box is still manageable with the steps above. On free-draining chalk, box avoids waterlogged roots, and an open, breezy position helps the air circulation that blight-prone shaded corners lack.

For Broadstairs gardens where box has repeatedly failed despite treatment, or where the topiary programme is too labour-intensive to maintain, species that provide similar visual structure without the same pest and disease exposure include:

  • Ilex crenata (Japanese holly): visually very similar to box, clips to the same sharp-edged topiary form, and is not a host for either box tree moth or box blight. Slower to establish than box.
  • Lonicera nitida (Wilson's honeysuckle): clips well and grows fast, though it needs more frequent trimming than box to hold a tight formal edge. Tolerates coastal exposure well.
  • Taxus baccata (yew): the correct choice for a formal hedge wanting the same density, longevity and clippable precision as box at larger scale. Cuts once a year in late August. The coastal species guide covers yew in detail, including the persistent myth that it dislikes chalk.
  • Pittosporum tobira: an evergreen that clips into a rounded form useful for topiary and copes with coastal conditions in a sheltered spot. Less formally precise than box or Japanese holly, and best in a mild, sheltered position.

Replacing box topiary is a multi-year commitment: even fast-growing alternatives take three to five years to reach the size a mature box piece occupied. Factor that timeline in honestly before deciding to start over.

Box in Broadstairs conservation areas

Box hedges and topiary pieces in the four Broadstairs-area conservation areas (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA and Kingsgate CA) are subject to the usual Thanet District Council section 211 six-week notice rules only where a plant counts as a tree (a trunk over 75mm in diameter at 1.5m above ground). Hedges and shrubs, including low box edging and typical domestic hedge runs, are normally not covered. If you are removing or severely cutting a very large, mature specimen box, check first with Thanet District Council whether a s.211 notice is required. The conservation area rules are covered in detail in the conservation areas guide.
