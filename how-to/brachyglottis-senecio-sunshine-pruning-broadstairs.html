How to prune Brachyglottis 'Sunshine': the bare-wood rule, spring timing and coastal notes for Broadstairs CT10.

Brachyglottis (Dunedin Group) 'Sunshine' is one of the most reliable silver-foliage shrubs for a salt-exposed Thanet garden. Its felted grey-green leaves, its tolerance of Margate chalk and its indifference to salt-laden easterlies make it a standard filler in front gardens from Kingsgate to Stone Bay. Pruning it comes down to one non-negotiable rule: never cut into bare old wood. Unlike yew, cherry laurel or escallonia, Brachyglottis will not break new growth from leafless brown stems. If you cut past the zone of live foliage, you end up with an open wound that does not close, and the branch is effectively dead. This guide gives the step-by-step method for the annual spring cut, the nesting-season check that must come first, and the approach for bringing back a plant that has been left to sprawl.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the plant and check for active nests

The annual spring prune falls in April and May, squarely inside the bird nesting window that runs from 1 March to 31 August. Brachyglottis has a low, spreading habit with a dense outer canopy of silver foliage, and dunnocks, robins and wrens use shrubs at this height for ground-level nests. Before any cut, walk slowly around the full perimeter of the plant, watching for adult birds repeatedly entering and leaving the same spot in the canopy, and listening for faint alarm calls or nest noise. Any section with an active nest must be left uncut and marked. Most garden species complete a brood in three to five weeks from hatching.

Brachyglottis pruning and the nesting window

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly take, damage or destroy a nest while it is in use or being built. The accepted guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. The annual Brachyglottis spring prune falls directly inside that window. The nest check is not optional: carry it out before every cut, however routine the job feels. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both.

Step 2: Confirm the timing is right

The RHS places Brachyglottis in Pruning Group 8: cut shoots back to vigorous young shoots in mid to late spring, once the last frost risk has passed. In CT10, that window runs from mid-April to the end of May. Cutting before mid-April risks exposing fresh new growth to a late frost, which can scorch soft young shoots and set the plant back two to three weeks. Cutting after the end of May risks overlapping with the June-to-July flowering flush, which reduces that year's daisy flower display.

The practical test: once night temperatures have been consistently above 4°C for two weeks and there is no frost forecast in the next ten days, the timing is right. In a typical Broadstairs season, mid-April to early May hits this window reliably; in a cold spring, hold until the second or third week of May.

Step 3: Assess what to cut

Stand back and look at the plant from two or three metres away. Identify the zone where silver-grey felted foliage ends and bare brown stem begins. That boundary is the absolute limit of your cut. Everything above it carries live foliage; everything below it does not. This varies from stem to stem: some old stems at the base of a mature plant will be bare for 300-400mm before they carry live growth; others closer to the perimeter will carry foliage almost to ground level. Assess each stem individually rather than deciding a single cut height across the whole plant.

For the standard annual cut on a plant that was pruned last spring, the new season's extension growth will typically be 100-200mm long. Cut back to two or three buds above last year's cut point, into wood that still carries short lateral shoots with foliage.

Step 4: Gather tools

Brachyglottis has soft-ish growth on the outer canopy and older woody stems at the base. You need:

  • Sharp secateurs for the annual shortening of extension growth on the outer canopy. The stems at this point are typically 5-10mm in diameter, well within secateur range.
  • Loppers for any stems at the base of an older plant that have become genuinely woody (15mm diameter and above), particularly where you are removing a dead or damaged stem entirely at its point of origin.
  • A tarpaulin or garden sheet under the plant to collect the clippings. Brachyglottis produces a moderate quantity of soft growth, and rolling up the tarpaulin is quicker than raking individual stems off gravel or bark mulch.

Avoid a powered hedge trimmer for the main prune. The irregular habit and the strict "stop at the foliage boundary" rule mean you need to judge each stem individually, which a trimmer pass prevents. Secateurs give you that control.

Step 5: Make the cuts

Work methodically around and through the plant, cutting each shoot back to a point where the stem still carries live foliage, two to four buds above the base of the current season's growth. Cut cleanly just above an outward-facing bud or lateral shoot, angled slightly away from the bud to drain rainwater. Do not leave stubs: a cut stem severed mid-internodal length without a bud to draw growth dies back to the nearest bud below, leaving a dead tip in the canopy.

For stems at the base that are entirely bare with dead or dying tips, cut them back to their point of origin at the main structural framework, or as close to it as you can reach. These will not regenerate from the bare section, and leaving dead wood in the centre of a mature Brachyglottis creates a site for fungal disease and congests the plant's airflow.

As a rough guide, removing around half the current season's extension growth and tidying the base of dead material keeps the plant at a consistent, manageable size (typically 1 to 1.5m high and up to 2m across) and maintains the dense, domed habit that makes it effective as a low informal boundary or front-garden filler.

Step 6: Clear and dispose

Brachyglottis clippings are soft and compost well: add them to a garden compost heap or the kerbside green bin. The felted leaves break down within a season in a reasonably active heap. The clippings are not toxic and do not need separate disposal.

After clearing, check the base of the plant for any further dead or hollow stems that the main cut revealed. The interior of a dense mature Brachyglottis often holds a few dead stems that were not visible from the outside; removing them in the same session saves a second trip.

Why Brachyglottis works on the Thanet coast

Three things make Brachyglottis 'Sunshine' better suited to a Broadstairs or Kingsgate garden than the coastal gardening mainstream tends to acknowledge:

  • Salt-wind tolerance. The thick, felted leaf surface that gives the plant its silver-grey colour is an adaptation to exposed conditions. It traps a thin boundary layer of still air next to the leaf, reducing water loss and salt deposition. This is the same mechanism that makes it native to exposed coastal and hill environments in New Zealand, and it transfers directly to the North Sea front.
  • Margate chalk compatibility. Brachyglottis tolerates poor, sharply alkaline soils. It will not thrive in a waterlogged heavy clay, but on the thin, free-draining chalk topsoil of the Thanet clifftops it establishes well, provided it is watered through the first summer. Thanet's annual rainfall of around 615mm (Manston station, Met Office 1991-2020 average) is not especially generous, and drought kills more new Brachyglottis plants here than wind or cold does.
  • Hardiness. RHS hardiness rating H4, tolerating temperatures down to -10°C. In a typical Broadstairs winter, that is a comfortable margin. Even in a hard east-Kent winter with extended night frosts, established Brachyglottis rarely shows significant stem damage, unlike Griselinia or Pittosporum, which can be caught by an unusual cold snap.

Nomenclature: Brachyglottis or Senecio?

Most gardeners and nurseries in CT10 still sell and label this plant as Senecio 'Sunshine' or Senecio greyi (a name that strictly applies to a different and rarer New Zealand species). The RHS uses Brachyglottis (Dunedin Group) 'Sunshine' as the accepted name following a taxonomic reclassification. For practical purposes in a Broadstairs garden the two names describe the same plant: the same silver felted leaf, the same yellow daisy flowers in June and July, the same habit and the same pruning requirement. If you are looking for it at a local nursery, searching for either name will find it; "Senecio 'Sunshine'" remains the more common retail label in Kent as of late 2026.

Managing a leggy or overgrown Brachyglottis

A Brachyglottis that has not been pruned annually for several years commonly develops a leggy habit: an open, sprawling structure with long bare stems and foliage concentrated at the outer tips rather than evenly across the plant. This is not a sign of disease. It is the natural growth pattern when the annual shortening is missed.

The difficult truth is that renovation is limited. Because the plant cannot regenerate from bare old wood, you cannot cut a leggy Brachyglottis back hard the way you would a forsythia or a dogwood. If the bare stems are already 500mm or more long, you cannot take the plant back to a compact shape in a single cut without exposing sections of bare stem that will not recover.

The practical approach is a staged reduction over two or three seasons. In year one, cut back only to the current foliage boundary on each stem, removing as little as necessary to tighten the outline slightly, and removing any dead stems at the base entirely. In year two, cut back a little further into the previous season's wood, to the point where short lateral shoots are still present. By year three, the plant will have produced new growth across its surface, reducing the bare-to-foliage ratio and making a tighter cut achievable without leaving bare sections exposed.

If the plant is too far gone, replacement is the honest answer. A young Brachyglottis 'Sunshine' in a one-litre or two-litre pot, planted in April or May with good watering through its first summer, will reach 1.2m across within two to three years on a Thanet coastal soil.

Common problems with Brachyglottis in CT10

Brown patches in the canopy after winter. Occasional frost damage on the exposed outer foliage is normal after a hard winter. The felted coating is damaged by sustained frost combined with cold wind, and the affected leaves turn brown and papery. Do not cut these sections in autumn or winter: wait for the spring prune, by which point new growth will show behind the damaged area and you can see clearly how far back the damage actually extends. In most cases it is superficial, confined to the outer leaf layer.

No flowers. If the plant flowers poorly or not at all in June and July, the most common cause is the annual spring prune being carried out too late, in June rather than May, removing the bud sites before they can develop into the current season's flower spikes. Move the prune to late April or early May and the flower display usually returns within one season.

Thin, pale foliage. Brachyglottis is at its best in a sunny position on a free-draining site. In partial shade, or on a heavy soil, the leaves lose some of their silver lustre and the plant grows more loosely. This is not a pruning problem: it is a site-suitability issue. It performs at its best on the well-drained, sunny, chalk sites that the Thanet clifftop positions provide in abundance.
