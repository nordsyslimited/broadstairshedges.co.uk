How to remove brambles from a garden hedge in Broadstairs: root-level control for CT10.

Brambles (Rubus fruticosus agg.) are the single most persistent unwanted coloniser in Broadstairs garden hedges. They arrive as seeds dropped by birds from the coastal scrub above Stone Bay and Joss Bay, root quickly on thin Margate chalk, and within two or three seasons have canes arching through the interior of a privet, hawthorn or cherry laurel hedge. The stems look superficially like the hedge itself from a distance, which is why so many CT10 hedges end up with a tangle of thorny growth running through them unnoticed for years. Pulling live canes out by hand spreads them further and causes injuries. The method below works from the roots up, timed around nesting season, and is the approach I use across Broadstairs, St Peter's and the clifftop gardens.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the hedge and check for active nests before touching anything

Brambles inside a hedge are some of the most productive nesting sites in a CT10 garden. Blackbirds, dunnocks and song thrushes use the thorn-protected interior of a bramble tangle extensively, and the fruiting stems that remain from the previous season persist as structural material long after the leaves have dropped, giving mid-winter nest-builders cover from January onward. Before lifting secateurs or loppers, walk the full length of the hedge slowly from both sides and watch for a full two minutes. Look for repeated flight-lines into the hedge, alarm calls from adults nearby, or the cup of a nest visible through the tangle. If you find any active nest, postpone the work. October to February is the safest working window for bramble clearance in most years; work done in September or from March onward carries a meaningfully higher nesting risk.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: bramble clearance and nesting birds

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest while it is in use or being built. The Act sets no calendar dates; the practical caution window for most garden birds is 1 March to 31 August, but blackbirds in sheltered south-facing gardens and robins year-round mean a late-October nest is entirely possible in CT10. The check at Step 1 is the only way to avoid committing the offence. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine per nest. Taking two minutes before every clearance session, not just the first one, costs nothing and is not optional.

Step 2: Assess how far the bramble has penetrated the hedge

Before cutting, understand what you are dealing with. There are three situations, and they call for different approaches:

  1. Surface colonisation only. Bramble canes have grown up the outer face of the hedge but roots are at the base rather than deep inside the root system of the hedge itself. This is the easiest situation: a single cutting session followed by root extraction deals with it completely.
  2. Interior colonisation. Bramble has grown through the interior of the hedge and canes are interwoven through the hedge framework. Roots may be distributed along the full length of the colonised run. Work is more time-consuming but the method is the same.
  3. Long-established multi-rooted infestation. Bramble has been growing in the hedge for five or more years, multiple root crowns are present, and the canes have produced layered nodes that have rooted at multiple points. In this case, a single autumn clearance will not give full control. Plan for an initial major clearance followed by two further autumn follow-up sessions over the next two years.

Prod the base of the hedge with a fork to find the main root crowns before starting. On Thanet chalk, which tends toward dry, free-draining conditions, bramble roots often stay near the surface rather than going deep, which makes extraction easier than on heavier soils.

Step 3: Cut the long canes back to 30cm above ground level

Do not try to pull live bramble canes from a hedge. The thorns grip every stem and branch they contact, pulling established hedge material with them and causing injuries even through heavy gloves. Cut first.

Wear thick leather or thorn-proof gloves and long sleeves for the entire job. Use loppers for main canes over 15mm diameter and a pair of sharp bypass secateurs for finer stems. Work systematically from one end of the affected run to the other, cutting each visible bramble cane to around 30cm above ground level. This stub length matters: cutting flush with the ground at this stage makes finding the root crown harder, whereas a 30cm stub gives you a handle to grip when you come to extract the root.

On a run of hedge with bramble growing through the interior, you will need to lean in and cut sections from inside the hedge as well as from the outside. A pair of long-handled loppers with at least 60cm handles makes this easier and keeps your hands further from the thorns. Cut the canes into manageable 60-90cm sections as you go and drop them at the base of the hedge rather than trying to pull them through the hedge structure whole, which causes more damage to the hedge than is necessary.

Step 4: Extract the root crowns from the hedge base

This is the step that determines whether the bramble comes back. A bramble root crown that is left in the ground will regenerate from the cut stub within one growing season, and the regrowth will be more vigorous than the original because the root system is already established. Cutting alone is a recurring maintenance task, not a permanent solution.

Use a border fork or a mattock to loosen the soil around the base of each stub. On thin chalk, this is usually straightforward: the root crown of a typical garden bramble is within 15-20cm of the surface. Lever out the crown with the fork, following the thicker roots outward as far as you can. The RHS advises using a fork rather than a spade because a spade slices roots into sections, each of which can resprout; a fork levers the whole crown out more cleanly. Where roots go beneath the base of an established hedge, cut them off as deep down as you can reach rather than leaving them attached to the crown.

Place extracted roots and crowns immediately into a bag or wheelbarrow. Do not lay them on the surface where they can dry and apparently die, then reroot if they contact bare soil during subsequent handling.

Step 5: Clear stem sections from the hedge interior

Once the root crowns are out, the cut cane sections inside the hedge can be removed. Work from one end to the other, pulling sections downward and outward with gloved hands. A stiff-tined hand rake drawn through the hedge interior dislodges the worst of the tangled material where hand-pulling is awkward. On a deeply colonised section of privet or hawthorn, you may find canes 2-3m long running horizontally through the interior: cut these into shorter sections with loppers before trying to pull them through, as a rigid cane 3m long acts as a lever and takes hedge material with it.

It is normal to remove some of the hedge's own interior material during this process on a heavily colonised run. Privet, in particular, generates a lot of dead interior wood over time, and the combination of bramble removal and a light interior thinning often leaves the hedge in better condition than before: more light penetrates to the base and the lower growth, which tends to be sparse under a dense bramble tangle, begins to recover.

Step 6: Dispose of roots and stems correctly

Bramble roots and green stems should not go into a cold home compost heap. The roots can survive and resprout even in a partially decomposed heap, and the stem sections can root if they contact moist compost during turning. The RHS is explicit on this: cut stems and roots should go to a council green-waste collection rather than a home compost bin, unless the heap reaches 60 degrees Celsius consistently (a hot compost method).

Broadstairs and St Peter's are served by Thanet District Council's kerbside green-waste collection (alternating-week cadence on most CT10 rounds) and by the Manston Road household waste recycling centre. Both accept bramble material. Dry, dead stem material that has been left to desiccate fully can also be shredded and used as a fibrous mulch, as the RHS notes, since dried dead canes present none of the rerooting risk of green material.

The thorny stems are a puncture risk in standard garden waste bags. Double-bag or use a purpose-made thorn-proof garden sack if you are putting them out for collection.

Step 7: Monitor for regrowth the following spring and cut any new shoots immediately

Even after thorough root extraction, some regrowth from missed root fragments is normal, particularly in a long-established infestation. New shoots emerging from the base of the hedge in April or May will be soft and easy to pull by hand at this stage, with the root fragment still near the surface. Catch them at this point: a shoot pulled at 15cm with its root fragment takes thirty seconds, whereas the same root left until September has had a full growing season to re-establish.

Walk the cleared run once a month from April to September in the year after clearance and remove any new bramble growth immediately. By the second autumn, regrowth should be minimal or absent if the initial extraction was thorough. A third check the following spring confirms whether any further treatment is needed.

Why brambles are particularly persistent on Thanet chalk

The Margate Chalk that underlies most of Broadstairs and St Peter's creates conditions that suit bramble establishment unusually well. The chalk drains freely, meaning a bramble plant that roots successfully never experiences the waterlogging that limits establishment on heavier clay soils. The south and east aspects of the CT10 coastline give good light levels year-round, and the maritime climate, relatively mild winters compared with inland Kent, means young plants survive their first winter reliably. The chalk subsoil also warms quickly in spring, giving an early start to root extension.

Seed dispersal is another CT10-specific factor. The scrub communities on the clifftops above Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate produce large quantities of bramble fruit each autumn, and the blackbirds, woodpigeons and starlings that strip this fruit are the same birds that pass through garden hedges along the cliff-edge roads. A bird that has fed on scrub brambles and then perches in a garden privet deposits seeds in the hedge in a single visit. This is why gardens within 200 metres of the clifftop SSSI scrub tend to have more persistent bramble pressure in their hedges than gardens further inland.

The case for partial retention: when some bramble in a hedge is worth keeping

Not every bramble shoot in a hedge warrants removal, and this is worth saying plainly before giving a clearance method. Bramble fruit is one of the highest-value food sources for birds in a CT10 garden in September and October. Blackbirds, song thrushes, robins and starlings all use it heavily in the weeks before and after the main summer nesting season ends. The flowering period in June and July produces nectar for bumblebees and hoverflies. The thorny interior of a bramble-colonised section of hawthorn or blackthorn hedge is some of the most productive nesting habitat in an ordinary Broadstairs garden.

The question worth asking before clearing is not "is this bramble?" but "is this bramble causing a functional problem for the hedge?" If the hedge can still be cut to its intended shape, if the lower growth of the hedge is not being suppressed, and if the bramble is not growing out over a pavement or path, managing the growth by annual cutting of overlong canes in September is a reasonable alternative to full extraction. The method above is for situations where full removal is the right decision. Partial management, keeping the bramble contained to one or two sections of a native hedge rather than extracting it, is a legitimate and wildlife-positive choice in gardens near the clifftop where fruit-bearing cover has some genuine ecological value.

Glyphosate as a last resort: risks and timing in a garden hedge

For a heavy multi-rooted infestation that has defied two years of physical extraction, a targeted glyphosate application can break the cycle. The BBC Gardeners' World guidance is to train the cut bramble stems up canes away from the surrounding hedge plants and apply glyphosate to the foliage only, from when bramble starts to flush in spring through to early autumn. This separates the treated material from the hedge plants sufficiently to reduce the risk of contact herbicide reaching the root system of the hedge itself, though it does not eliminate it entirely.

Glyphosate kills any green plant tissue it contacts, including the foliage of nearby hedge plants if application is not precise. It is systemic, meaning it moves from the treated foliage down into the root system, which is what makes it effective against established bramble crowns. It is not selective. I do not recommend glyphosate as a first approach in a garden hedge: the physical method above is safer for the hedge plants and produces good results in most CT10 cases. Where it is used, apply it on a dry, still day to minimise drift, and leave a 30cm buffer between the treated canes and any hedge foliage. Many UK councils have withdrawn glyphosate from public land use; it remains legal for garden use as of 2026 but follow current product labelling and dispose of containers correctly.
