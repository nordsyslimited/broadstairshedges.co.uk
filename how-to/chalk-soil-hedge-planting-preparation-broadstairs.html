How to prepare chalk garden ground for hedge planting: a step-by-step guide for CT10.

Thanet sits on the Margate Chalk Member: Upper Cretaceous White Chalk, marl-free, that drains fast, holds moisture poorly and runs sharply alkaline at pH 7.5-8.5. In a mature Broadstairs or St Peter's garden bed there might be 60-80cm of decent topsoil before you hit solid rock; on a south-facing slope or a recently disturbed boundary, it can be as little as 15-20cm. That combination of fast drainage and alkalinity kills newly planted hedging more reliably than frost or coastal wind. The fix is what you do before the first plant goes in: the trench method rather than individual holes, a correctly proportioned backfill, and a year-one watering routine calibrated for chalk. This guide covers those steps for typical sheltered garden positions in CT10. For clifftop positions at Stone Bay, North Foreland and Kingsgate, where salt-laden north-easterly wind adds a further constraint, see the companion guide on planting a coastal hedge on North Foreland chalk.

Steps

Step 1: Test the soil and assess topsoil depth before you buy a single plant.

Put a teaspoon of soil from your planting strip into a glass and add a splash of vinegar. Active fizzing and bubbling confirms free calcium carbonate in the soil, which means chalk or limestone parent material. This is consistent across most of Broadstairs, St Peter's and Westgate. If you want a more precise figure, a cheap pH testing kit or electronic meter from a garden centre gives a reading within about 0.5 of a unit: Thanet garden soils over chalk typically return 7.5-8.0 in a cultivated bed, sometimes higher on undisturbed ground.

Assess depth to solid chalk with a metal bar or a narrow spade. Drive the bar down in several spots along the planting line. Where it meets sharp resistance above 30-40cm you have a shallow profile that needs extra imported topsoil before you proceed; where you get 50cm or more of looser material before meeting resistance you have a workable profile that just needs the trench method and backfill improvements described below.

Step 2: Clear the planting strip, check for underground services, and check for nesting birds if clearing during the spring or summer.

Remove any existing vegetation from the planting strip: weeds, grass, old shrub roots. For a boundary that has not been disturbed, the soil will often be compacted and the top 10-15cm may be a dense mat of old roots. Take these out before marking the trench line. If the boundary runs close to a building, check with your utility provider before digging: buried cables, pipes and drainage runs can be closer to boundary fences than is obvious. The HSE recommends a check through the relevant mapping service before any significant excavation.

Clearing an overgrown boundary strip in spring or summer: check for nesting birds first.

If you are clearing an existing overgrown boundary strip, brambly undergrowth or scrubby hedging to prepare for new planting, and that clearing work falls between 1 March and 31 August, check carefully for active bird nests before you cut or dig. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest. Ground-nesting birds including dunnocks and wrens use dense low scrub; blackbirds and chaffinches use boundary hedging at low level. Walk the full strip slowly, part the foliage at several points, and listen before picking up any tool. If you find eggs or unfledged chicks, leave that section alone and return at least three to four weeks after the chicks have fledged. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest disturbed, with potential imprisonment for aggravated offences.

Step 3: Dig a continuous trench along the full planting line, not individual holes.

This is the step most home-planting projects skip, and it is the main reason CT10 chalk-garden plantings fail. Individual holes in chalk create a bathtub effect: the chalk pan around and below the hole is impermeable, water pools in wet weather, the roots sit in waterlogged compost, and in a dry summer the same hole drains in days and the root ball has nowhere to reach. A continuous trench breaks up the pan along the entire run, gives roots a lateral route into the surrounding soil rather than circling inside a contained pocket, and allows drainage to dissipate along the trench length.

Dig the trench at least 45-50cm deep and 40cm wide. This is deeper than a standard garden border fork can go in one pass; use a spade and work in stages, taking out material in layers. Set the excavated material aside: you will use the best of it in the backfill mix at Step 5. Discard any solid chalk lumps larger than a fist, any large stone, and any material that has clearly been brought in as rubble infill by previous owners.

Step 4: Break up the chalk below the trench floor to give roots a route downward.

Once the trench is at depth, the base and lower sides are likely chalk or chalk-influenced subsoil. Take a fork or a mattock and work the base of the trench thoroughly: break up any solid chalk pan to a further 20-30cm below the trench floor. You are not trying to remove it; you are fracturing it so that fine feeder roots can penetrate and water can move downward rather than pooling. On a site with exceptionally thin topsoil where chalk starts at 20-25cm depth, consider importing an additional 10-15cm of topsoil to bring the effective depth up before planting: the target working depth from soil surface to undisturbed chalk is ideally 60-70cm for a hedge that will stay in place for 20 years or more.

Step 5: Prepare the backfill mix and refill the trench to planting depth.

The backfill mix that works consistently on CT10 chalk is: 2 parts good-quality topsoil : 1 part well-rotted garden compost or composted farmyard manure : 1 part of the best original soil from your excavation. This 2:1:1 ratio provides enough organic matter to hold moisture through Thanet's dry late-spring period, without the pure-compost mistake: backfilling in pure compost holds too much winter moisture, compresses rapidly as it rots, and can leave root balls sitting in a waterlogged slump by February. The topsoil-dominant mix stays open and drains, while the compost fraction retains enough moisture to get new plants through July and August. If your topsoil depth was very thin and you have imported extra topsoil, use that as the topsoil fraction in the mix.

Refill the trench with the mix to a depth that places the top of the root ball approximately 2-3cm below the final soil surface. Firm gently as you fill to avoid air pockets, but do not compact hard: the roots need to be able to push out into the mix easily.

Step 6: Plant bare-root stock during the November to March window and use mycorrhizal inoculant at the root ball.

Bare-root hedging plants, lifted from nursery fields and planted dormant, establish faster on CT10 chalk than container-grown stock. The root system extends directly into the prepared backfill without the adjustment period that a container root ball requires, and the November to March planting window means the roots have three to four months to settle before the June-August dry period arrives. For a 10-metre hedge at 3 bare-root plants per metre, 30 plants is the right quantity at standard planting density for most single-row hedging species; some fast-growing species like hawthorn or privet can be planted at 4 per metre in a double-staggered row for a quicker screen.

At the moment of planting, dust the roots with a mycorrhizal inoculant powder or dip them in the granular mix according to the product instructions. On thin chalk, mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone of newly planted hedging significantly, improving both moisture and nutrient uptake. It is not expensive and the difference in first-year establishment is noticeable on difficult sites.

Step 7: Mulch the full planting strip immediately after planting, 8-10cm deep.

Apply composted bark mulch at 8-10cm depth along the full run immediately after planting. Keep the mulch clear of the stem bases of each plant by about 5cm: bark packed against the stem traps moisture, encourages collar rot and can harbour slug damage. The mulch layer does several things simultaneously on chalk: it insulates the root zone in cold weather, suppresses competing weeds and grass that would compete directly for the limited available moisture, and most importantly slows the rapid evaporation from chalk-influenced soils during hot dry weather. On a south-facing Broadstairs garden boundary, bare chalk soil can dry to below wilting point in a June dry spell within days of rain; a good mulch layer buys an extra week before the soil moisture drops below the threshold where plants start to stress. Replenish the mulch layer each autumn for the first three years.

Step 8: Run a systematic year-one watering regime from May to September, with deep soaks rather than surface sprinkles.

Year one on CT10 chalk lives or dies by irrigation. Thorough, infrequent deep soaking outperforms frequent light watering every time on free-draining chalk: a light surface sprinkle wets the top 5cm and evaporates; a slow deep soak of 10-15 litres per plant delivered over 10-15 minutes drives moisture down to the root ball where it is needed. The regime that works for established bare-root hedging in a CT10 chalk garden: once a week in May and June, increasing to twice a week through July and August, returning to once a week in September. In a dry spell in July, increase to every five days. Use a hose with a slow trickle at the base of each plant, or a seep hose laid along the trench and left to run for 20-30 minutes. Do not water in winter once the plants are dormant; chalk-soil overwatering in December and January rots fine roots.

Species that establish well on CT10 chalk in a sheltered garden position

Thanet chalk is strongly alkaline and free-draining. The species below establish reliably in sheltered garden positions away from the cliff-line salt exposure:

  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica): arguably the defining tree of chalk downland across the North Downs. Dense, retains russet winter leaves, one of the best formal hedges for a sheltered CT10 garden. Establishes well on chalk with the trench preparation above.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): similar leaf retention to beech, slightly more tolerant of heavier soils. Very well suited to CT10 chalk garden conditions.
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): native to chalk downland, hardy, tolerates a wide range of soil depths. One of the most reliable hedging plants on the Thanet chalk in both garden and field boundary contexts.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): UK native, common in Kentish chalk hedgerows, tolerates the alkaline pH well and provides good autumn colour.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): native to the North Downs chalk. Every East Kent churchyard has evidence of yew's chalk affinity. A superb formal hedge given the chalk-trench preparation and year-one watering.
  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium or L. vulgare): widely used in Broadstairs and St Peter's gardens, tolerates chalk-influenced soils, establishes quickly.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): UK native, chalk-tolerant. Currently requires monitoring for box blight and box tree moth in CT10 (see the separate guide), but as a chalk-adapted species the soil preparation is not the limiting factor.

Species and genera to avoid entirely on CT10 chalk

Ericaceous plants, those that require acidic conditions (pH 4.5-6.0), will not establish on Thanet chalk regardless of soil preparation. The chalk acts as a near-limitless alkaline buffer; adding sulphur or acidifying fertilisers makes no lasting difference in a chalk garden because the buffering capacity of the underlying rock constantly pulls the pH back up. Plants to avoid:

  • Rhododendron, azalea, camellia, pieris (all ericaceous; will chlorose yellow and die)
  • Blueberries, cranberry
  • Skimmia in thin chalk soil (tolerates alkalinity better than the above, but struggles on very shallow chalk)

Also worth noting: common laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is not ericaceous and tolerates chalk reasonably well in a sheltered position, but on thin chalk with poor summer moisture retention it shows leaf scorch in dry July and August spells. The trench preparation and mulching above improve its performance, but on the shallowest chalk profiles, Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) is a significantly better choice: it is more drought-tolerant and handles chalk-influenced soils more reliably.

Topsoil depth: when to import before planting

If your depth assessment at Step 1 shows less than 30cm of usable soil above solid chalk, importing topsoil before planting significantly improves outcomes. Aim to bring the effective workable depth to 50-60cm before you dig the trench. Use a good-quality, peat-free topsoil with a pH of 6.5-7.0 (not topsoil sourced from an acid heath or peat base, which will have a very low pH and will read as a good depth on the rod test while being entirely unsuitable for the species listed above). Spread the imported topsoil and fork it into the existing chalk-influenced surface before marking the trench line, so the transition between imported and original soil is gradual rather than a sharp layer boundary.
