How to prune a cherry laurel hedge: secateurs, timing and renovation for Broadstairs gardens.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is probably the most commonly planted garden hedge in Broadstairs and St Peter's. It grows fast, provides year-round screening, tolerates deep shade and establishes readily on the free-draining Margate Chalk under CT10. It also grows at a pace that catches people out: a newly planted laurel hedge can put on 60-90cm in a single growing season, and without a consistent pruning method the hedge quickly becomes a solid wall pressing against the house or a barrier blocking the path. This guide covers the step-by-step pruning method, the tool choice that determines whether your laurel looks crisp or brown-edged, the timing window on the Thanet coast, and the renovation approach for a laurel hedge that has run out of control.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the hedge and check for active nests before touching it

This check is legally required before any cut carried out between 1 March and 31 August. Cherry laurel provides excellent nesting cover: its dense, large-leaved canopy suits blackbirds, song thrushes, dunnocks and robins in particular. Walk the full length of the hedge slowly, watching for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section and listening for alarm calls or faint chick noise from inside the canopy. Pay particular attention to any section where the foliage is especially dense or where the hedge abuts a wall or fence, as birds often select these sheltered spots. If you find an active nest, mark the section and leave it uncut until the brood has fledged: most garden species complete a brood in three to five weeks from hatching.

Nesting season and cherry laurel: what the law actually requires

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest containing eggs or young, or to disturb a bird while it is on an active nest. The practical window when nesting is most likely in a Broadstairs garden hedge is 1 March to 31 August. Cherry laurel's year-round canopy and rapid spring growth make it a particularly favoured nesting site: I have found blackbird nests in laurel hedges in the Reading Street area as late as mid-August, well within the nesting window. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest, with up to six months in prison for aggravated offences. The check takes five minutes; skip it and the legal and moral consequence is entirely yours.

Step 2: Choose the right time to cut

Cherry laurel is not as sensitive to cut-timing as beech or hornbeam, but the Thanet coast adds a constraint that inland gardeners do not face. The two practical windows for CT10:

Late May to mid-June is the primary cut for a formally maintained hedge. By late May the spring flush of new growth has fully extended and hardened. Cutting at this point gives the hedge the whole summer to push a second, shorter flush of growth that will harden before autumn. The result is a dense, tight face going into winter.

Late August to mid-September is the secondary cut, or the only cut if you are doing a single annual trim. By late August most broods in a normal Broadstairs garden season have fledged, and the hedge can be cut without a nesting risk in the overwhelming majority of cases (the legal obligation to check still applies: always check before cutting, even in late August). September is the absolute deadline on the Thanet coast: a hard cut in October leaves fresh growth that can be damaged by the sea-wind frosts common on the CT10 coastal strip from late October onward, producing a patchy, bronzed face that will not recover until the following spring.

Do not cut a cherry laurel hedge in winter unless you are doing a structural renovation cut (see the renovation section below): winter-cut laurel puts its recovery energy into replacing lost foliage rather than extending, and the hedge face looks sparse and gappy until mid-summer.

Step 3: Choose your tool: secateurs or hedge trimmer?

This is the question that produces the most visible difference in finished results on a cherry laurel hedge, and the answer depends on how you weigh speed against appearance.

Secateurs and loppers give a clean finish. When you cut a cherry laurel stem at a node with a sharp pair of bypass secateurs, the leaf surface remains intact on both sides of the cut. The result is a hedge face that looks crisp and dark green within a week as the cut stems dry back neatly. Use secateurs on all stems you can reach comfortably, and loppers on thicker stems inside the canopy. Burncoose Nurseries, a well-established UK nursery in Cornwall with a YouTube video specifically on laurel pruning, recommends pruning individual stems back to a leaf joint with secateurs as the method that produces the neatest finish on large-leaved laurel. For a hedge up to 3m long, secateurs throughout are realistic for one person in a morning.

A powered hedge trimmer is faster on long runs but cuts straight through each leaf blade rather than at a stem junction. The sliced half-leaves on the cut surface turn brown within a few days, giving the finished hedge face a brown-flecked appearance for several weeks until new growth pushes through and covers the damage. On a long laurel hedge managed purely for screening rather than formal appearance, this may be an acceptable trade-off. On a hedge that is visible from the house or from the street, secateurs on the face give a markedly better result. A practical middle approach: use a hedge trimmer to do the bulk of the shaping and to remove the majority of the new growth, then follow up with secateurs to cut back individual leaves and stems that the trimmer left sliced on the face. The follow-up pass adds 20-30 minutes on a typical Broadstairs boundary hedge but makes a visible difference in the finished appearance.

Step 4: Set up safe access for the height involved

Mature cherry laurel hedges in Broadstairs gardens frequently reach 2-3m and sometimes considerably more. For hedges up to 2m, a pair of sturdy aluminium platform steps works well: the wide standing platform lets you work steadily along the hedge top without repositioning after every cut. Above 2m, scaffold platform steps with outriggers or a purpose-built hedge-cutting platform give a safer, more stable position than a standard ladder, and a better sightline along the top of the cut. Do not lean a standard ladder against a laurel hedge: the foliage provides no lateral support and the ladder base will slide on wet grass.

Laurel clippings are large and heavy when freshly cut. Lay a tarpaulin along the base of the hedge before you start; it makes collection much faster than raking up leaf fragments from a lawn or gravel path after the event.

Step 5: Cut the sides first, working from the base upward

Always cut the sides before the top. Working from the base upward gives you a consistent reference line and prevents cut material from falling onto sections you have already finished. Aim for a slight batter angle: the base of the hedge face should be wider than the top by roughly 80-120mm across a typical 1.5-2m face. A hedge cut with perfectly vertical sides eventually loses its lower foliage because the spreading upper growth shades out the base. On a cherry laurel that is already vertical or overhanging at the base, start correcting toward a batter angle from this cut onward, rather than trying to restore the full taper in a single season.

Work in sections of 1-1.5m, stepping back after each section to check the face is consistent before moving on. Cherry laurel produces irregular growth: some sections will have pushed 30-40cm further than adjacent ones in the same season, and a consistent finished face requires more frequent step-back checks than a fine-leaved species like privet.

Step 6: Set a string line and cut the top level

Measure the target cut height at both ends of the hedge run, drive a cane at each end, and run a taut string line between them at the target height. For runs over 6m, add a centre stake to prevent the line sagging: a 40-50mm sag produces a visible bow in the finished top of a long hedge run. Cherry laurel's irregular growth is particularly pronounced at the top: some leads may have grown 60cm or more beyond the general canopy in a single season, and without a string line as a consistent reference point, cutting these taller leads down while following the existing canopy profile results in a top that tracks the irregular growth rather than a level finished line.

Cut along the string line with a steady sweeping motion, removing growth down to the target height. Where individual stems are substantially higher than the string, cut them back with loppers before running the trimmer along the top: trying to force a hedge trimmer blade through a thick central stem overloads the blade and produces a rough, torn cut on the stem.

Step 7: Follow up with secateurs on the cut face

If you used a hedge trimmer for the bulk of the work, spend 20-30 minutes working along the cut face with secateurs, cutting back the sliced leaf stubs to the nearest stem joint. On each sliced leaf, make the cut at the node where the leaf meets the stem, leaving the remaining half of the leaf to drop naturally within a few weeks. This pass does not need to be exhaustive: concentrate on the most visible sections of the face, particularly the top third of the hedge that is at eye level from the street or from the house. The lower sections of the face are less visible and will green up from new growth in any case within four to six weeks.

Step 8: Collect and dispose of clippings

Cherry laurel clippings are toxic if ingested by dogs, horses or other animals: the leaves and stems contain hydrocyanic acid as a natural defence compound. Fresh clippings on a lawn are a hazard if animals access the garden. Roll up the tarpaulin and bag all clippings for the kerbside green bin collection or disposal at the Thanet District Council household waste recycling centre on Poorhole Lane, Broadstairs. Cherry laurel clippings can be hot-composted if your heap reaches sustained high temperatures, but cold-composting is slow because the waxy cuticle on laurel leaves resists breakdown: the kerbside bin is the simpler option for most Broadstairs gardens.

Why laurel needs different tool choice from yew, privet or Leylandii

The reason secateurs matter more on cherry laurel than on almost any other common garden hedge is the leaf size. Privet has leaves roughly 3-4cm long and 1-1.5cm wide: a hedge trimmer cuts through them cleanly and the sliced surface is small enough that the brown damage is barely noticeable against the overall face. Yew needles are 1-3cm long and narrow: trimmer damage is almost invisible. Cherry laurel leaves are 10-15cm long and 4-6cm wide: when a hedge trimmer blade cuts through the middle of a cherry laurel leaf, both halves remain attached to the stem and both turn visibly brown within two to three days. The larger the leaf, the more visible the damage, and cherry laurel is at the large end of the range among common UK garden hedge plants.

The same logic applies to any large-leaved laurel variant: Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) has smaller leaves than cherry laurel and responds slightly better to trimmer cutting, but still benefits from a secateurs follow-up pass on visible faces. Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) has leaves similar in size to Portuguese laurel and is occasionally used as an informal hedge in sheltered Broadstairs gardens.

Cherry laurel in Broadstairs and on the Thanet chalk

Cherry laurel is so widely planted in Broadstairs because it ticks most of the boxes that Victorian and Edwardian gardeners needed for a boundary hedge in a coastal town: it grows fast from a young plant, provides year-round privacy, tolerates the partial shade of terraced house gardens, and is not fussy about soil. The Margate Chalk under CT10 suits laurel well: it prefers a slightly alkaline to neutral pH and the free-draining chalk prevents the waterlogged root zone that laurel dislikes. I see established laurel hedges in the Reading Street conservation area and along the older terraces behind the town centre that are clearly 40-50 years old and still in good condition on chalk soil.

Salt wind is a constraint. Cherry laurel is not a front-line coastal plant: fully exposed sites above Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate will see leaf scorch and dieback in a hard winter gale, and laurel is not the right choice as the primary windbreak on a clifftop property directly exposed to the south-westerly. In a sheltered second-row position, behind Escallonia or Griselinia on the front line, laurel is fine even on the Thanet coast clifftop fringe. In the sheltered gardens of St Peter's, Broadstairs town, and the inland side of the conservation areas, where the cliff edge and roofline provide enough shelter from the direct salt blast, laurel performs entirely without modification. The coastal species guide covers front-line and second-line species choices for exposed Thanet sites in more detail.

One Broadstairs-specific note: if your cherry laurel hedge sits within the Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA or Reading Street CA and is over 75mm stem diameter at 1.5m height, it may be subject to the section 211 notification requirement under the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The standard annual pruning cut on an established hedge is exempt, but significant reduction or removal within a conservation area requires the six-week notice to Thanet District Council before works begin. The conservation areas guide covers this in detail, including the £20,000-per-tree fine for non-compliance.

Renovating an overgrown cherry laurel hedge

Cherry laurel shares with yew the valuable property of breaking freely from old wood: cut it back hard into bare brown stems and it will push new growth from dormant adventitious buds within one growing season. This makes laurel renovation, in principle, less stressful than a Leylandii renovation where cutting into the brown zone is effectively permanent.

In practice, a staged approach over two seasons gives better results than a simultaneous hard cut on all four sides:

In the first winter (November to February, outside the nesting season): cut one side hard back to the main structural stems, taking off as much as needed to restore the hedge to a manageable footprint. The cut face will look bare and brown immediately. By late spring the dormant buds along the woody stems will break, and by mid-summer a new flush of growth will have appeared across the face. By the following autumn, the face will be largely covered and will accept a normal shaping cut.

In the second winter: cut the back side and top in the same way. The simultaneous hard cut on all three remaining faces is safe in a vigorous laurel once one face has already recovered, as the root system has had a season to adjust. In the second growing season after this second cut, the hedge is typically back to a manageable, fully covered framework and will respond normally to annual pruning from that point onward.

Where the top has grown to 3-4m on a hedge that was intended to sit at 2m, a top reduction of more than 50% of total height in one cut is feasible on cherry laurel (unlike Leylandii, where this is a death sentence), but give the plant a full growing season before trimming the new growth back to the target finished height: the first season's recovery flush on a hard-cut laurel produces long, vigorous upright shoots that look untidy but are generating the photosynthetic capacity the plant needs to fuel further recovery. Let them grow through the first season after the renovation cut, then cut back to target height in the following autumn or winter.
