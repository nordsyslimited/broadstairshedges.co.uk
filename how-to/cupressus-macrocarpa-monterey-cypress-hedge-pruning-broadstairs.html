How to prune a Monterey cypress hedge in Broadstairs: timing, the no-brown-wood rule and canker watch for CT10.

Cupressus macrocarpa (Monterey cypress) is the Thanet coast's default windbreak species: you see it as a dense green screen above Kingsgate, along North Foreland garden boundaries and in the sheltered yards behind the seafront terraces at Stone Bay. It tolerates salt wind, grows fast enough to give you a hedge in three to four years from whip-stage, and in the right spot it stays healthy for decades. The pruning rule that overrides everything else: never cut into the brown dead zone. Unlike yew, macrocarpa will not regenerate from old wood, and a cut into the brown is permanent.

Steps

Step 1: Check for nesting birds and confirm you are in the pruning window

Walk the full length of the hedge before picking up any tool. Watch for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section, listen for nestling calls from inside the canopy, and look for nest material pushed to the edge of the foliage. If you find or suspect an active nest, stop and wait. The legal pruning window for macrocarpa is April to the end of August (the growing season), but that window overlaps entirely with the main nesting season, so the nest check must always come first.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: the nesting-season overlap

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly take, damage or destroy a nest while it is in use or being built. Monterey cypress hedges are frequently used by house sparrows and song thrushes, both of which can nest from March through to August. Near clifftop buildings, herring-gull nests on any adjacent surface carry the same legal protection. The penalty on summary conviction is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine per nest, or both. A nest check is not optional: it is how you stay on the right side of the Act.

Step 2: Find the green zone before the cut

Part your gloved hand into the hedge from the outside surface, pushing 5-10cm into the foliage. The outer layer is live green growth. Behind it, where the light has not penetrated for several seasons, is a zone of brown dead wood that holds no live buds. The safe cutting depth is within the green zone only. On a well-maintained macrocarpa hedge this is typically 10-20cm from the surface. On a hedge that has not been cut for two or more seasons, the green zone may be shallower as the live foliage has moved outward.

Mark the hedge face before cutting if needed. A length of coloured twine stretched along the hedge at the intended finished depth is a reliable guide on a long run. On the top surface, use two canes and a string line as you would for any formal hedge, set at the finished height, to give you a level reference as you work.

Step 3: Set tools and lay a tarpaulin

You need a hedge trimmer with a blade 45-55cm long (a longer blade is harder to control precisely at the green-zone boundary), a good pair of thick gloves, and a tarpaulin at the base of the hedge to catch clippings. On a large hedge, a telescopic hedge trimmer with a straight or adjustable head makes the top surface manageable without a ladder. Check blade sharpness: a blunt blade tears foliage rather than cutting it and leaves brown frayed edges that take weeks to green over. Wipe blades with a disinfectant cloth between hedges to reduce Coryneum canker spore transfer, a step that matters more here than for many other species.

Step 4: Trim the sides, bottom to top, staying in the green zone

Work from one end to the other on the near face, using smooth sweeping strokes from below and keeping the blade parallel to the hedge surface. The batter angle applies here as it does to any formal hedge: keep the base slightly wider than the top so the lower portions receive light year-round. On an exposed clifftop garden where the seaward face takes salt scorch and wind desiccation, a slightly more pronounced batter (base 15-20cm wider than the top on a 2m hedge) helps maintain foliage density at the base over several seasons.

At each pass, take off no more growth than brings you back to 8-10cm inside the previous season's cut surface. Two light cuts in a season at that depth do far less damage than one hard cut that risks breaching the green zone. Once the near face is done, repeat for the far side before moving to the top.

Step 5: Trim the top to the string line

Work along the top with the blade held horizontally at the string line. Check from 5-6m back every few metres. Monterey cypress grows vigorously at the apex on sheltered sites, and the top can put on 30-40cm in a season, so check the depth of green on the top surface before cutting: a lush top with dense growth will have a good green zone; a top that was cut heavily last year may be shallow. On a lollipop-shaped hedge that has grown too tall, a single-season reduction of the top by up to one-third is usually safe. More than that risks cutting into old wood on the upper profile.

Step 6: Inspect cuts for Coryneum canker signs

After the main trimming, step along the hedge and look at the cut surfaces and at the stems behind the cut line. Coryneum canker (Seiridium cardinale) enters through wounds, particularly cuts made in wet or humid conditions, and it is the most serious disease risk for macrocarpa in UK coastal gardens. The signs are: elongated sunken patches of discoloured bark, often with orange-red or dark-brown resin bleeding out from the affected area, and a wedge-shaped zone of yellowing and dying foliage above the canker point. Small affected branches can be cut out well below the sunken area and the prunings removed from the site rather than composted. On the main framework stems, there is no cure: an established canker in a stem that holds a large portion of the hedge will spread. In that case a staged replacement of the affected section, planting new young stock at the base to grow through, is the most realistic option.

Step 7: Clear clippings and record the cut depth

Fold the tarpaulin inward and bag or barrow the clippings. Macrocarpa clippings do not compost well in a cold domestic heap; they are better in a council green-waste bin or taken to the recycling centre at Poorhole Lane, Broadstairs. Before tidying up, note the finished height and profile of the hedge: this season's cut depth becomes next season's reference for where the green zone starts. A quick photograph from the end of the hedge run, level with the hedge, is a reliable record of the profile and height.

Why macrocarpa is different from Leylandii and Thuja

All three are fast-growing conifer screens used along the Thanet coast, and all share the no-regeneration-from-old-wood rule. The differences matter in practice. Leylandii is more vigorous but less salt-tolerant than macrocarpa: on a first-line clifftop site that takes direct North Sea wind, macrocarpa generally outperforms Leylandii in the long run. Thuja plicata (Western Red Cedar) is the most drought-tolerant of the three on the thin Margate Chalk, but it is more susceptible to foliage scorch from direct salt wind than macrocarpa. Macrocarpa sits in the middle: it is the best of the three for a front-line coastal screen on a CT10 site where direct salt wind is combined with periodic dry periods. It is rated RHS hardiness H3, meaning it is reliable in coastal and relatively mild UK locations but can suffer foliage damage in hard inland frosts (rare on the Thanet coast, more relevant to gardens well back from the shore).

The other key difference is canker susceptibility. Leylandii and Thuja carry some canker risk, but macrocarpa is more vulnerable to Coryneum canker than either, and the risk increases if the hedge is cut in wet weather or with blades that have not been disinfected between jobs. A Leylandii hedge can tolerate a damp-day autumn cut more readily than a macrocarpa hedge: if possible, cut macrocarpa only in dry weather during a settled April-August spell.

The Goldcrest variety: trim schedule and foliage character

Most macrocarpa sold in CT10 garden centres and by mail-order nurseries is the variety Cupressus macrocarpa 'Goldcrest': a columnar form with bright yellow-green to soft gold foliage and a strong lemon scent when cut. It is often sold as a patio specimen or gap-filler hedge, but it is also planted in rows to create a golden screen on sheltered Broadstairs garden boundaries and in conserved-area gardens where a formal dark-green hedge is not appropriate. The pruning rules for Goldcrest are the same as for green macrocarpa: April-August window, green zone only, disinfect blades. Goldcrest can also be trimmed lightly in late spring with hand shears to tighten its columnar shape: a single light shear of 3-5cm in May, before the main summer growth, sets a denser internal structure that needs less correction later.

Cypress aphid: when brown patches are not canker

Cypress aphid (Cinara cupressivora) is a sap-sucking pest that causes patches of yellow and then brown foliage in late summer and early autumn, typically on Leylandii and macrocarpa. The key difference from Coryneum canker: aphid damage appears as patches of general browning in the inner foliage without the bark lesions, sunken stems or resin bleeding that characterise canker. Look at the stems behind the brown patch: if the bark is normal in colour and texture, with no sunken or resinous areas, aphid is the more likely cause. Biological controls (parasitic wasps are active on the Thanet coast) usually keep aphid populations in check on established hedges; a damp cloth wiped along affected stems in May removes early colonies before they build up. Insecticide sprays applied to established hedges are rarely warranted and risk harm to beneficial insects.
