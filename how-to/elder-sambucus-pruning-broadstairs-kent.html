How to prune elder in a Broadstairs hedgerow: annual trim, renovation coppice and CT10 timing notes.

Elder (Sambucus nigra) is the fifth most common hedgerow species in England, and in a Broadstairs garden or field boundary it almost certainly arrived uninvited: a seed dropped by a blackbird, a shoot from a root run under the fence, a seedling missed until it was already a metre tall. The elderflower in June and the elderberries in September and October are among the most valuable things a CT10 hedge can offer to bees, hoverflies and migrating birds. The problem is that elder grows vigorously, outpaces its hedgerow companions, and turns into a wide, woody trunk that crowds out hawthorn and blackthorn if it is not managed. The pruning approach is simple once you have made the key decision: annual trim, or full coppice renovation.

Steps

Step 1: Confirm the timing window before touching any tool

Elder is best pruned between November and late February, while it is dormant and fully leafless. This window avoids the nesting season (1 March to 31 August under the practical guidance issued alongside the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981) and it avoids disturbing the elderberry harvest, which finishes when fieldfares and redwings have stripped the remaining fruit in October. On the Thanet coast, the main fieldfare and redwing arrival from Scandinavia happens in October, and the berry crop in a CT10 elder hedge is typically gone by early November: late October is therefore the natural start of the working window.

Do not prune elder in September or early October. The berries are still present and in active use by migrating birds. Do not prune it in March or later: nesting blackbirds, robins, blackcaps and whitethroats use elder as a preferred nesting site and will be active from early March onward in a sheltered CT10 garden.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: elder is prime nesting habitat

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly take, damage or destroy an active nest. Elder's dense branching structure makes it one of the most heavily used nesting shrubs in a mixed CT10 hedge: blackbird, robin, blackcap, and house sparrow all use it regularly. The practical caution window runs from 1 March to 31 August; a nest in elder can be present from early March. Penalty on summary conviction: up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine per nest, or both. The November to late February window is not just the horticulturally correct window, it is the legally safe one.

Step 2: Decide whether to annual trim or fully coppice

Elder managed regularly every one to three years responds well to an annual trim that keeps it within the hedge line. Elder that has been left unmanaged for four or more years, or that has developed a wide, woody trunk dominant over its neighbours, needs a full coppice renovation rather than a trim.

Use this check to make the decision:

  • Annual trim: the elder section is roughly in line with the rest of the hedge; stems are green-barked and mostly under 50mm diameter; the base has not thickened into a wide woody stool wider than about 300mm. Trim back to shape using the same technique as for the rest of the hedge, cutting back to a lateral or bud within the existing hedge profile.
  • Full coppice renovation: the elder is clearly taller and wider than its neighbours; the base stool is broad and woody; secondary stems are spreading out horizontally rather than growing upright within the hedge line; or the hawthorn and blackthorn neighbours have clearly been suppressed. Cut the elder section back to 100-200mm stumps above ground level, as described in Step 4 below.

Step 3: Annual maintenance trim

For elder sections that have been managed in the last two or three years, the annual trim is straightforward. Cut back the current season's long, whippy growth to within the hedge profile, removing about one third of the previous season's extension. Elder puts on 600-1,000mm of growth in a good season on the fast-draining Margate Chalk that most CT10 gardens sit on, so this is a meaningful cut, not a cosmetic one.

Use loppers or a pruning saw for stems thicker than 25mm. A hedge trimmer will handle the fine growth but will tear rather than cut cleanly on stems much thicker than 15mm, leaving ragged ends that brown off and slow regrowth. Work from the base up and from the outside in: clear the obvious long leaders first, then step back and look at the hedge line before cutting to shape. Elder sap stains tools and clothing; gloves and old clothes are worth the inconvenience.

After trimming, walk the base of the hedge and remove any seedling elders that have germinated outside the hedge line. Elder seeds prolifically and seedlings establish quickly in the free-draining chalk soil: a seedling caught before it is 300mm tall can be pulled by hand; anything larger needs cutting and root-pulling.

Step 4: Full coppice renovation of an overgrown elder section

Full coppice cuts elder down to short stumps of 100-200mm above ground level. This looks severe but elder is one of the most coppice-tolerant native hedgerow species: the RHS classifies it in pruning group 1 and notes it can be coppiced every three to four years without harm. The stool will regrow vigorously in the first growing season, sending up multiple new vertical shoots from the cut stumps and from the lateral roots.

To coppice an elder section in a mixed hedge, work as follows. Clear any bramble or other climbers from the elder section first, so you can see the full structure. Cut all stems down to 100-200mm above ground level using a pruning saw or loppers, working from the outside of the hedge inward. Leave the stumps as low as practical: a stool cut at 100mm regenerates more tidily than one cut at 400mm, which tends to produce a ruff of epicormic shoots from the cut surface rather than clean new growth from the base. Stack the cut material out of the hedge base immediately as you work, so you can see what remains.

The coppice stumps look bare and dead for the first six weeks. From April onward, new growth will emerge from the base and from the stumps. By July of the first year after coppicing, a healthy elder stool will typically have sent up new stems of 500-800mm on the fast-growing Margate Chalk conditions in CT10. Leave those shoots alone for the first season. In the second season after coppicing, select three or four of the strongest vertical shoots from each stool to form the new hedge framework and cut the remainder back to the base: this encourages upright hedge-line growth rather than a spreading stool. From year three onward, manage the reformed elder section on the annual trim schedule described in Step 3.

Step 5: Clear and dispose of the cut material promptly

Elder cuttings should not be left lying in or against the hedge base. Stems in contact with damp soil will root if left for more than a few days in autumn and winter, creating a tangle of small rooted cuttings that complicate future management. This is not a theoretical risk: elder is propagated commercially by hardwood cuttings taken in exactly this season, and the rooting success rate is high on the moist chalk-loam soils found in sheltered CT10 gardens.

Remove all cut material from the hedge bottom on the same day it is cut. For a renovation coppice with a significant volume of woody material, the most practical disposal route is chipping, either on site if you have access to a chipper or through Thanet District Council's household waste recycling centre on Poorhole Lane, Margate. The chips can go into the compost or be used as mulch around other shrubs, though avoid mulching directly over bulb plantings as the fresh chips can cause nitrogen draw-down as they break down. Green leafy elder trimmings from an annual summer trim (in the unlikely event you are tidying in late August) can go into the domestic compost, but the woody winter cuttings are better chipped.

Elder in the Broadstairs landscape

Sambucus nigra is a native UK shrub that colonises disturbed ground, chalk banks, cliff edges, and field boundaries with little encouragement. In CT10, it turns up in the hedgerows along the Reading Street road fringe, in the chalk-rubble banks at the edges of the Broadstairs and St Peter's Conservation Areas, and in virtually any mixed hawthorn hedge that has gone unmanaged for a decade or more. It is a pioneer species on chalk: the shallow, alkaline, fast-draining Margate Chalk (pH 7.5-8.0) that most CT10 gardens sit on does not slow elder down the way it slows some other hedgerow species, and it establishes and grows vigorously even on thin chalk soils where hawthorn and blackthorn are slower.

The problem identified by UK hedging practitioners is specific: elder tends to form a wide, woody, spreading base that crowds out its hedgerow companions over ten to fifteen years. A hawthorn hedge that included elder at planting often loses hawthorn sections to shading as the elder expands, and the elder's horizontal spread means the hedge profile widens outward rather than thickening inward. Regular management keeps this in check. Coppicing an overgrown elder section does not destroy the elder: it resets the clock on a trajectory that otherwise ends with the elder dominating the whole hedge.

Wildlife value: elderflowers, berries and the migrant connection

Elder in a CT10 hedge provides two major wildlife windows each year. In late May and June, the flat-topped flowerheads (corymbs) produce large quantities of nectar and pollen accessible to a wide range of insects: hoverflies, bumblebees, solitary bees and small beetles all use elderflower extensively. The flower timing coincides with peak nesting activity, so an elder in flower in late May is simultaneously providing food for adult insects and cover for nesting birds, which is why the nesting-season caution applies so directly to it.

In September and October, the elderberry crop is one of the richest fruit sources in a Broadstairs hedge. Blackbirds begin taking berries from late August; starlings from September; and from mid-October the first fieldfares and redwings arrive on the Thanet coast from Scandinavia, using the coast as a navigation line and landing in gardens and clifftop hedgerows to feed on exactly this kind of fruiting shrub. The arrival of fieldfares above Viking Bay in mid-October is a reliable seasonal marker in CT10. By early November most berry crops in sheltered gardens are stripped, which is why the November to late February pruning window aligns so neatly with the post-harvest, pre-nesting period.

Elder and conservation areas in Broadstairs

The four conservation areas in Broadstairs (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA, Kingsgate CA) impose a requirement to give six weeks' notice to Thanet District Council before cutting, uprooting or topping any tree with a trunk diameter above 75mm at 1.5 metres from ground level, under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Elder in a managed hedge is almost always cut before individual stems reach 75mm diameter, and the hedge itself is not a "tree" for section 211 purposes. However, a standalone elder that has grown into a multi-stemmed specimen over many years without any management could reach this threshold, particularly if it has developed from a base stool rather than a single trunk. If the elder you are managing is clearly a hedge component and is being managed as such, section 211 does not apply. If it is a large, free-standing specimen in a garden within one of the four Broadstairs conservation areas, check the trunk diameter before cutting: anything at or above 75mm at 1.5 metres needs TDC notification first.
