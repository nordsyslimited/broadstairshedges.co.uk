How to prune and coppice a hazel hedge in Broadstairs: annual trim vs coppice rotation for CT10 native hedgerows.

Hazel is one of the most common native hedgerow shrubs in the Kent countryside around Reading Street and the farmland west of Broadstairs, and it turns up regularly in CT10 garden wildlife hedges planted in the last twenty years. Unlike formal clipping hedges, hazel does not respond well to being buzzed over annually with a trimmer: it builds up congested, twiggy growth that progressively loses vigour and wildlife value. The right management is either a light annual formative trim while young, or a periodic renovation coppice on a five to eight year cycle once the hedge is established. This guide sets out exactly when and how to carry out each approach.

Steps

Step 1: Check for nesting birds before any cutting begins

Walk the full length of the hazel hedge slowly, from one end to the other, before picking up any tool. Hazel is a first-class nesting habitat: the dense, layered canopy and lower branch structure attract blackcap, whitethroat, dunnock, song thrush and wren, among others. In a CT10 garden wildlife hedge you may also have robin, chaffinch or long-tailed tit nesting within the hazel section.

Look for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same part of the hedge, carrying food or nesting material, or behaving as though disturbed. If you find a nest with eggs or dependent young, or a behaviour pattern that indicates an active nest you cannot see, stop. Leave that section alone until the brood has fledged, which is typically two to three weeks from hatching for small passerines. You can continue with sections clearly free of active nesting, but return to inspect the stopped section before proceeding.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: hazel hedges and the nesting season

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a bird on an active nest. For hazel hedges in CT10, the practical enforcement window runs from 1 March to 31 August, though blackbird and song thrush nests appear as early as mid-February in sheltered positions along the south-facing terrace gardens of St Peter's and inland Broadstairs. The safest management window for any renovation coppice is October through to the end of January: well within the dormant season and well outside the nesting window. Annual light trims in late January or early February sit at the edge of the safe window; check first. Penalty for an offence: an unlimited fine per nest, with up to six months in prison for aggravated cases.

Step 2: Decide which management regime the hedge needs

The correct regime depends on the age and current state of the hedge. Use this two-way guide:

Annual light trim: suitable for young hazel hedges (one to five years old) and for established hedges being maintained at a fixed height and width. Use a bowsaw or secateurs rather than a hedge trimmer: hazel's large, soft leaves are bruised and browned by spinning trimmer blades, and the result looks ragged for months. The light trim removes the current season's growth by about one third, encourages branching, and holds the hedge to its target dimensions without opening it to the coarse regrowth that annual hard cutting produces.

Renovation coppice: suitable for established hazel hedges that have grown beyond their intended size, have become congested and twiggy at the centre, or have not been managed for several years. Coppicing cuts all stems back to near ground level and prompts vigorous new growth from the base. It is the traditional management method for hazel in British hedgerows and woodland, produces useful material (pea sticks, bean poles, hedge stakes, hurdle-making rods), and restores the hedge's wildlife value by opening the structure and stimulating dense new regrowth.

Step 3: For an annual light trim, cut in late January or February during dormancy

The target window for an annual formative trim on a young hazel hedge is late January to mid-February, while the plant is still fully dormant but before catkin display peaks. Hazel produces catkins from late January onward: the long yellow male catkins are a key early-season pollen source for queen bumblebees, and trimming before they open is preferable to cutting them off mid-display.

Use a sharp bowsaw for any stems thicker than 15mm, and sharp bypass secateurs for thinner shoots. Work along the hedge, shortening new growth by roughly one third and shaping to a slight inward taper (wider at the base than the top) to keep light reaching the lower stems. Remove any crossing or rubbing branches to keep the interior open. Take out dead, damaged or diseased wood completely, cutting back to a healthy outward-facing bud or branch junction. Do not use a reciprocating hedge trimmer on hazel: the blade damage to the large, soft leaves is visible through the following growing season.

On a newly planted hazel hedge in its first or second year, cut back all stems by about a third immediately after planting or in the first February, to encourage branching from the base. This is the single most important formative cut: hazel not cut back hard in year one develops as a collection of individual stems rather than a dense interconnected hedge.

Step 4: For a renovation coppice, assess which stools to cut and plan the timing

If the hazel hedge has not been managed for five or more years, or has become very tall and congested, a renovation coppice is the correct approach. Before cutting, walk the hedge and assess:

  • Are there any dead stools (plants that have died back to the base and show no live growth)? Mark these: they need to come out and be replaced, not just cut back.
  • Are there any gaps in the hedge line? A renovation coppice will temporarily make gaps more visible; plan gap-filling planting to coincide with the coppice work.
  • Is the hedge in a conservation area? Hazel is a native hedgerow species and its management is generally permitted development, but check whether any stems approach tree size (over 75mm diameter at 1.5m height): large specimens in a Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA or Reading Street CA may require a section 211 notification to Thanet District Council before felling. The threshold for individual stems is unlikely to be triggered on a managed garden hedge, but worth confirming if any stems look substantial.

Timing for a renovation coppice: carry out all coppice cutting between October and the end of January, ideally in November or December. This puts the cut well outside the 1 March-31 August nesting season and well within full dormancy. Avoid coppicing in late February or March even in a mild spell: hazel sap is already rising and the plant is entering its catkin season.

Step 5: Execute the coppice cut, working from the outside in

Have sharp tools: a bowsaw for stems up to about 50mm diameter, a pruning saw or coupe-saw for larger stems, and loppers or secateurs for thin material. A billhook is useful for trimming loose bark from the cut surface, though it is not essential. Lay a tarpaulin at the base of the hedge before you start: hazel coppice produces a large volume of material and laying it off the ground as you go is much faster than raking after the fact.

Work from the outside of the clump inward, taking outer stems first so that you have room to work towards the centre without tripping on cut material. Cut each stem down to a stool height of no more than 50-75mm above the ground, making a clean angled cut to shed water. The angle is important: a flat horizontal cut on the stool holds moisture and encourages fungal rots. A slight 30-45 degree bevel, cut just above a visible bud or node if one is present, gives the best result.

Do not cut completely flush with the ground: a stool left at 50-75mm responds more vigorously than a flush cut, and the remaining nub protects the root zone from soil compaction during the clearing work that follows. On Thanet chalk where the topsoil is often thin (200-300mm over solid chalk), this protection matters: bruised or damaged surface roots on thin chalk soils recover slowly.

Sort cut material as you go. Stems 10-25mm diameter and 1.0-1.5m long are excellent pea and bean sticks (use them as cut or split with a knife for finer supports). Longer, thicker stems (25-50mm, 2m+) make good hedge stakes and bean poles. Very thin material (under 10mm) is best composted or bundled as habitat piles against a fence or the hedge base itself: small log and twig piles at the hedge foot are valuable overwintering habitat for hedgehogs, slow worms and ground beetles.

Step 6: Aftercare in the first season after coppicing

New shoots emerge from the coppice stools from late March onward. Hazel regenerates rapidly: by the end of the first growing season, new shoots commonly reach 600mm-1m in height on a healthy stool on well-drained chalk. By the end of the second season, 1.5-2m is typical in CT10 conditions.

In the first growing season, one management task is important: check the new growth at the stool for any competing vegetation that may swamp the regenerating shoots. On CT10 chalk, bramble can colonise the cleared base quickly, and ground elder is also common in older gardens. Cut or pull competing vegetation at the stool base in April-May and again in July if needed, leaving a 300mm clear radius around each stool. Do not apply herbicide at the stool base: hazel roots run close to the surface on thin chalk soils and the root bark is vulnerable.

Mulch the cleared hedge base with a 75mm layer of bark mulch or woodchip (your own coppice waste, if chipped) applied to the soil between stools. On thin Margate Chalk, the mulch helps retain moisture during dry spells, which matters in the first summer after coppicing when the new shoots are establishing their root systems in competition with any neighbouring plants. Leave a 50mm gap between mulch and stem bases.

No feeding is necessary in the first year: hazel is a light feeder that does well on the alkaline, mineral-rich Thanet chalk without supplementary fertiliser. On heavily impoverished soil or where the hedge has been in a lawned area with compacted roots, a light dressing of balanced granular fertiliser (e.g. Growmore at the label rate) in April can help, but it is not standard practice.

Hazel in the Broadstairs and Thanet landscape

Common hazel (Corylus avellana) is native throughout the British Isles and one of the most abundant hedgerow shrubs in the Kent countryside. The lane hedgerows around Reading Street, the footpaths running west from Broadstairs towards Minster and Monkton, and the remnant field boundaries in the farmland between the Thanet towns include hazel as a regular component, often mixed with hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and elder in a classic chalk-downland hedgerow type.

In CT10 gardens, hazel turns up in two contexts: as a component of a native wildlife hedge planted deliberately for biodiversity value, and as an inherited plant in older gardens where hedgerow species were encouraged to grow on rather than being replaced with formal evergreens. In both cases, the management approach is the same: do not treat hazel like privet or box and clip it annually into submission. Hazel is a multi-stemmed woodland edge shrub that thrives on periodic hard cutting followed by a rest period; the hedge trimmer annually removes the best-quality regrowth without ever allowing the plant to mature through its natural coppice cycle.

Hazel handles the Margate Chalk formation well. The fast-draining, alkaline (pH 7.5-8.0) chalk soils of CT10 suit hazel better than they suit some hedging species: it is naturally associated with chalk downland and calcareous woodland-edge habitat across Kent and does not suffer the chlorosis that affects species preferring neutral or acidic soil. On the thinner soils above the chalk, hazel benefits from the mulching regime described in Step 6, particularly in the first two to three years after planting or coppicing.

Wildlife value: why hazel hedges earn their place in CT10 gardens

Hazel offers wildlife value at every season, which distinguishes it from many formal hedging species:

Late winter (January-February): the male catkins are one of the earliest pollen sources of the year in CT10 gardens, opening before most flowers. Queen buff-tailed bumblebees, which come out of hibernation on warm January and February days, use hazel catkins as a first food source. On sunny south-facing hedge sections in St Peter's and Broadstairs town, catkins can open as early as the last week of January in mild winters.

Spring and summer: the leaf canopy supports a high invertebrate community. Hazel sawfly, hazel leaf roller moth, nut weevil and a range of aphid species all feed on hazel foliage, and this insect diversity in turn supports nesting insectivorous birds. The dense, layered structure of an uncut or recently coppiced hazel section is preferred nesting habitat for blackcap and whitethroat, both summer visitors to Thanet gardens from late April.

Autumn: hazel nuts (ripe from late August to October in CT10) are collected by wood mice, grey squirrels and bank voles. Dormice, where they are present in Kent hedgerow networks, are entirely dependent on hazel nuts as a pre-hibernation food source, though dormice are now very rare in Thanet and are unlikely to be present in an urban CT10 garden. Nut harvest by squirrels and wood mice is no reason to delay management work: both species relocate cached nuts when the hedge is later coppiced.

A hazel hedge that has been coppiced within the last two to three years also provides open, sunny ground flora habitat at the base that is valuable for ground-nesting bumblebees and solitary bees in spring.

Planting new hazel in a CT10 wildlife hedge

If you are establishing a new native wildlife hedge and want to include hazel, plant bare-root specimens (available November to March) at 400-450mm spacing, mixed with hawthorn and blackthorn as the primary structural species and field maple, dogwood and wild rose as the secondary species. This is a standard Kentish hedgerow species mix and suits Thanet chalk well.

Do not plant hazel as the dominant species in a formal boundary hedge intended for annual clipping: hazel does not produce the fine-textured, consistently dense growth that formal clipping requires, and the large leaves bruise and brown under trimmer blades. Use hornbeam, beech or field maple for a formal deciduous hedge on chalk. Use hazel where you want a looser, wilder hedge that will be managed on a rotation rather than clipped annually.

Bare-root hazel is inexpensive: 40-60cm transplants typically cost under £1.50 per plant in bundles of 25, making a 10m hazel section achievable for under £35 in bare-root stock. Pot-grown hazel is available year-round but more expensive and offers no advantage on chalk soil where bare-root establishment is reliable.
