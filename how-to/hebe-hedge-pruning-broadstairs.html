How to prune a Hebe hedge: timing, the old-wood rule and coastal varieties for Broadstairs CT10.

Hebe is one of the most reliably salt-tolerant evergreens you can grow on the Thanet coast. I see it throughout the older gardens of Broadstairs and St Peter's, along the sheltered terraces above Viking Bay, and tucked into the lee-side positions of clifftop properties where the exposure allows enough shelter to protect its softer varieties through a hard winter. Its tolerance of salt wind, its flowering through late summer, and its year-round density make it a useful garden hedge. But Hebe is unforgiving of one specific pruning mistake: cut into the old, bare, leafless wood at its base, and the plant will not recover. This guide gives the step-by-step method for the annual post-flowering trim, the nesting-season check that comes first, and the careful renovation approach for a hebe that has been left too long.

Steps

Step 1: Carry out the nesting-season check before you begin

The main pruning window for Hebe falls in late August and early September, after the summer flowering flush. This places the trim squarely within the legal nesting-season caution window for UK garden hedges: 1 March to 31 August. Hebe's dense, low growth and evergreen leaf cover make it attractive to nesting birds, particularly dunnocks, wrens and robins, which build low in sheltered evergreen shrubs. Before you put a blade anywhere near the plant, walk slowly around the full perimeter. Watch for adult birds flying in and out of the same section of growth repeatedly. Listen for alarm calls or soft chick-begging calls from inside the canopy. Pay particular attention to any section where the growth is especially thick, where the plant grows against a wall or fence, or where it is sheltered from the prevailing wind on its south- or west-facing side. If you find an active nest, mark the section with a cane, leave it entirely alone, and return after the brood has fledged, typically three to five weeks from hatching.

Hebe pruning in August: cutting inside the nesting window

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest containing eggs or young, or to disturb a nesting bird on or near an active nest. The standard caution window for garden hedges in Broadstairs is 1 March to 31 August. A late-August hebe trim, the timing that produces the best results for next year's flowering, sits right at the boundary of this window. In practice this means the nest check is not optional, even on a date you carry out every year. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest, with a potential prison term for aggravated offences.

Step 2: Confirm that flowering is complete before you cut

Hebe flowers on the current season's growth. After the flowers fade, the same shoots continue to extend slightly, and it is the new growth tips on those shoots that will carry next year's flower buds. If you cut before the flowers have fully finished, you shorten the current year's display. If you cut too late in the season, into October and November, the new growth carrying next year's buds has not had time to harden off before the autumn frosts arrive, and the plant goes into winter with vulnerable soft wood at its tips.

Walk the full plant and read its state. In a typical Broadstairs season, most hebe varieties complete their main summer flush somewhere between late July and early September depending on variety, aspect and exposure. A south-facing plant against a warm garden wall will often finish earlier than a north-facing open-ground specimen. The indicator you are looking for: the majority of flower spikes are now brown and spent, no significant new spikes are emerging from the growing tips, and you can see that the stem tips themselves are still green and leafy rather than still in active flower. If the plant is still in full flower across most of its length, wait two weeks and check again.

Step 3: Gather the right tools and confirm the old-wood boundary

Before picking up any cutting tool, take a few minutes to read the plant from the outside in. Push your hand carefully into the outer canopy at several points and look at what lies beneath the green surface. You should find:

  • Green zone: the outer 100-200mm of the canopy, where the current and previous season's growth is still clothed in leaves. This is the zone you can safely cut.
  • Transition zone: stems that have some leaves but with visible bare patches, 200-350mm inside the canopy. Cut into this zone cautiously if renovation is needed, only to a point where leaves are still visible.
  • Old-wood zone: bare, brown or grey stems with no leaves and no visible buds, towards the centre and base of the plant. Do not cut into this zone. Unlike yew, cherry laurel or box, Hebe does not reliably regenerate new shoots from old leafless wood. A cut deep into the old-wood zone typically produces dieback all the way down to the cut point, with no recovery. This is the single most common cause of hebe failure in Broadstairs gardens.

Tools for the annual trim:

  • Sharp secateurs: the primary tool, for cutting individual stems back to a leaf node. Bypass secateurs, not anvil types. Hebe stems, even in the green zone, are semi-woody and crush rather than cut cleanly with a blunt or anvil blade.
  • Hand shears or electric hedge trimmer: suitable for small-leaved varieties (Hebe rakaiensis, whipcord types, Hebe pinguifolia) where the leaf damage from trimmer blades is not visible. For large-leaved varieties (Hebe salicifolia, Hebe 'Midsummer Beauty'), stick to secateurs: trimmer-cut large leaves brown at the cut edge and remain visible through the following season.

Step 4: Deadhead all spent flower spikes

Work around the full plant using secateurs and remove every brown, spent flower spike. Cut each one back to just above the nearest pair of healthy leaves below it. This is not optional tidying: leaving spent spikes in place reduces the number of new growth tips that form behind the old flower positions, and in some hebe varieties it suppresses the repeat-flowering tendency that produces a second, lighter flush in autumn.

In most Broadstairs gardens, this step alone takes the majority of the pruning time. A hebe with a good season's flower display will have hundreds of spent spikes, and working through them systematically by hand is slower than a single pass with shears. But it produces a far more precise result and keeps you well clear of the old-wood boundary that shears can reach through inadvertently.

Step 5: Trim the outer shape, cutting back to leaf nodes only

Once the deadheading is complete, step back and assess the overall profile of the plant. A well-maintained hebe grown as an informal screen or low hedge should have a rounded, mounded profile, wider at the base than at the top, with the outer surface a consistent green without bare patches.

Where the overall shape is acceptable, the deadheading alone may be sufficient for the season. Where growth has been uneven, or where individual stems are substantially longer than their neighbours, use secateurs to cut the overlong stems back to a leaf node just inside the general profile of the plant. Work stem by stem, not in a continuous shear pass. Each cut should land immediately above a node where leaves are attached: this is where the new shoot will break from. A cut in mid-internodal space (between two nodes) leaves a short dead stub that slowly dies back to the node below, creating bare patches on the surface.

Remove no more than one-third of the overall height and width in a single season. Attempting to make a large structural reduction in one pass almost always takes the cuts further into the transition zone than intended, and on a stressed or elderly plant it produces more dieback than the plant can recover from in a single growing season.

Step 6: If the plant is very large, renovate over three seasons

A hebe that has been left uncut for several years, or that was hard-pruned at the wrong time by a previous owner, often presents as a large, semi-open plant: tall, with the green living growth concentrated on the outer surface and a mass of bare woody stems filling the centre. The outer surface still looks reasonable; the problem is inside.

Attempting to hard cut a plant in this state in a single season, back to a fraction of its size, almost invariably fails. The cut will reach the old-wood zone, there are no leaf nodes to break from, and the sections cut back to bare wood simply die.

The approach that actually works is a staged renovation over three seasons:

  • Season 1 (late August): cut back the outer third of the volume using the standard post-flowering method. Deadhead, trim back to leaf nodes throughout the outer and transition zones. The plant will look smaller but still presentable.
  • Season 2 (late August): the plant will have produced new growth from the season-1 cut points. Some of this new growth will have extended into areas that were previously transition zone. Cut back again to the leaf nodes on the new growth, working slightly further into the plant's original volume than you could safely reach in season 1.
  • Season 3 and after: the plant should now have a more open structure with living growth throughout, rather than a thin green shell over a dead centre. Maintain from this point with the standard annual post-flowering cut.

Do not attempt this renovation process between 1 March and 31 August without a thorough nest check at each session. Renovation work that opens the canopy is considerably more disruptive to any nesting birds than the annual light trim.

Step 7: Apply a feed and mulch after pruning

After the late-summer trim, give the plant a balanced slow-release fertiliser at the label rate for an established shrub. Hebe is not a heavy feeder, but an annual granular application in the period immediately after pruning gives the new growth that forms over autumn and in the following spring the best start. Broadcast the granules evenly across the soil surface to the drip-line of the plant, avoid placing them directly against the stems, and water in if the soil is dry.

Apply a 50-75mm layer of mulch (garden compost, composted bark or well-rotted manure) over the root zone, keeping it clear of the stem base by 50mm. On the free-draining Margate chalk soils common throughout Broadstairs, St Peter's and the North Foreland area, Hebe establishes well once the chalk is covered, but moisture retention in summer is limited and the mulch layer makes a measurable difference to the consistency of growth from one side of the plant to the other through a dry July and August.

After a hard Thanet winter: the spring assessment

In a severe winter on the Thanet coast, typically when hard frost coincides with a sustained easterly from the North Sea for several days, the softer-leaved hebe varieties show tip dieback. The growing tips and the most recent season's growth on the exposed faces brown and collapse. This is frost damage, not a disease and not a sign that the plant is dead.

The critical rule: do not cut back the dead material until April. Cutting in February or March removes the dead material, but it also exposes the live tissue immediately behind it to any further cold. The dead tips, however unattractive through winter, provide a marginal degree of insulation for the live growth behind them. By April, the live growth will have started to move again, which clarifies exactly where the live-dead boundary is. Cut each damaged stem back cleanly to just above the first pair of leaves on the live growth behind the dead material. In most years, and with most of the hardier varieties planted in CT10 gardens, the recovery is complete and the plant is back to full density by July.

If the plant shows no new growth anywhere on the stem by mid-April, scratch the stem with a thumbnail. Green tissue under the bark means it is alive but late. No green tissue means that section has not survived the winter, and the cut should go back further until you reach a point where the stem is still green under the bark. If a plant shows no live tissue anywhere and produces no new growth by late April, it has not survived and should be removed.

Hebe varieties in Broadstairs and the Thanet coast

The Thanet coast's combination of mild winter temperatures (moderated by the sea), summer dryness, salt wind and the free-draining Margate chalk makes it a suitable climate for a wider range of hebe varieties than inland UK gardens can sustain, with one caveat: the easterly winter wind chill is harder than the temperature alone would suggest, and frost in combination with desiccating wind is more damaging to hebe tips than frost in still air.

Hebe 'Mrs Winder' is the variety I see most reliably surviving in fully exposed clifftop positions in Broadstairs. It has narrow, dark purple-flushed leaves, deep violet-blue flowers from late July into September, and a compact growth habit that stays well within a manageable size without severe annual cutting. It is among the hardiest of the flowering hebes in UK conditions and is the one I recommend most often when a client in a directly exposed position wants a hebe hedge rather than a Griselinia or Escallonia.

Hebe salicifolia is the large-leaved willow-leaved species that produces long white to pale lilac flower racemes in June and July. It is common in the older gardens of St Peter's and in sheltered positions along Viking Bay. It grows larger than the compact cultivars (often reaching 1.8m or more unchecked) and requires more careful post-flowering pruning because its leaves are large enough that trimmer-cut edges are clearly visible. It is not reliably frost-hardy in an exposed CT10 clifftop position but does well in sheltered situations.

Hebe rakaiensis is a compact, white-flowered species growing to about 60-80cm in typical UK garden conditions. Its small leaves take a shear trim well, and it is among the hardiest hebes for exposed conditions. It works as a low boundary definition hedge or as a front-of-border edging plant. Growth rate is slow, which reduces annual pruning commitment but means a new hedge takes three to four seasons to reach a usable density.

Hebe 'Great Orme' produces deep pink, long flower spikes from late summer into autumn. It is less frost-hardy than 'Mrs Winder' or rakaiensis and needs a sheltered position in CT10: the lee side of a taller hedge or wall, a south-facing aspect with good drainage. In a sheltered position it is a striking plant; in a fully exposed clifftop position it will need tip-dieback remediation after most winters.

The whipcord hebes (Hebe armstrongii, Hebe ochracea 'James Stirling') are an entirely different type: scale-leaved, conifer-like in texture, very slow-growing and very hardy. They are genuinely front-line coastal plants but they are too slow-growing and too architectural to function as a hedge and are better used as accent specimens within a mixed border. They require almost no pruning.

Common problems with hebes in CT10

Bare patches developing in the hedge face. Almost always caused by previous cuts having reached into the old-wood zone, or by a hard winter following a cut that was taken too deep. The affected sections will not recover from the bare wood. Cut them out to their junction with a nearby live stem, allow the neighbouring growth to fill the gap, and tie it in if necessary to cover the space over the following season. Prevention is far better than correction: use the old-wood boundary check described in Step 3 before every cut.

Poor flowering despite regular cutting. If the plant is cut in autumn or winter, the bud sites forming on the current season's growth are removed before they can mature into next year's flower spikes. Switch to the late-August post-flowering window described in Steps 2 and 5 and the flowering display will recover within one to two seasons. A secondary cause is dense shade from nearby trees: hebe flowers most freely in full sun, and progressive overhanging from a large tree will reduce both the flower count and the density of the outer growth.

Yellowing leaves on the interior of the plant. Normal and expected in a dense hebe as the interior older leaves are shaded out by the outer canopy. Yellowing that progresses from the tips inward and involves the newest growth is different: it can indicate waterlogging or root damage, both of which are problems on heavy soils, and less common on the free-draining chalk of Broadstairs. If the yellowing is widespread and involves new growth, dig carefully near the root zone and check for standing water or the characteristic white mycelium of honey fungus (Armillaria species) at the base of the stems.
