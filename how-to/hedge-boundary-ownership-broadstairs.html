How to find out who owns a boundary hedge: title register, T marks and the ditch rule for Broadstairs CT10.

Every week I get asked the same question before a job: "Is this actually my hedge?" A privet hedge on a terrace in Broadstairs CA, a large Griselinia on the cliff-side boundary above Stone Bay, an old mixed hawthorn-blackthorn on the back edge of a Victorian semi in St Peter's. Before anyone cuts, renovates or removes a boundary hedge, the ownership question needs a clear answer. Here is the step-by-step process for finding it.

Steps

Step 1: Order the title register and title plan for your property from Land Registry

Go to gov.uk/get-information-about-property and search for your address. You can download the official title register and the filed title plan for £3 each. The title register is the legal document confirming ownership and any entries (charges, covenants, rights). The title plan is the Land Registry map showing the approximate extent of your registered title in red. Download both. You cannot properly read one without the other.

Note: if your property was last sold before 1998, it may not be registered at Land Registry. In that case you will need to obtain the historic conveyance documents from your solicitor's file or via a Land Registry application for voluntary registration. Unregistered title is uncommon in CT10 but not rare in older Broadstairs estate property.

Step 2: Read the title plan boundary lines carefully

The title plan shows your registered title edged in red. The boundary lines on a Land Registry title plan are general boundaries only, not precise legal lines. Land Registry Practice Guide 40 Supplement 3 states plainly that the exact boundary is rarely recorded. A hedge can make the original line impossible to determine from the map alone. What the title plan does show you is the approximate position: whether the boundary runs along the inner edge, the outer edge, or through the middle of the hedge. For a wide mature hedge (a 1m-thick privet or a substantial hawthorn bank), this distinction matters. The red line is a guide, not a judgement.

Step 3: Look for T marks in the filed title documents

A T mark is a small T-shaped symbol drawn on a deed plan, with the base of the T touching the boundary line and the top pointing into the land of the party responsible for that boundary. If the T is on your title plan, you own (and are responsible for maintaining) that boundary feature. If it is on the neighbouring title plan, they do. You find T marks in the filed title documents attached to the register, not always on the basic plan view. Look for:

  • A single T: one owner is responsible for that boundary. The T sits on their land.
  • Two Ts facing each other (forming an H): the boundary is shared. Both owners have maintenance responsibility.
  • No T mark: common in older titles. The deeds may have wording ("the purchaser shall maintain the boundary marked 'T' on the filed plan") even when the graphic mark is not clearly visible. Read the full register, not just the plan.

T marks are the strongest written evidence of ownership, but they are not proof on their own. The register wording must be read alongside them. A solicitor who handled your purchase should have explained any T mark entries. If they did not, look at your original Transfer document (form TR1 or earlier conveyance).

Step 4: Apply the hedge-and-ditch rule if the deeds are silent

Where the title documents say nothing specific about who owns the hedge, English common law provides a rebuttable presumption: the hedge-and-ditch rule. When a man dug a boundary ditch in the past, he dug it at the edge of his own land, piled the excavated soil on his own side, and planted a hedge on top. The result: the ditch is on the far side of the hedge from its owner. If there is a ditch on one side of the hedge and not the other, the hedge belongs to the owner on the side without the ditch, and the legal boundary sits on the far edge of the ditch.

This rule is most useful in rural Kent: it applies well to older field boundaries on the Thanet plateau around Reading Street and to back-lane boundaries on the edge of St Peter's village. It applies less usefully in Victorian terrace rows in Broadstairs CA where hedges were planted on the line rather than at the edge of a dug ditch. If both sides of the hedge are ditchless (as they commonly are in a shared back-garden boundary in CT10 terraces), the rule does not apply and you fall back to the deeds or to shared responsibility.

Step 5: Check historic evidence where the deeds are ambiguous

Three other sources of evidence that regularly resolve ownership questions when the register is silent or unclear:

  • Original estate agent's sale particulars. Particulars from the 1970s-90s sale of a Broadstairs terrace often specify "right-hand boundary as viewed from the road maintained by owner" or show T marks on the floorplan. Ask the previous owner or find a copy in the Land Registry's filed documents for older titles.
  • Neighbours' title registers. Download the title plan for the adjacent property (£3 from Land Registry). If their plan shows a T mark on the shared boundary, it is their hedge. Cross-referencing neighbouring plans often resolves ambiguity quickly.
  • Historic Ordnance Survey maps. The National Library of Scotland's online OS map viewer shows historic 25-inch Ordnance Survey maps of Broadstairs going back to the late nineteenth century. These show hedge and ditch positions that existed before modern registration. A hedge that appears in the same position since 1897 is likely a historic boundary feature with an owner, even if the title plan is uninformative.

Step 6: Write to your neighbour before doing any work on a shared or uncertain boundary hedge

If the steps above produce a clear answer and the hedge is yours, you have the legal right to manage it. But if there is any uncertainty, the right approach before sending anyone in with a hedge trimmer is a brief written letter to your neighbour. State what work you are proposing, when you intend to carry it out, and that you believe the hedge is yours (or shared). Keep a copy. This is not a legal formality required by any Act for a hedge (it is different from the Anti-Social Behaviour Act 2003 high-hedge complaints process). It is good practice. A trimmer arriving without notice on a disputed boundary is the single most reliable way to generate a formal complaint, an injunction application, and a bill for a surveyor's report. A letter costs nothing and takes five minutes.

One thing the letter does not change: whatever the ownership position, you may always cut branches or roots that overhang or encroach on your side back to the legal boundary. That right does not require the owner's consent. The cuttings, technically, belong to the hedge owner and should be offered back to them.

Nest check before any cutting on a boundary hedge, 1 March to 31 August

Once you have established ownership and agreed the work, the nesting-season constraint still applies. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest or to disturb a bird on a nest. The penalty on summary conviction is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine, assessed per nest. A boundary hedge in a Broadstairs terrace garden is a prime nesting site for dunnocks, blackbirds and robins from early March. Before any trimming or renovation between 1 March and 31 August, inspect the hedge from a short distance for 10 to 15 minutes during early morning and look for birds entering and leaving. If in doubt, leave the hedge until September.

Step 7: If ownership is genuinely disputed, use a boundary surveyor before solicitors

If both you and your neighbour believe you own the same hedge and neither title register resolves it clearly, the least expensive first step is not a solicitor: it is a RICS-accredited boundary surveyor. A surveyor's report, typically £500-£1,000, will examine all available documentary evidence, apply the presumptions, visit the site, and produce a reasoned opinion. That report is often enough for both parties to reach a private agreement. If it is not, it becomes the foundation for any formal boundary determination application at the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), which is cheaper and faster than the High Court route taken in most historic boundary-dispute cases.

The myths that do not hold up

Several "common knowledge" beliefs about hedge ownership are not grounded in law, and acting on them leads to disputes. Worth stating plainly:

  • "Each homeowner owns the hedge on their left." There is no such rule in England or Wales. Ownership is determined by the documents and the position of the feature, not by a fixed left-right convention. The left-side rule is sometimes stated in building leases, but only where it is written into the specific lease: it is not a general principle.
  • "If I have maintained the hedge for 20 years, it is mine." Maintaining a boundary feature does not transfer ownership. A neighbour who has cut a shared hedge every year for two decades has no stronger legal claim to it than when they started. Adverse possession of registered land in England requires 10 years of factual possession with no permission and an application to Land Registry under the Land Registration Act 2002, not just maintenance of a boundary feature.
  • "The good side faces my garden, so it is my hedge." The "good side" (the neater, flatter face of a hedge) traditionally faces away from the land of the owner who planted it. By this logic the good side faces your neighbour, meaning the hedge is theirs. But this is a custom, not a rule. The documents override it in every case.
  • "The T mark is always on the title plan." It is not. T marks appear on filed plans, on Transfer documents, and on old-format conveyances. The basic Land Registry title plan view often omits them. Download the filed documents, not just the plan.

Boundary hedges in Broadstairs conservation areas: the extra consideration

If the boundary hedge you are investigating is in one of Broadstairs' four conservation areas (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA or Kingsgate CA) and it includes trees with a trunk diameter of 75mm or more at 1.5m height, any significant pruning or removal requires a section 211 notice to Thanet District Council at least six weeks before the work. This applies to you whether the hedge is yours, shared, or next-door's. The ownership question and the planning-notice question are separate. A hedge that you clearly own in a conservation area still requires the s.211 process if it includes qualifying trees. See the full guide for the s.211 notice process.
