What to do with hedge clippings in Broadstairs: shredding, composting and TDC collection for CT10 gardens.

After the hedge is cut, most of the practical decisions still sit in front of you. A cherry laurel hedge cut once in late summer generates a substantial pile of waxy, slow-to-decompose material. A privet trimmed twice a year produces plenty of soft green clippings that can be composted in minutes. A hawthorn boundary cut back hard produces thick woody brash that no domestic bin will take. The handling question is different for each species and each season, and getting it wrong costs time: a pile of cherry laurel left on the lawn in a wet October does not break down, it just rots. Six steps cover the full decision process for CT10 conditions.

Steps

Step 1: Sort the cuttings into three categories before moving anything

Mixing all hedge material together at the start makes every subsequent step harder. Before you start filling bags or loading a barrow, separate the pile into three categories on site: soft, leafy green growth; woody stems and brash; and any material you suspect is diseased, pest-affected or invasive. Keep each category separate throughout.

Soft green growth includes the leafy tips and thin new extension shoots produced by privet, hawthorn, hornbeam, beech and similar species when trimmed during the growing season. This material wilts quickly and is the easiest to process. It is the closest thing in a hedge to conventional compost-heap green material.

Woody stems and brash covers anything thick enough that snapping it takes real force. Cherry laurel, leylandii, yew, box and the main structural branches of any hedge species produce material that takes at least two years to break down in a standard compost heap. This needs either a shredder or a different route entirely.

Suspect material is anything showing signs of honey fungus (white mycelium sheets under bark, bootlace-like rhizomorphs in the soil), box blight (brown patches, bare stems, white fungal growth), fireblight (wilted, scorched appearance on hawthorn or pyracantha), or box tree caterpillar webbing on cut box sections. Set this aside and do not mix it with the healthy piles until you have decided how to handle it: Step 2.

Nesting season: check before any hedge-base clearance

This guide covers what to do with clippings after cutting is done. If you are still in the process of trimming the hedge that generated them, section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 applies: it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird nest or to disturb a Schedule 1 species. The practical caution window for hedge-cutting work is 1 March to 31 August. Outside those dates, including the autumn and winter months when most heavy cutting happens, normal work carries low risk. Clearing debris from the base of an established hedge during the spring and summer months warrants a slow walk to check for ground-level nests before you start.

Step 2: Deal with diseased or pest-affected material immediately and separately

Do not compost, shred or build into a dead hedge any material you have categorised as suspect. The reasoning is practical: shredders do not kill Phytophthora root rot spores, honey fungus rhizomorphs or box blight spores, and spreading infected mulch around other plants in the garden is a direct route to spreading the problem. Burning is rarely practical in a CT10 residential garden, and is not always legal given smoke-nuisance legislation. The correct route for most diseased material is the Thanet District Council household waste recycling centres, where it goes into the general or bulky waste stream, not the garden waste skips.

Box tree caterpillar (Cydalima perspectalis) is established across Thanet and is widespread in CT10 box hedges. Box clippings may contain pupae in silk webbing or small brown caterpillars that have retreated into the cut material. Do not compost box clippings in a standard home compost heap: the pupae survive and hatch from warm compost. Bag the clippings in sealed black sacks for general waste, or take them to the recycling centre in a tied bag.

Cherry laurel clippings from a healthy plant are safe to shred and compost, despite the plant's toxicity. The RHS confirms there is no evidence that potentially harmful substances found in cherry laurel are transmitted when the wood is composted or used as a surface mulch. The same applies to yew: safe to shred and use as mulch from a healthy plant, despite the plant's toxicity. The distinction is between healthy and diseased material, not between toxic-plant and non-toxic-plant species.

Step 3: Process healthy soft green growth

Soft leafy clippings from privet, hornbeam, beech, hawthorn and similar species can go into a home compost bin mixed with carbon-rich material such as cardboard, newspaper or dry autumn leaves. Hedge clippings on their own are nitrogen-rich and will form a slimy, airless mass if not balanced with dry material. The standard rule is one part clippings to two parts dry material by volume, turning the heap after a week to introduce air.

For small amounts of soft growth, particularly from a light second trim, the RHS recommends chopping the material with a spade and dropping it back under the hedge as a thin surface layer. On thin Margate Chalk, where the topsoil above the rock is often 300mm deep or less, even a modest annual addition of decomposing organic material at the hedge base is useful over time. This only works well with genuinely soft material: leafy privet clippings decompose in weeks when chopped small, whereas cherry laurel or leylandii leaves sit intact on the soil surface for a year or more.

One constraint: chopped privet clippings have a strong smell in wet weather. Keep them away from boundaries with neighbours and from seating areas, or add them to a closed bin rather than dropping them in place.

Step 4: Process healthy woody material with a shredder or a slow woodchip pile

Woody clippings that are too thick to compost quickly are best processed through a garden shredder. Shredded wood used as a surface mulch around the hedge or on borders has three practical benefits: it suppresses annual weeds, retains soil moisture during dry spells (Manston averages around 615mm of rain per year, making East Kent one of England's drier areas, and mulched borders dry out noticeably more slowly than bare chalk topsoil), and it breaks down slowly into organic matter that improves the structure of the thin chalk soil over time.

A common concern is that surface wood chip robs the soil of nitrogen as it decomposes. The RHS is clear that shredded wood used as a surface mulch does not deplete the soil of nitrogen: the nitrogen draw-down from decomposing woody material only occurs when wood is dug into the soil. Surface application creates no competition for nutrients with established hedge roots.

Apply shredded material at 75-100mm depth along the hedge base, keeping a 50mm gap from the stems to avoid crown rot. Do not dig shreddings into the chalk: the nitrogen draw-down effect is real in the soil and can slow new growth in an established hedge.

If you do not own or have access to a shredder and the volume is large, a slow woodchip pile is a useful alternative. Stack the woody material in a separate bay or corner, not mixed with your main compost heap: woody material that is slow to break down will crowd out the faster-composting green material if the two are mixed. Turn the woodchip pile once in spring and once in autumn: after two to three years it produces a usable coarse compost for mulching. This is a better approach for the larger brash generated by a formal cut-back of a cherry laurel or leylandii hedge: the material is genuinely useful eventually, but it needs its own space and time.

Step 5: Use TDC collection or the recycling centre for material you cannot process at home

Thanet District Council's paid garden waste service collects fortnightly throughout the year. The 2026/27 annual subscription is £73 per year, with an additional charge of £44 for the bin itself for new subscribers. The brown-lidded garden waste bin accepts hedge clippings, grass cuttings, leaves, prunings and other plant material. To check your collection day and subscribe, use the address look-up on the TDC garden waste pages at thanet.gov.uk. The garden waste subscription runs separately from the free food waste and recycling collections introduced from April 2026.

For material that will not fit in the brown bin, or for large one-off volumes from a renovation cut or hedge removal, Thanet's household waste recycling centres accept garden waste in the skip provided. The centres operate standard council hours; check thanet.gov.uk for current opening times before loading a trailer. Garden waste at the recycling centre is taken at no charge for Thanet residents.

Where a contractor has done the cutting, the question of who takes the cuttings away is worth clarifying in advance. I quote with or without removal. Removal adds to the job cost but simplifies your afternoon: the cuttings go in the van and the garden is clear when I leave. If you want to keep the shreddings or have a compost setup, I can leave the sorted piles for you to process instead.

Step 6: Return useful material to the garden

After sorting, the practical options resolve into three useful outputs. Composted soft material, which you apply around the hedge base or into borders in spring, as described in the feeding and mulching guide. Shredded woody material applied as surface mulch, which is particularly valuable on thin chalk topsoil and for moisture retention through CT10 dry spells. And larger, longer brash from hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn and similar species, which can be stacked between posts as a dead hedge: a low boundary or wildlife-habitat structure that is built from the hedge's own prunings and returns them to the garden in a useful form.

The dead hedge route is most appropriate for the autumn material from native species. A cherry laurel or leylandii dead hedge has less wildlife value than a hawthorn or hazel one, and the waxy leaves make the inner structure slow to settle. If you have a mix of native and evergreen brash, use the native material for the dead hedge frame and the evergreen material for the shredder or slow woodchip pile.

Species-specific notes for CT10 gardens

These are the commonest species across Broadstairs, St Peter's and the Victorian terrace gardens around Viking Bay, and the specific handling points that differ from the general guidance above.

  • Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus): the highest-volume clipping species in CT10. Produces thick waxy leaves that do not break down in a standard compost bin for two years or more. Route: shredder, or slow woodchip pile. Safe to shred despite plant toxicity. Check for signs of cherry laurel die-back (Phytophthora cinnamomi is recorded on Prunus in UK RHS surveys): if whole sections are dying back from the root, do not shred; take to the recycling centre.
  • Leylandii and Thuja: produces large volumes of fine feathery growth with an aromatic sap. The material composted without shredding knits into an impermeable mat. Shred first, then add to compost or use as mulch. Keep shredded conifer material away from ericaceous or acid-loving plants (bluebells, camellias) as conifers are moderately alkaline in decomposition and CT10 chalk is already alkaline. The slight additional alkalinity from conifer shreddings on chalk is negligible in practice, but not worth concentrating around species that prefer acidic conditions.
  • Yew: safe to shred and use as surface mulch from a healthy plant. Yew shreddings are among the slowest-decomposing mulch materials: they last well but add little to soil organic matter relative to deciduous shreddings. Keep yew clippings away from pets and livestock. The toxicity of yew is in the foliage and seed, not in the decomposed material, but there is no reason to scatter fresh yew clippings where animals can access them before the material has decomposed.
  • Box: treat box clippings as potentially pest-affected regardless of visible box tree caterpillar activity, particularly between April and October. CT10 has high box tree caterpillar pressure: the moth arrived in Kent earlier than most English counties and overwinters here reliably in the warmer coastal microclimate. Seal in black sacks for general waste or take to the recycling centre; do not compost.
  • Privet: easy to compost. Soft summer clips go directly into a compost bin. The smell from wet privet clippings fades within a week when mixed into a well-aerated heap. Autumn privet from a late tidy does not need shredding: the growth is soft enough to break down quickly.
  • Hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hornbeam: mixed material from these native species is the best feed for a home compost heap. A mix of hawthorn leaves and blackthorn stems produces compost in under a year in a covered heap. Thorned material can be awkward to handle: wear leather gloves and use long-handled tools when moving hawthorn or blackthorn brash to avoid blackthorn pricks, which are prone to infection if they penetrate the skin.

A note on burning

Burning hedge clippings in a CT10 garden is legal but increasingly problematic in practice. Broadstairs and St Peter's are dense residential areas: smoke from a garden fire travels quickly and the 615mm annual rainfall means there are few reliably dry weeks in autumn when burning is practical without producing black smoke from damp material. There is no specific law against domestic bonfires in England, but the Environmental Protection Act 1990 allows a council to serve an abatement notice if the smoke is a statutory nuisance, and Thanet DC does respond to smoke-nuisance complaints. In practice, shredding and composting is a better route than burning for all but the most remote CT10 properties. I do not burn on site: all arisings from a job come away in the van or are left sorted for the client to process through one of the routes above.
