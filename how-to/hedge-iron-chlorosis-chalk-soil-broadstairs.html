How to diagnose and treat iron chlorosis in a hedge on Margate Chalk: a step-by-step guide for Broadstairs CT10.

Yellow leaves with green veins appearing on a hedge that was healthy last season are almost always caused by the same thing in Broadstairs and CT10: the alkaline pH of Margate Chalk is locking up the iron and manganese that plants need to produce chlorophyll. This guide sets out the seven steps I use to confirm chlorosis, correct it quickly and prevent it coming back, from checking the yellowing pattern through to applying chelated iron and renewing the mulch.

Steps

Step 1: Check the yellowing pattern before buying any product

Walk the hedge at close range in good light and look at individual leaves carefully. Iron chlorosis produces a specific pattern: the leaf tissue between the veins goes yellow while the veins themselves stay green. This interveinal yellowing is the diagnostic sign, and it appears on the newest growth first (younger leaves are most affected because they have the highest iron demand). Uniform yellowing, where the whole leaf fades evenly, usually points to nitrogen deficiency or drought stress. Yellow leaf edges or tip scorch are more likely to be salt-wind damage or waterlogging damage. If you are seeing a mix of patterns, treat each cause separately: they can coexist, but applying chelated iron to a waterlogged or drought-stressed hedge will not resolve those underlying problems.

Step 2: Rule out drainage problems and root compaction

Before spending anything on iron, probe the soil at the base of the hedge with a trowel or metal rod. On CT10 chalk, the typical drainage problem is not waterlogging in the classic sense but root compaction at the junction between imported topsoil and the chalk rubble below. Roots that cannot penetrate the chalk show the same nutrient uptake failure as roots sitting in saturated ground. Push the trowel down 25cm at two or three points along the hedge base. If the soil pulls away as a wet, grey-blue smelling plug, the root zone is at least intermittently saturated. If you meet solid chalk at 10 to 15cm, the roots have nowhere to go in a dry spell. Both findings need addressing before iron treatment will hold.

Step 3: Confirm soil alkalinity with a vinegar test

Dig out a lump of subsoil from 15 to 20cm depth, break it open to expose a fresh face and drop a small piece into a jar of plain white vinegar. Free chalk fizzes immediately on contact with acid: strong fizzing confirms significant free calcium carbonate and a pH that is almost certainly above 7.5. Most CT10 gardens sitting on the Margate Chalk formation will show pH 7.8 to 8.2, and in a garden where chalk rubble was used as hardcore under paths or driveways near the hedge root zone, local pH can be even higher. At that pH, iron and manganese are present in the soil but are bound to calcium carbonate in forms roots cannot absorb. A pH meter confirms the number if you want a figure to record. The vinegar test takes thirty seconds and costs nothing, and it establishes the cause before you spend money on a treatment.

Step 4: Apply a liquid chelated iron drench to the root zone

Chelated (sequestered) iron is iron bonded to an organic carrier molecule that keeps it available to roots even at high pH, bypassing the calcium carbonate lockup that defeats ordinary soluble iron salts. Products sold as sequestered iron tonic, chelated iron or by the trade name Sequestrene are mixed with water at the packet rate and watered into the root zone. Apply along the drip line of the hedge canopy, which is where the greatest concentration of feeder roots sits, not against the base of the stems. Use approximately 2 litres per metre of hedge at the application rate on the product label. Water the area first if the soil is dry: chelated iron moves into the root zone with the soil water, and applying it to bone-dry chalk in a drought will slow uptake.

Step 5: Follow up with a granular chelated iron and manganese soil application

The liquid drench gives a faster visible response, with new growth emerging noticeably greener within three to four weeks. It is short-lived on CT10 chalk, however, because the alkaline chemistry degrades organic chelates over weeks rather than months. A granular chelated iron and manganese product, worked lightly into the top 5cm of soil along the root zone, releases more slowly and extends the correction across the growing season. Timing matters: apply the granular treatment in early spring, when the growth flush is greatest and iron demand is at its highest for the year. Work it in with a hand fork, taking care not to damage surface roots on established hedges, and water it in after application.

Step 6: Apply a 75mm organic mulch along the hedge base

A 75mm layer of well-rotted bark chippings or homemade compost along the full length of the hedge base does two things in a chalk garden. First, it suppresses the topsoil surface from drying out and forming the cracks that allow fine alkaline chalk dust to migrate up and reset the pH of the root zone. Second, as organic matter breaks down it releases weak organic acids that buffer the pH slightly in the top 10 to 15cm, exactly where most feeder roots are. Keep the mulch 50mm back from the base of stems to prevent collar rot. This is not a permanent fix: the chalk below will reassert its pH over time, and in a dry Thanet summer the buffering effect disappears faster. Renew the mulch each spring as part of the annual hedge aftercare routine.

Step 7: Reassess after six weeks and decide on the medium-term approach

Look at the new growth on treated plants six weeks after the first chelated iron application. Fresh shoots should be noticeably greener than the yellowed growth that was there before treatment. Older yellowed leaves will not turn green again, but new growth emerging after the treatment should show a healthy colour. If the same plants show chlorosis every growing season despite correct spring treatment and generous mulching, that pattern tells you the species is fighting the soil chemistry at this site. The practical answer at that point is a species substitution: replace the affected plants with alkaline-tolerant species on the next bare-root season, rather than treating the same plants with chelated iron every year indefinitely.

Why CT10's Margate Chalk creates this problem specifically

The Margate Chalk formation that underlies most of Broadstairs, St Peter's, Kingsgate and North Foreland is Upper Cretaceous chalk: very fine-grained, free-draining and naturally alkaline. In a wet winter, rainwater draining through it dissolves calcium carbonate and gradually raises the pH of any overlying topsoil. An imported topsoil that started at a reasonable pH 6.5 when a garden was laid out can sit at pH 7.8 or above within a decade in a chalk-underlain CT10 garden, particularly if chalk rubble was used as hardcore under nearby paths or the soil was not deeply prepared before planting. The RHS notes that at high pH, iron and manganese form insoluble compounds with calcium that roots simply cannot absorb, even though the nutrients are physically present in the soil. The only practical approaches are chelated iron to bypass the lockup, organic matter to buffer the surface pH, and an honest assessment of species choice before the next planting cycle.

Species notes: which hedging plants cope well on Margate Chalk

On Margate Chalk, the hedging species that rarely show iron chlorosis are the genuinely alkaline-tolerant plants: beech, hornbeam, field maple, yew, privet, hawthorn, blackthorn, box, Viburnum tinus, Escallonia, elder and most native hedgerow species. These will grow well on CT10 chalk without chelated iron treatment, provided they have sufficient organic matter at the root zone. At the other end of the scale, rhododendron and azalea have no place in a chalk garden at all and will die without acidifying compost. Cherry laurel is the hedging species I see showing interveinal chlorosis most often in Broadstairs: it will survive on chalk with annual chelated iron treatment, but it is not a natural chalk-garden plant. Photinia (Photinia x fraseri 'Red Robin') can show mild yellowing during hot dry spells on very thin chalk at the clifftop. If the same species shows chlorosis every year on the same site, consider whether the annual treatment cost and effort is justified or whether a genuinely alkaline-tolerant species would serve the boundary better.
