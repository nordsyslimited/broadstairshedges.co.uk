How to manage a mixed native hedge on a rotation: the hedgerow management cycle for CT10 wildlife.

A mixed native hedge of hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, field maple and dog rose manages very differently from a formal clipped privet or laurel. Cut it every year in the same way and it gradually produces fewer berries, less nesting cover, and becomes a thinner, gappier structure through its middle. The hedgerow management cycle, developed by the People's Trust for Endangered Species (PTES) and HedgeLink, is a practical rotation approach: divide the hedge into sections, cut each on a two or three year cycle, and leave at least one section to grow on each year. Applied to the native hedges in Broadstairs gardens and on the rural fringe around St Peter's and Reading Street, it is the technique that keeps a hedge dense, productive and genuinely valuable to the wildlife that depends on it.

Steps

Step 1: Decide whether your hedge is a rotation candidate

Rotation management is designed for mixed native hedges: hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), hazel (Corylus avellana), field maple (Acer campestre), dog rose (Rosa canina), elder (Sambucus nigra) and guelder rose (Viburnum opulus). Any hedge made up of two or more of these species is a rotation candidate and will respond well to being cut on a longer cycle with sections left uncut each year.

Formal ornamental hedges, privet, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, leylandii, yew and box, work on a different management model. They need regular trimming (typically once or twice per year) to maintain a clean shape and do not benefit from the same rotation approach. If you have a privet along the front of a Broadstairs terrace, this guide does not apply to it. If you have a hawthorn or mixed native hedge along the back of the garden or against a garden wall, it does.

In CT10, the most common rotation candidates are the old hawthorn-dominant boundary hedges in the rural fringe west of Broadstairs (around Reading Street and the Margate Road corridor), the blackthorn and hawthorn hedges along the back gardens of older properties in St Peter's, and new wildlife hedge plantings put in over the last five years or so as garden biodiversity interest has grown.

Step 2: Divide the hedge into sections

Walk the hedge and divide it into equal sections: three sections for a hedge of 15 metres or more, two sections for a shorter run. Mark the dividing points with a bamboo cane pushed into the base of the hedge at each boundary, or simply note them in a phone as paces from one end. Label the sections A, B and C (or A and B for a two-section plan).

For a typical 30-metre hawthorn boundary hedge, three 10-metre sections gives you one section cut per year on a triennial (three-year) cycle. For a 20-metre hedge split into two, each section gets cut every two years on a biennial cycle. The exact lengths do not need to be precise to the metre: the goal is roughly equal sections, so the hedge always has at least one-third (or one-half, on a biennial plan) left to grow on each year.

Write the section labels and cut dates somewhere you will find them: a note on your phone, a tag hung on the gate post, or a simple spreadsheet. The rotation only works if you remember which section you cut last year and which one to leave alone this year.

Step 3: Choose your cutting interval

Three options:

  • Annual (all sections, every year). Lowest wildlife value. This is the default that most hedge owners fall into through habit, and the PTES hedgerow management cycle is specifically designed to move people away from it. If you currently cut every section every year, switching to biennial or triennial is the single most effective change you can make for the hedge's wildlife value.
  • Biennial (each section every 2 years). A practical middle ground for most CT10 gardens. Section A is cut in year 1, section B in year 2, back to section A in year 3. The hedge always has half its length in the growing phase at any point. The RSPB recommends a two-year minimum rotation for mixed native farm hedges, and it is an achievable target for a garden setting.
  • Triennial (each section every 3 years). Best wildlife value. Research from the Natural Environment Research Council (NERC) found significantly more berries available to overwintering birds from hedgerow plots cut on a three-year cycle compared with annual-cut plots, across hawthorn, blackthorn, dog rose and bramble. Fieldfare, redwing and song thrush (all three passing through the Broadstairs coast on autumn and winter migration) benefit directly from the berry surplus. If your hedge is on a garden large enough to allow it to reach 2 metres or more in height without causing problems with light or boundaries, triennial is the right choice.

Step 4: Time your first cut

The working window for cutting any native hedge is the winter period: September or October through to the end of February. November is an excellent month to start in CT10. The hedge is coming out of active growth and beginning to slow for winter, the coastal drying winds of November allow any cut surfaces to begin drying and callusing before hard frosts arrive in December and January, and you are well clear of the nesting-season restriction.

Do not begin any cutting, even on the rotation section, while there is any chance of active nests in the hedge. Early October is usually safe in CT10, but if you are uncertain, do a systematic nest check before starting: part the hedge carefully at regular intervals to look for cup nests. A nest with eggs or chicks means work stops entirely until the nest is vacated. See the nest check guide for the method.

When does nesting law apply to rotation cutting?

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest, or disturb a Schedule 1 species at or near its nest. The Act sets no calendar dates, but the accepted guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. Rotation management keeps you comfortably within the winter window if you plan your first cut for October or November. The key risk is a late start: if the rotation cut slips to March, it falls inside the nesting window and requires a careful nest check before any work begins. On summary conviction, the penalty is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine per nest.

Step 5: Cut the rotation section and leave the rest alone

Cut only the section scheduled for this year. Leave the other sections completely uncut, even if they look untidy. The untidy sections are the productive ones: last year's growth produces this year's berries, and the thicker growth in the uncut sections is where birds nest, roost and shelter through the winter.

When you cut the scheduled section, work to these principles:

  • Height. Resist the urge to cut hard back to the previous year's stub line. On a biennial or triennial rotation, the hedge should be allowed to gain height: aim for 1.8 to 2.4 metres (6 to 8 feet) over time, not the tight 1.2-metre clip that many gardeners impose. Taller hedges in CT10 provide better shelter from the coastal wind, generate more berry volume, and give nesting cover to a wider range of species including chaffinch, bullfinch and long-tailed tit, all of which breed in Broadstairs gardens and hedgerows.
  • Sides. Trim the sides of the rotation section so they taper slightly inward at the top (a slight batter, narrow at the top and wider at the base). This keeps the base of the hedge in light so it stays thick. A hedge with vertical or flared sides eventually loses its base growth, which creates the gaps through which wind, rabbits and foxes push through.
  • Incremental cutting. Do not cut below the previous season's growth: take off this year's new shoots, leaving the woody framework of last year's growth. Cutting back to old wood on every rotation cycle is slower and harder on the hedge than taking the new growth off the top and sides year by year.
  • Arisings. Collect the cut material from the base of the hedge. Clippings left on the root zone over winter can harbour fungal disease and suppress the grass and low vegetation at the hedge base, which provides further foraging habitat for wrens and dunnocks.

Step 6: Allow hedgerow trees to develop

As you divide and manage the rotation sections, look for stems growing as individual trees within the hedge: single leaders with a clear main stem, 3 centimetres or more in diameter at the point where they emerge from the hedgerow base. These are hedgerow trees, and the rotation management plan should work around them rather than cutting them in.

In practice this means leaving them out of the rotation entirely and allowing them to grow up through and above the hedge canopy. Old hedgerow hawthorns, elder trunks, and field maple standards growing from hedgerow bases in St Peter's and Reading Street gardens provide nesting habitat for jackdaws, starlings and stock doves, as well as invertebrate habitat in dead wood. They are worth far more left standing than cut back to the hedge line.

One note for conservation-area gardens: a tree growing from a hedge in Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA or Kingsgate CA is covered by section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 once its stem reaches 75 millimetres in diameter at 1.5 metres above ground. At that point, six weeks' notice to Thanet District Council is required before any work. This applies to removing or heavily pruning the tree, not to the routine management of the surrounding hedge. The conservation areas guide covers the s.211 threshold and notification process in detail.

Step 7: Review the rotation annually and log what you observe

At the end of each winter season, write a one-line note against each section: what you cut, the approximate date, the height the section is now at, and what you observed using the hedge. The log does not need to be complicated: a phone note or a line in a diary is enough. It gives you the information you need the following year to decide whether to continue the same rotation or adjust it.

Three things to look for when reviewing:

  • Berry load. If the uncut sections in October and November are carrying good berry crops being taken by fieldfares and redwings (which arrive in Broadstairs from late October most years), delay the cut on those sections until late January or February, by which point the berries will have been largely consumed. This is the rotation working as it should.
  • Base density. If any section is becoming thin or gappy at the base after a few years, it may need more frequent cutting on that section to stimulate basal regrowth, or a heavier cut to encourage new shoots from lower down. Hawthorn and blackthorn respond well to harder cuts by shooting vigorously from the base. Field maple and hazel are slower to recover from severe cutting but reliable.
  • Nesting evidence. Old nests visible in October and November (after leaf fall) show which sections birds prefer. Sections with the highest nest density are the ones to keep in the uncut phase as long as the structure allows.

What the rotation does to berry production

The connection between cutting frequency and berry output is well established in UK hedgerow research. Hawthorn berries (haws) are produced on wood that grew in the previous season. If you cut the same section every year, you remove the wood that would have produced berries, so the hedge flowers but carries very few haws. The same applies to blackthorn sloes, dog rose hips, elder berries and guelder rose berries. Switching to a biennial or triennial cycle gives each section at least one full growing season to mature its fruit-bearing wood before the next cut, which produces a substantially larger berry crop.

For Broadstairs specifically, the berry surplus from a well-managed native hedge can be significant for migrating and overwintering birds using the Thanet coast as a feeding corridor. Fieldfares and redwings arrive from Scandinavia each autumn and move along hedgerow networks looking for berry-rich stands. A well-stocked hawthorn hedge in St Peter's or Reading Street in November is part of that corridor network. Cutting it every year removes most of the reason they would stop.

Species-specific notes for CT10 native hedges

Hawthorn. The most resilient of the CT10 native hedge species. Regrowth after cutting is largely unaffected by the timing and intensity of the cut. This makes hawthorn-dominant hedges forgiving to manage on a rotation: if you miss a year or cut harder than intended, the hawthorn recovers reliably.

Blackthorn. Responds well to rotation management but is more sensitive to very hard cutting than hawthorn. On a triennial cycle, the annual growth increment is higher and the berry crop (sloes) in the uncut years can be substantial. Blackthorn also spreads by root suckers, so cutting around the base in winter keeps the suckering in check without affecting the berry-bearing wood above.

Hazel. On a rotation cycle, hazel benefits from occasional hard coppice cuts (taking the whole section back to low stumps) every 10 to 15 years, rather than continuous incremental trimming. Between coppice cuts, the rotation plan works normally. Coppiced hazel regrows fast: within two or three seasons it reaches a height where it starts producing catkins and nuts again. The timing for a coppice cut is the same as for ordinary rotation cutting: winter, before 1 March.

Field maple. Slower to respond to hard cutting than hawthorn or blackthorn. On a biennial rotation, field maple typically gains 30 to 50 centimetres of new growth per season from an established root system. Its autumn colour (yellow to orange on chalk soils over Margate Chalk in October and November) makes it worth leaving in the uncut phase for as long as the structure allows, since it makes the hedge visually striking in autumn and that growth carries berries and invertebrate habitat into the following year.

Dog rose. Produces hips on the previous year's arching shoots. On an annual-cut regime these arching shoots are removed before they fruit; on a biennial or triennial cycle they ripen fully and feed thrushes and blackbirds through November and December. The main management note is to keep dog rose from dominating the rotation section: if rose is overrunning hawthorn or blackthorn, the rotation cut can prioritise cutting rose shoots hard while leaving the hawthorn framework longer.

CT10 coastal conditions and the rotation plan

The Broadstairs coastal environment imposes a few practical adjustments on the rotation plan.

Wind exposure. On clifftop or exposed-garden sites above Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate or North Foreland, strong salt-laden winds from the north and east can scorch unprotected new growth in late October and November. This does not change the rotation plan, but it does make October the better month to carry out winter cuts in CT10 rather than February: a cut made in October allows the cut surfaces to begin the callusing process before the worst of the winter winds arrive. February cuts on exposed sites can leave soft new buds starting to swell when late frosts and northerly gales hit in March.

Margate Chalk drainage. The thin, free-draining chalk that underlies most of CT10 (the Margate Chalk formation) means hedgerow root zones dry out quickly in summer. If the rotation plan involves a heavy cut or significant reduction in one section, water that section in prolonged dry spells in the following April and May to support the regrowth. A bark or straw mulch 8 to 10 centimetres deep along the base of any section that has been cut hard reduces moisture loss and suppresses weeds competing with the regrowth.

Reading Street and rural fringe sites. The hedges in and around Reading Street CA are some of the most species-rich in CT10, with old hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple stands mixed with hedgerow trees (some of which are covered by the conservation-area notification requirement). The rotation plan is especially valuable here because these hedges are often managed as part of a larger rural-fringe boundary network. Keeping sections at different stages of the rotation creates a continuous wildlife corridor along the boundary rather than a hedge that is uniformly cut or uniformly neglected.
