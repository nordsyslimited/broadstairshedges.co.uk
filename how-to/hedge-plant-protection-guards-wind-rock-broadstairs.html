How to protect newly planted hedge plants from rabbits and wind rock in Broadstairs CT10.

Two things finish off newly planted hedge plants in CT10 before they ever get a chance to establish: rabbits working the base of the stems overnight, and wind rock loosening the root ball on every coastal gust until the roots lose contact with the chalk below. Neither shows up dramatically. The damage is cumulative, quiet, and by the time it is visible in the foliage it has usually been happening for weeks. This guide covers the protection steps that happen at planting, not as an afterthought three months later.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the boundary and assess the rabbit pressure before the plants arrive

Do this assessment at least a week before planting day, when you still have time to order guards if you need them. Walk slowly along the full length of the proposed hedge line and look for: rabbit droppings in the soil (small, dark, round, usually in clusters near the boundary edge), rabbit runs where the grass is worn to a clear track crossing the fence line, gnaw damage on any existing woody plants or shrubs at the boundary, and active burrows within 20 metres of the planting site.

On the Thanet chalk, rabbit populations are highest where gardens border the agricultural land that runs inland from Broadstairs towards Reading Street and the Kingsgate hinterland, and along the unfenced margins above Joss Bay and Stone Bay where open downland meets the coastal edge. Gardens in central Broadstairs and St Peter's with solid wall or close-board fence boundaries typically have lower rabbit pressure, though rabbits are mobile enough that a gap under any fence is sufficient access. Score the risk honestly: light pressure means spiral guards as a precaution; medium to high pressure means mesh cages or tree tubes with canes as the minimum response.

Step 2: Choose the right guard type for the threat level

There are three practical options for newly planted hedging in CT10.

Spiral plastic guards (60cm height): The standard choice for rabbit-only pressure on sites with no deer. A clear perforated spiral wraps loosely around the stem and held upright by a bamboo cane. It provides physical protection against gnawing at the stem base and retains some warmth and humidity around the stem in its first winter. It grows with the plant as the stem thickens. For a hedge planted at 33-50cm spacing, one spiral per plant; for bare-root whips under 60cm tall, the guard encloses most of the above-ground stem, which suits the plant well through the establishment period. Cost is roughly 30-50p per guard in bulk packs from hedging suppliers.

Mesh wire cages (900mm to 1.2m height): Necessary where muntjac deer are present, or where rabbit pressure is heavy and spirals have repeatedly been pushed aside in the past. A cylinder of 13mm galvanised wire mesh, 450-600mm in diameter, placed around the plant and held by two canes. Heavier to install, more expensive, and less elegant, but significantly more robust. The lower coils should be pushed 50-75mm into the soil surface to prevent rabbits pushing under the edge.

Tubular tree shelters (60cm or 90cm height): Solid or slotted polypropylene tubes that enclose the whole stem. Originally developed for forestry, they are used in hedging where the dual benefit of physical protection and a warm, humid microclimate for the young stem is worth the extra cost. On exposed coastal CT10 sites the microclimate benefit is real: a tube shelter reduces the rate at which a young stem loses moisture to a salt-laden sea wind. Fit over a cane, one per plant. Remove before the stem diameter reaches the internal tube width, which on fast-growing privet can be as soon as two to three growing seasons.

Deer are not a significant issue in built-up Broadstairs or central St Peter's, but they are encountered on the Reading Street fringe and on the farmland edges of CT10. If you know deer are present from neighbouring land, go straight to wire mesh cages regardless of cost: a muntjac deer at 45cm shoulder height can reach the growing tips of young hedging plants at 1m, and spirals offer no resistance to deer browsing at all.

Step 3: Fit guards at planting time, not as an afterthought

This is the most common error I see on sites I am asked to look at after something has gone wrong. Guards fitted three weeks after planting are not the same as guards fitted on planting day. In a garden with active rabbit pressure, a bare-root whip left unguarded overnight can have its bark ring-barked or its leader browsed off before the owner has a chance to notice. The time to guard is when the plant goes into the ground.

For spiral guards: plant the hedge first to the correct depth and backfill, then firm in each plant with your heel. Immediately push a bamboo cane (at least 600mm long) firmly into the soil 75mm from the stem, on the side away from the prevailing wind. Wrap the spiral around the stem from the base, coiling it upward, and push the base 25-30mm into the surface mulch or soil. The cane and the spiral do not need to be fixed to each other; the weight of the spiral and its contact with both the cane and the soil keeps it upright. Lay any remaining guards flat in the planting trench until the plant in front of you is done; picking through a pile of tangled spirals on a cold November afternoon is a reliable way to rush the job and miss plants.

For mesh cages: form the cylinder from a cut length of roll mesh and close the overlap with two or three cable ties or a piece of wire. Push the base coil into the surface 50-75mm all the way around before driving the support canes. The cage should not rock when pushed; if the soil is very hard chalk, pre-punch the entry points with a crowbar before trying to push the mesh in by hand.

Step 4: Stake each plant against wind rock on exposed CT10 boundaries

Wind rock is the mechanism by which a newly planted hedge plant is effectively uprooted without the soil visibly moving. Every gust flexes the above-ground stem, which levers the root ball in a small cone of loose soil around the planting hole. After several weeks of gales, the roots have broken their contact with the surrounding chalk, the plant is sitting in a self-made air pocket, and no amount of watering will compensate. On a sheltered inland garden in East Kent this is a minor risk; on a CT10 site anywhere near the clifftop bays or on a north or east-facing boundary in Broadstairs, it is the single most common cause of newly planted hedge failure that I am asked to investigate.

The staking method for hedging is different from tree staking. Do not drive a single stake alongside the plant and tie the stem tightly to it: this fixes the stem but the root ball still pivots. Instead, drive two canes or light stakes at 45 degrees into the soil either side of the planting position, crossing them 150mm above soil level, and tie the stem to the crossing point with a figure-of-eight tie using soft garden twine. This holds the root ball rather than the stem, bracing the underground pivot point against movement in the two dominant wind directions.

The cane already placed for a spiral guard (Step 3) can serve as one of the two stakes. Add a second cane on the opposite side, push it to the same depth, and cross-tie as described. For pot-grown plants with a larger root ball, a single vertical stake driven outside the root ball edge and a soft tie at stem level is sufficient, since the heavier pot-grown root ball rocks less than a bare-root plant. Remove the ties and stakes in the second autumn once the roots have established firm contact with the surrounding chalk, which you can confirm by gently pushing the stem from one side: a plant that does not rock is ready to have its stakes removed.

Step 5: Check guards and stakes through the first growing season

Guards and stakes need inspection every four to six weeks from the end of the first winter through to the end of the first growing season. Three things go wrong regularly and all of them are fixable if caught early.

Spiral guards fill with weeds. Annual weeds germinate inside the humid, sheltered space of a spiral guard and can grow to 30cm inside the tube before they are noticed. They compete directly with the young plant for water and nitrogen in the root zone and, if left until summer, become a woody tangle that is hard to remove without disturbing the plant. Pull weeds inside each guard by hand at every inspection; do not use a hoe around guarded plants, as the hoe will catch the base of the spiral and pull it loose.

Stakes loosen in wet weather. The chalk soaks up winter rain readily but the topsoil layer can become soft enough for a bamboo cane to work loose in the ground. Check the vertical and angle of each cane at every visit; push loose canes firmly back in or replace them with fresh ones pushed to a greater depth.

Rabbits find the gaps. A persistent rabbit will work at the base of a mesh cage until it has made a gap wide enough to pass through. If you find rabbit droppings inside a cage, the animal has found the entry point; close the gap and push the base coil deeper into the soil. A spiral that has been knocked off its cane and is lying flat around the stem offers no protection at all; reset it immediately.

Inspecting around guarded plants in spring and summer

When you go out to check guards between 1 March and 31 August, look into the interior of each plant as well as the guard. A newly planted hedgerow can attract nesting birds in its second spring even at modest height: robins and wrens in particular nest in low, dense cover close to the ground. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest. If you spot a nest with eggs or young while removing a weed from inside a guard, leave that plant alone for two to three weeks and return when the brood has fledged. The penalty on summary conviction is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine, assessed per nest.

Step 6: Remove guards and stakes once the hedge is established, correctly

Guards left on too long cause problems. Spiral guards left for more than three to four growing seasons can girdle the stem as it thickens, cutting off the flow of water and nutrients. Mesh cages left on indefinitely become buried in the growing hedge, rust, and require cutting out with snips. Tree tube shelters left past the point at which the stem fills the tube interior can cause distorted, weakened stem tissue that breaks in wind.

The removal test for spirals: after two to three growing seasons, hold the top of the spiral and try to slide it upward off the stem. If it pulls clear with gentle upward pressure, the stem has not yet filled the spiral and it can stay. If the spiral is clearly biting into the bark or has become difficult to slide, remove it by cutting the spiral lengthwise with a pair of snips and unwinding it, being careful not to cut the bark beneath. Do this in autumn when the plant is not in active growth.

Mesh cages: remove once the stems inside have reached 15-20mm in diameter and the plant is clearly established. Simply snip the cable ties, unroll the mesh, and pull the canes. A mesh cage that is partly woven into the hedge growth can be cut out section by section using snips; do not attempt to pull the whole cage out from one end, as this can tear bark and break young stems.

Stakes: remove as described in Step 4, in the second autumn, once the root system has established firm soil contact. Cut the cross-tie and ease the canes out rather than pulling them sharply; on Margate Chalk, which can be very firm by late autumn, a cane driven in deeply may need to be loosened with a gentle twist before it will come free without snapping.

Which hedge species are most vulnerable to rabbit browsing in CT10

Not all hedging plants are equally at risk. In my experience on CT10 sites, the species rabbits target most reliably are privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium), field maple (Acer campestre), hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus). These are all palatable to rabbits and their bark at stem base level is soft enough for rabbits to gnaw through in a single night. Any of these planted in a garden with confirmed rabbit access should be guarded without question.

Species with less palatability that are lower risk, though not zero risk: holly (Ilex aquifolium), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), Rosa rugosa and yew (Taxus baccata). Holly and blackthorn in particular are largely left alone by rabbits once the spines have emerged on the young growth, though in the first winter after planting, when whips are small and the spines are not yet fully developed, even these are not immune. Spirals on all newly planted stock in a medium-to-high-risk rabbit garden is a sound and inexpensive default.

Evergreen coastal species used in CT10 clifftop gardens, such as Griselinia littoralis, Escallonia and Euonymus, appear to be less palatable than deciduous hedging to rabbits. In practice, I still guard young Griselinia on any site near open land: its bark is soft in year one and a rabbit will browse soft bark opportunistically regardless of palatability.

Wind rock and the Thanet chalk: why it matters more here than inland

The chalk that runs under Broadstairs and CT10 is not a single solid layer. It is a fractured medium with a variable depth of rendzina topsoil above it, and that thin topsoil layer is what newly planted hedge roots must grip during the first growing season. On a sheltered inland site on Wealden clay, the soil mass around a planted root ball provides resistance to wind rock from its sheer weight and cohesion. On CT10 chalk, especially on north or east-facing garden boundaries within a kilometre of the coast, the topsoil layer may be only 100-150mm deep. A gust of 50mph, which is a routine autumn and winter occurrence at Broadstairs, can move a lightweight bare-root whip in chalk topsoil far more readily than the same gust would move the same plant on heavier soil.

The clifftop bays above Broadstairs, Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate, experience the strongest wind exposure, particularly from the north-east quarter. Any new hedge planting on a boundary facing the sea, or on a boundary perpendicular to the prevailing wind, on these sites should be staked as a matter of course even for species that would not need staking on an inland garden. The extra cost in bamboo canes and half an hour of labour at planting time is insignificant compared to the cost of replanting a hedge that has failed because its roots never got contact with the chalk.
