Reducing a clifftop hedge above Stone Bay, Joss Bay or Kingsgate.

A clifftop hedge reduction in Broadstairs is not the same job as reducing a hedge in a back garden two streets inland. The wind, the salt, the shallow chalk under the plant, and the protected wildlife nearby all pull the job in different directions. Three checks have to be passed before any saw comes out, and getting one wrong is expensive.

Many clifftop hedges along the stretch from Stone Bay north through Joss Bay, past the North Foreland lighthouse, out to Kingsgate, have quietly grown two or three metres past their design height. The owners want the sea back. That is a reasonable request. The way to get there is not to point a hedge trimmer at the top and take it down 40%. Here is what actually needs to happen.

Check one: the designated coast

The Broadstairs coastline is not just chalk cliffs. It lies within the Thanet Coast Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) and the Thanet Coast and Sandwich Bay Special Protection Area and Ramsar site, which are notable for their coastal chalk and for their birds.

The practical consequence: where the hedge sits at or overhangs the cliff face, it is worth asking Natural England or Thanet District Council whether the works need any consent before you start. In the private-garden context this rarely means the reduction is blocked; it means you should check and keep a note of what you were told. Timing the work outside the nesting season and doing a careful walk-round before you start is usually the sensible approach. Turning up in June with a chainsaw is not.

Check two: nesting gulls and other birds

The herring gull is on the UK red list of birds of conservation concern. Like all wild birds, its nests, eggs and young are protected by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly take, damage or destroy a nest while it is in use or being built, or to take or destroy eggs. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest. Disturbing a bird at the nest is an offence only for Schedule 1 species, but a hard reduction in the nesting window can still destroy a nest or its contents, and that is where the offence sits.

Herring gulls nest on cliff faces and roofs, and a tall boundary hedge close to a nest site can be affected by the work. If a gull nest is anywhere near the job, do not carry on without proper advice: ask Natural England whether a licence is needed.

When can clifftop reductions actually go ahead?

The practical window is September to the end of February. September catches the last of the growing weather; October and November are the calm months before the winter gales; late February gets ahead of the March to August nesting guidance window. Book that window early, because it fills up.

Check three: the shallow-chalk windthrow risk

This one is where the mistake usually happens, and it is the one nobody warns you about. The Thanet coast sits on the Margate Chalk Member, thin, alkaline topsoil over solid chalk. Leylandii in this soil profile does not develop a deep root plate. On an inland site, that is fine. On a clifftop plot exposed to coastal gales, a mature Leylandii carrying two decades of top growth is a windthrow candidate.

Named storms such as Ciaran in November 2023 brought damaging winds to southern England, and top-heavy conifers on shallow soil are the first to suffer. What people rarely notice: a hard reduction of a mature Leylandii, dropping 40% of the height in one go, does not make the tree safer. A sudden loss of canopy can leave the plant weakened, and the wind still catches the trunk. The right answer on a top-heavy clifftop Leylandii is a staged reduction over two or three seasons, bringing the height down gradually so the root-canopy balance is never shocked in one go.

The reduction spec for a clifftop hedge

  • Timing: September, October or the last ten days of February. Not March to August unless you have checked carefully for nests and, where gulls are involved, taken advice from Natural England.
  • Pre-works survey: a walk of the hedge line before the saw comes out, looking for inactive nests, active nests, cliff-edge overhang and adjacent gull activity. A short note filed with the quote is worthwhile.
  • Reduction depth: no more than a third of the standing height in a single season on Leylandii, cypress, holm oak or laurel. Escallonia, Griselinia, Elaeagnus and Olearia take a harder cut but still respond better to two moderate reductions over consecutive years than one hard one.
  • Cut placement: back to green growth on evergreens. Cutting into bare brown wood on Leylandii or cypress is close to permanent, those species do not regenerate from old wood.
  • Access: clifftop plots can mean rope access or a mobile platform; a scaffold tower over a lawn is often the safest option on small back gardens.
  • Arisings: bagged and taken away to a licensed waste site. Thanet District Council's garden waste subscription (£73 a year for 2026/27) takes stems under 7.5cm; for heavier material from your own property, Margate household waste recycling centre on Manston Road (run by Kent County Council, booking required) accepts household garden waste but not trade waste.

Species-by-species clifftop reduction notes

Leylandii and Monterey cypress. Staged reductions. Never cut into brown wood. On a tall clifftop hedge the windthrow risk climbs with height and staged reduction alone may not fully solve it, so it is worth flagging honestly at the survey stage.

Escallonia and Elaeagnus. Both take a hard clip and tend to regenerate cleanly. A substantial reduction on Escallonia in late September is a workable spec, and by the following summer the plant looks intentional again.

Holm oak. The classic East Kent salt-spray hedge, and more forgiving than people think. It reduces well in late summer or early autumn and regenerates from old wood.

Hawthorn. The native windbreak. Reduce November to February only, outside the nesting window. It takes a hard renovation cut back to the framework, and thickens up strongly the following spring.

Yew. A set-back species, not front line. If it is on the plot it wants a single late-summer cut and can be reduced hard back to old wood, one of the few conifers that will.
