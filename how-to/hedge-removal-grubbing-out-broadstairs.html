How to remove an old hedge in Broadstairs: grubbing out privet, Leylandii and laurel in CT10.

The most common call I get in late summer is from someone who has had enough of a Leylandii or privet hedge that has grown too large to renovate, and wants it out. Complete hedge removal is heavier work than most people expect, particularly on the free-draining chalk soils under Broadstairs gardens where the root system spreads wide rather than deep. This guide sets out the steps from the nesting-season timing check through to stump treatment and ground reinstatement.

Steps

Step 1: Check the timing against the nesting season

Hedge removal involves cutting branches, and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 applies to that cutting in exactly the same way it applies to trimming. Before booking or starting the job, confirm the date falls outside the practical nesting-season window. September through to the end of February is the safe zone for complete removal of most hedges in Broadstairs and CT10. If the hedge must come out during the March-to-August window, carry out a thorough nest check by walking the full length at close range, looking into the interior for active nests or adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section. If you find a nest with eggs or chicks, the job stops until the nest is vacated.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it applies to removal, not just trimming

Section 1 of the Act makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a nesting bird. The enforcement window on the Thanet coast runs from 1 March to 31 August, and on clifftop properties above Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate and North Foreland the additional constraint is the herring gull (UK Red-Listed since BoCC5, December 2021), for which general licences for nest disturbance have been withdrawn. An individual Natural England licence is now required for work near active herring-gull nests. Penalty: up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine per nest. Cutting a hedge down to the stump counts as disturbing the structure, so the same check applies as for a routine annual trim.

Step 2: Check for legal restrictions before any cutting starts

Three checks to run before you touch anything:

  • Conservation area (s.211): If the hedge is in one of the three Broadstairs conservation areas (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA) and any individual stem has a trunk diameter over 75mm at 1.5m height, that stem needs a six-week notification to Thanet District Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 before you cut it. Most hedging plants, including privet and Leylandii, never reach that diameter individually, so this rarely applies in practice, but check before cutting anything with a visible trunk. The fine is up to £20,000 per unauthorised tree.
  • Hedgerows Regulations 1997: These apply to hedgerows adjacent to agricultural land, a public right of way, or common land that are over 30 years old. The Regulations require you to notify the Local Planning Authority (Thanet District Council) at least 42 days before removing such a hedgerow. Most domestic garden hedges in Broadstairs, St Peter's and Reading Street are not caught by this because they do not meet the adjacency criteria, but if your hedge runs along the edge of a footpath or a field, get advice from TDC first.
  • Party hedge: If the hedge is shared with a neighbour or sits on or near the boundary, get written agreement from the neighbour before starting. Removing a shared hedge unilaterally opens you to a civil claim even if no planning law is involved.

Step 3: Cut the above-ground growth down before digging

Do not start digging around a full-height hedge. The above-ground weight makes the stump almost impossible to lever, and the branches obstruct your digging. Work in 2-3m sections from one end of the hedge to the other:

  1. Use loppers or a pruning saw to remove lateral branches first, working from the outside in.
  2. Cut the main stems down to 30-50cm above ground level using a pruning saw or chainsaw. Leave enough stump to give you a handle to push and lever against when digging.
  3. Stack cut material in manageable lengths (60cm or under for chipping or green-waste bags) as you go. Do not let it pile up behind you where it creates a tripping hazard.

For a 10m run of mature privet, plan around two hours for this stage before you pick up a spade. For Leylandii of 4m or more, factor in chainsaw time for the main stems.

Step 4: Dig out the root ball

This is the most variable stage: the root system you find depends heavily on the species and on the chalk soil profile underneath Broadstairs gardens. The Margate Chalk sits at 15-30cm below the surface in most of CT10. Roots cannot penetrate chalk, so they spread laterally in a wide, shallow slab rather than going down. That actually works slightly in your favour compared with deep clay soils: you are cutting under a slab rather than chasing roots vertically.

  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium): Roots are woody but not usually large. Start 30cm from the stump base and dig a spade's depth all round, then use a fork to prise outward and upward. Cut any roots you cannot break with loppers. Rock the stump back and forth to snap the deeper laterals, then lift. On chalk soils, most privet stumps come out in one piece within 20-30 minutes per plant.
  • Leylandii (×Cuprocyparis leylandii): The root plate is wide and dense, sometimes 1.5-2m across on a mature plant. Start digging 50-60cm from the stump, expose the main laterals and cut them through with a root saw or chainsaw close to the plate. Once the outer roots are cut, use a crow bar or log-splitter wedge to lever the stump upward. For a run of more than five or six Leylandii plants over 4m tall, hiring a tracked mini-digger (1-tonne machine) for a half-day is worth the cost: the machine lifts stumps in seconds that would take two people an hour each by hand.
  • Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus): Large and spreading root system. Same approach as Leylandii but the wood is harder. Expect the stump to resist more than privet. A stump grinder is an option for large established laurel rather than full excavation.
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): Sinuous, tough roots that anchor hard into any crack in the chalk. Allow more time than for privet: hawthorn does not lever out cleanly and the final few roots often need cutting with a root saw rather than pulling through.

Step 5: Treat remaining stumps and roots to prevent regrowth

For non-suckering species (Leylandii, yew, laurel, hawthorn): any stump or large root fragment left in the ground will not regenerate if cleanly separated from the main plant. Dig out as much as is practical; the remaining fibrous roots will decay over two to three seasons. No chemical treatment is needed for these species.

For suckering species (privet, blackthorn, hawthorn to a lesser degree): every root fragment left in the ground can send up new shoots. Privet is particularly persistent on Thanet soils: I have seen privet regrowth appear 60cm from a removed stump twelve months after grubbing out. Two approaches:

  1. Thorough manual removal: Follow every lateral root and cut it out to beyond the drip line. Time-consuming on a long hedge run, but leaves the soil chemical-free. Best practice if the area is to become a vegetable bed.
  2. Stump treatment: Within 30 seconds of making each cut, apply a concentrated glyphosate-based stump killer to the cut surface. The narrow application window matters: the cut surface begins drying and the cambium layer stops absorbing within about a minute on a warm day. A paintbrush applicator works better than a spray for this. One treatment at the point of cutting is usually enough for privet; hawthorn may need a second application six weeks later if regrowth appears.

Step 6: Remove arisings and dispose of green waste

Freshly cut hedge material from a 10m run of privet or Leylandii produces a significant volume of waste. Options in Thanet:

  • Garden shredder on site: Woody material up to 35-40mm diameter can be processed through a petrol shredder and the chip used directly as a path mulch or compost ingredient. Leylandii chip is too resinous for most compost heaps; use it as a path surface instead.
  • Thanet District Council household waste recycling centre: Green and garden waste is accepted at the Margate Road site in Broadstairs (between Broadstairs and Ramsgate). You can take hedge arisings in the back of a car or van; trade quantities require a permit. Check TDC's current opening hours before loading, as they vary seasonally.
  • Green-waste bags: Biodegradable sacks (available at most garden centres) are a clean solution for privet and soft deciduous material. Leylandii is too bulky and resinous for most bag services.

Allow for at least one and a half skips, or three or four full van loads, for a complete 10m run of mature privet hedge. Leylandii produces roughly twice the volume by mass.

Step 7: Reinstate the ground

After removal, the hedge line leaves a trench of disturbed soil and, in most CT10 gardens, a chalk face at 20-30cm depth. Fork over the area thoroughly, removing any remaining fine roots. On thin chalk soils, incorporate 10-15cm of compost or soil conditioner before any new planting or turfing: the compaction left by a long-established hedge, combined with the leached chalk near the surface, can make the strip very slow to establish grass or border plants without amendment.

If the area is to become lawn, give the soil two or three good waterings over a week after amendment before laying turf or seeding. On north or northeast-facing strips in North Foreland and Kingsgate gardens, which see very little summer sun, use a shade-tolerant grass seed rather than a standard lawn mix: the hedge will have created a suppression zone that persists for a season after removal.

Species by species: what to expect in a Broadstairs garden

Privet is by far the most common hedge removal job I take in CT10, usually because it has been allowed to grow to 3m or more and the owner has decided renovation is not worth the two-year effort. The above-ground work is fast, but the suckering root system requires either thorough manual clearance or herbicide treatment. Budget a full day for a 10m run, including disposal.

Leylandii removal is the other common request, usually from property owners who have inherited a screen hedge that has reached 5m or more and is now shading out a neighbour. The root ball is the hardest part. I always recommend a mini-digger for a run of six or more plants: it takes 20 minutes what otherwise takes a full day. Leylandii does not sucker, so there is no chemical treatment stage, but the volume of arising material is large. Most of what I remove in Kingsgate and North Foreland ends up going to a tree surgeon's wood-chip outlet rather than a skip.

Laurel (cherry and Portuguese) is the most physically demanding removal by hand. The root ball is dense and the wood resists cutting. For an established laurel hedge over 2m tall with stems thicker than 80mm, a stump grinder is worth hiring rather than trying to dig by hand: it grinds the stump to below soil level in minutes and the chips can be left in the hole to decompose.

Renovate or remove: the honest answer

Not every overgrown hedge needs taking out. Privet, hawthorn and blackthorn all regenerate from hard renovation cuts and can be brought back to a manageable size over two winters. Leylandii cannot: once cut back into brown dead wood it does not regenerate, so renovation beyond a certain point is not a real option and removal is the only outcome. Yew and box are the other extreme: both regenerate freely from old wood and almost never need complete removal. If you are considering removal because the hedge has grown too large, read the renovation guide first. If you are considering removal because the species is simply wrong for the site (Leylandii chosen for a small garden, a privet planted against a house wall), removal is usually the right call.

The chalk soil factor in CT10

The Margate Chalk that underlies most of Broadstairs and the clifftop bays creates a specific digging challenge. The topsoil layer, typically 20-30cm deep, is free-draining and often compacted under a mature hedge. Below that is chalk, sometimes soft and crumbly (flint-free chalk beds near Kingsgate and North Foreland) and sometimes harder. Roots cannot penetrate the chalk layer, so they spread wide and flat, which means your digging needs to be wide rather than deep: go 50-60cm out from the stump rather than trying to dig under it. The good news is that a root slab sitting on chalk is easier to lever up in sections than a root ball anchored 1.5m deep in clay.
