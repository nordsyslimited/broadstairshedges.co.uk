How to clean, sharpen and store hedge cutting tools at the end of the season: a CT10 contractor's checklist.

By late September, the main Broadstairs cutting season is behind you: the second cut on Escallonia and Griselinia is done, privet has had its September pass, and the blades are coated in a season's worth of plant sap, clippings and, on any clifftop property, marine salt residue. This guide sets out the nine steps I go through before the trimmer goes on the shelf until spring: safety first, then debris, sap, inspection, sharpening, lubrication, power management and storage.

Steps

Step 1: Confirm the last cut of the season and note the date

Before cleaning, decide whether the season is actually finished. On the Thanet coast, most evergreen hedges (Escallonia, Griselinia, Elaeagnus, Photinia, Pittosporum) have their second cut in late August or September. Privet's September pass is usually its last. Yew and box take their single annual cut in August and are done. Deciduous species that take a late-winter cut (hawthorn, beech, forsythia) will need the tools again in February or March, but you can still clean and store them properly between. If you are unsure whether a second cut is due before winter, check the CT10 calendar guide before putting the trimmer away.

Step 2: Make the tool safe before touching the blades

Never touch the blade assembly on a power hedge trimmer without first making it inert. For battery models, remove the battery pack. For corded electric, unplug it from the mains before approaching the blades. For petrol models, let the engine cool completely (at least 20 minutes after the last run), then disconnect the spark plug lead by pulling the rubber cap off the plug before working close to the blade. Hand shears and loppers are passive tools, but lay them open on a stable surface, cutting edges facing away from you, and work with gloves on throughout. This is not a formality: trimmer teeth remain sharp enough to cut skin after a full season.

Step 3: Remove plant debris from blades and body

Use a stiff-bristled brush (a detail brush or an old washing-up brush works well) to clear leaf fragments, packed clippings and stem debris from between the teeth on power trimmers, and from the pivot point and spring on hand shears. On power trimmers, clear the air intake vents and the guard plate too: compacted debris there reduces motor cooling and can cause the engine to overheat on the first warm day next spring. Work in good light so you can see what you are removing. On a trimmer that has had a full Broadstairs season through Escallonia, Olearia or Griselinia, this step alone takes 5-10 minutes done properly.

Step 4: Strip plant sap with a resin solvent

Dried sap is the invisible problem. Every cut releases sticky resin or juice that builds on the blade surface and, once dried, makes the blades drag instead of cut. On power trimmers, drag puts extra load on the drive motor and opens a gap between tooth tips and the backing plate that leads to missed stems. The best remover is a proprietary resin solvent: STIHL Superclean (available at garden machinery dealers in Margate and Ramsgate), Husqvarna blade cleaner, or an equivalent resin remover from any well-stocked garden centre. Apply it to a cloth and wipe the full blade length; do not spray it directly onto electrical components or battery contacts. On hand shears, kitchen degreaser or isopropyl alcohol on a cloth clears most sap residue from the blade faces and the pivot.

Coastal tools need more than a quick wipe

On clifftop properties above Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate and North Foreland, blades accumulate not just plant sap but marine salt residue that the northeast wind deposits on every outdoor surface. Salt and moisture together corrode carbon-steel tool edges faster than either would alone. A trimmer used on a coastal hedge and left uncleaned over winter in an unventilated lean-to will show surface rust on the blade faces by December. A resin solvent that leaves a thin protective film rather than leaving the surface bare metal (most proprietary hedge-trimmer cleaners do this) is worth the small extra cost for any tool that works regularly on the Thanet coast.

Step 5: Inspect blades for damage and wear

Hold the clean blade assembly up to a good light source and look along its length. On a power trimmer, each tooth should be intact and roughly uniform in profile. A bent tooth creates vibration during the cut and risks nicking the adjacent tooth on the return stroke. A chipped tooth edge leaves a ragged cut surface on stems and takes a visibly longer time to cut through the same thickness of growth. On hand shears, run your eye along each blade edge. A clean, sharp edge catches the light uniformly. A dull or rolled edge reflects light unevenly. Note any sections that need attention at Step 6.

Step 6: Sharpen the blades

For power trimmer blades: use a diamond whetstone or a fine flat file. Match the original bevel angle on each tooth, which is typically 30-45 degrees on most reciprocating trimmer blades (check your manufacturer's manual for the exact figure if the blade has been resharpened before and the original angle is unclear). Work tooth by tooth, applying 3-4 light strokes along the bevel face and keeping the file flat against the face. You are restoring the edge, not reshaping the tooth: 3-4 strokes per tooth is enough for a blade that has had a normal season. After sharpening the top face, run the flat face of the file lightly along the back of the blade to remove the burr. For hand shears: clamp each blade in a vice or between your knees, bevel face up, and draw a fine whetstone along the edge in smooth passes from heel to tip, maintaining the original bevel angle. Three or four passes per blade is usually enough for a season's normal use. Remove the burr from the flat back face with a single light pass, keeping the stone flat.

If a trimmer blade has significant damage (multiple bent teeth, deep chips, a blade that has been run into a wire or a hard post), it is worth taking it to a garden machinery specialist rather than attempting to file it back to service. Most dealers in Thanet offer blade-sharpening services; a professional grind done once a year is cheaper than replacing a motor worn out by dragging blades.

Step 7: Lubricate blades and moving parts

Apply a thin, even film of proprietary hedge trimmer oil, light machine oil, or 3-in-1 oil to the full blade surface on power trimmers. Do not use WD-40 as a long-term lubricant: it is a water displacer and corrosion inhibitor, but it evaporates relatively quickly and leaves little protective film over several months. For long-term storage, a blade oil with a slightly heavier viscosity holds on the metal surface through a winter. Run the power trimmer briefly (hold it clear of any surface, blades safely pointed away) to distribute the oil across the full blade length. On hand shears, apply oil to the pivot bolt and the blade faces, then open and close the blades several times to work the oil into the pivot. Wipe off any excess: a thick oil coat collects dust and grass seed over winter and turns into an abrasive paste by spring.

Step 8: Manage the power source for storage

For battery-powered tools: remove the battery pack and store it separately from the tool. Most lithium-ion battery manufacturers recommend a charge level of 40-60% for long-term storage. A battery stored fully flat over winter can lose capacity permanently; a battery stored fully charged at 100% stresses the cells. If the battery was at full charge after the last job, run a short task to bring it down to approximately half before removing it. For petrol-powered hedge trimmers: drain the fuel tank completely or add a fuel stabiliser to the tank and carburettor. Petrol that sits in a carburettor for three months degrades, leaves varnish deposits on the needle valve and main jet, and causes hard starting or no-start the following spring. To drain: remove the tank cap and tilt the trimmer to pour the fuel back into your can, then run the engine (outdoors, with the spark plug lead reconnected) until it stops from fuel starvation. This takes 2-3 minutes and leaves the carburettor dry.

Step 9: Choose the right storage location

A dry, frost-free, ventilated shed or garage is ideal. The trimmer can hang on a wall hook (blade guard on) or stand upright; avoid lying it flat with the blade assembly resting on a damp floor. For hand shears and loppers: hang them on a hook or store them standing up with the blades closed, not loose in a pile where the edges rub against other metal tools. The worst storage environment on the Thanet coast is an unventilated lean-to or exterior cupboard that gets condensation every cold night from the sea air. If that is your only option, wrap the tool in a dry cloth or breathable tool roll and check it once mid-winter for rust spots; a light re-oiling in January costs nothing and prevents a season-opening repair job in March.

Why the end-of-season clean matters more on the Thanet coast

Inland gardens dry out between jobs. On the Thanet coast, the prevailing northeast wind off the North Sea carries salt spray and moisture onto every surface between cuts. A trimmer used on a clifftop Griselinia hedge in late September will have both plant sap and marine salt residue on its blades by the time it goes into storage. Salt is hygroscopic: it pulls moisture from the air and holds it against the metal surface. A blade left in that state for four months, even in an apparently dry garage in North Foreland or Kingsgate, will have visible rust by February. Clean blades, properly lubricated, resist this. Blunt or damaged blades also bruise and tear plant tissue rather than cutting it cleanly. The first cut of the following spring on a well-maintained set of blades takes less time and leaves the hedge looking tidier than the same cut done with a blade that has spent winter in an uncleaned state.

Hand shears versus power trimmers: maintenance differences

The cleaning and lubrication steps are broadly the same for both, but the sharpening method is different. Power trimmer teeth are sharpened by filing each tooth individually on one face only, matching the original bevel; the back face is flat and only deburred. Hand shears are sharpened on both blades, one face per blade, with the pivot removed or held fixed. The pivot itself is a wear point on hand shears that power tools do not have: tighten the pivot bolt as part of the annual clean if the blades have developed any flex or sideways play. A correctly tensioned pair of shears should cut a single sheet of paper cleanly with no tearing; a pair that crumples the paper needs either the pivot tightened or the blades resharpened.

Disinfection: when it matters

For most Broadstairs hedges, standard cleaning and lubrication is all that is needed at end of season. But if you have been cutting box (Buxus) at a property where box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) has been identified or suspected, or cutting any plant showing signs of disease, the blades need disinfecting before storage, not just cleaning. Use a diluted bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water), wipe the blades, leave for 30 seconds, then rinse and dry thoroughly before oiling. The same precaution applies if you have cut between several properties in one day and want to avoid carrying fungal spores from a garden with known box blight into a clean one. At end of season, a single disinfection pass is a sensible precaution if box is in any of your regular gardens.
