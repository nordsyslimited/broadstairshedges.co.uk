How to prepare your hedge for winter in Broadstairs: the October checklist.

October is the month that decides how a Broadstairs hedge comes through winter. The nesting season closed 31 August. The holiday-let peak has wound down. And the northeast gale season that runs from October through February is about to begin: Storm Ciárán hit on 1–2 November 2023, Storm Eówyn in January 2025. A hedge that goes into winter with a bare base, a loose stake on a new planting, or an unaddressed section of structural weakness takes more damage and costs more to recover than one that had an hour's attention in early October. These are the steps I work through on every CT10 hedge before I put the trimmers away for the season.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the full boundary on a dry morning, hedge by hedge.

Do the whole walk in one go and note each hedge separately before you do anything. Species, approximate length, last cut date, and any visible issues. This matters because the decisions you make in Step 3 differ by species, and it is easy to forget an awkward section at the back of the garden if you do not walk the whole boundary first. On CT10 properties above the cliff edge at Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate or the North Foreland approach, also check whether any sections have been pushed or lopsided by summer northeast winds: a hedge that has been wind-shifted over the summer and not corrected before winter will set into that lean by spring.

Bring a notebook or use your phone to record the list. You want: species, current height and width, condition (good, needs trim, structural concern, gap), and estimated volume of mulch needed at the base. That last item sounds like overkill until you get to Step 6 and find you have underestimated how much ground to cover.

Step 2: Confirm nesting season has ended before carrying out any cutting.

October is outside the nesting caution window: what that means in practice

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest containing eggs or young. The practical caution window for Broadstairs hedge work is 1 March to 31 August. By October that window has been closed for over a month. Routine hedge cutting in October requires no nest check as a legal precaution. House martins and swifts, the two late-departing migrants that are most visible on the Thanet coast in late summer, do not nest in garden hedges; they are cavity and structure nesters and will have departed CT10 by the end of September in most years. For clifftop and cliff-face hedges, the herring gull caution that I extend to 30 September in gull-specific contexts is also clear by October. October through February is the most legally uncomplicated period to carry out hedge work of any kind.

The one practical check that still applies in October is not legal but operational: look for late-season spiders' webs and wasp nests before running a trimmer through dense evergreen growth. A wasp nest in an Escallonia or Griselinia hedge in early October is not uncommon on CT10, and the colony is still active. A quick inspection before cutting prevents an unpleasant encounter rather than a legal one.

Step 3: Make last-cut decisions by hedge type before mid-October.

Not every hedge type has the same autumn cutting window, and the most common October mistake is treating them all the same. Here is the relevant breakdown for the CT10 palette:

  • Formal evergreens: yew, box, holly. If a late-season cut is still needed to tidy up growth from August or September, carry it out before mid-October. Any later and the new wound surfaces are exposed to frost and northeast wind at their most vulnerable. Yew and holly handle late cutting well; box is more susceptible to cold-weather wound damage. A yew or holly hedge cut on 14 October will be fine. The same cut on 10 November in a year with early frosts carries more risk.
  • Evergreen coastal species: Griselinia, Escallonia, Pittosporum, Elaeagnus. Do not cut these after mid-October unless a specific structural problem demands it. These species are salt-tolerant and moderately frost-hardy but they do not like combination exposure of fresh cut surfaces to northeast wind and frost. Escallonia in particular is prone to die-back on late-cut stems in a hard winter. Any shaping for these should already be done from the late-August to early-October window.
  • Deciduous hedges: beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple. These are counterintuitively the easiest to manage in autumn and winter: dormant deciduous hedges can be cut from November through to the end of February, and this is the preferred window for native hedgerows under standard UK hedgerow management practice. If a beech or hornbeam hedge still needs its annual trim, there is no urgency in October: it will be fine in November, December or January.
  • Conifers: Leylandii, Thuja, Western red cedar. The October window remains open, but do not cut after mid-October. Leylandii will not reshoot from brown old wood, so any cut into the foliage in late October or November that exposes dead growth underneath cannot be corrected. If the annual trim has not been done by mid-October, wait until April.
  • Rosa rugosa and other thorny coastal species. These can be hard pruned in winter dormancy (November onwards). October is fine for any tidying but not necessary: they are tough enough to go through winter in overgrown condition without long-term damage.

Step 4: Clear debris and accumulated organic material from the base of each hedge.

Fallen leaves, fine soil that has migrated against the stems, and organic debris compacted into the base create a persistently damp zone that encourages fungal problems through winter, particularly phytophthora root rot on cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel, and the conditions that favour box blight on box hedges. On Margate chalk, the free-draining soil profile normally prevents waterlogging, but accumulated material at the stem base can create a localised wet zone that does not drain in the way that open chalk does.

Using a hand rake, clear the area from the outer stems back 150–200mm. You do not need to remove the material entirely: a light raking away from the stems is enough. What you want to expose is the actual soil surface, so that the mulch applied in Step 6 sits on soil rather than on a layer of old organic material.

If you find any sections of stem with visible fungal fruiting bodies (white or grey cottony growth, rust-coloured patches, or dark streaks in the bark), note these for a closer inspection. Most are superficial, but box blight in particular spreads through wet autumns and can be caught early if you are looking.

Step 5: Check for gaps and record bare-root planting requirements for November.

October is the right month to plan bare-root hedge planting because November is when bare-root stock starts to become available, and the best plants are taken first. Bare-root whips and transplants cost 30–40% less than pot-grown equivalents and typically establish better when planted between November and March, during the dormant season, on the free-draining Thanet chalk soils.

Walk the hedge base looking for: gaps from summer drought losses (CT10 records just 612.6mm of rain per year, and dry May-to-July periods kill newly established plants that were not watered); sections thinned by wind loading where internal branches have died back; and any sections that have dropped significantly below the target height and need additional plants to restore density at the base.

Measure any gaps that need filling: a gap under 300mm will usually close from neighbouring shoot growth given one growing season; a gap of 300–600mm needs one additional plant; anything wider than 600mm needs gapping-up with two or more plants depending on species and desired density. Note the species required: not every species is available in October for November delivery, so ordering early matters.

Step 6: Apply autumn mulch to all hedge bases.

This is the single most useful thing you can do for a Broadstairs hedge in October, and the step most often missed. On Margate chalk, with Manston recording 612.6mm of rainfall per year, the moisture that matters most for hedge root health is the moisture retained in the upper 200mm of topsoil from autumn onwards: it determines how much moisture is available when new root growth starts in April. A dry autumn on Thanet chalk, with no mulch applied, means the upper root zone is desiccated by the time spring growth demands water. A 75mm layer of composted bark or wood chip retains moisture, insulates surface roots against frost, and decomposes slowly through winter to add organic matter to the notoriously thin chalk topsoil.

Application method: lay the mulch in a 300mm band on each side of the hedge base, or in a continuous strip along the base if the hedge is on a boundary. Keep the mulch 50mm clear of the stems themselves, particularly on box and laurel, where contact with bark encourages fungal issues. If the base of the hedge runs alongside a path or hard surface, a 150mm depth on the single accessible side is still significantly better than nothing.

Best materials for CT10: composted bark chip, composted wood chip, or spent mushroom compost (which also adds lime, useful on the already-alkaline chalk, but in small amounts will not harm most species). Spent mushroom compost should be avoided on any ericaceous plants, but the CT10 hedge palette contains none of these: rhododendron, azalea, camellia and heather will not survive the alkaline chalk environment, so they are simply not present in CT10 gardens in any significant number.

Step 7: Check stakes and ties on all hedges planted in the last two years.

Run your hand along every stake on hedges planted in 2024 or 2025. The test: the stake should feel completely firm in the ground when you push against it with moderate hand pressure. Any stake that rocks or twists needs either re-driving or replacing before the first gales of November arrive. A stake that fails in a January storm cannot be corrected until the storm passes, by which point the root zone of the new planting has already been disrupted by movement.

On Margate chalk, stakes are typically driven 450–500mm into the ground. The chalk is hard enough that a correctly driven stake seats firmly, but the transition zone between thin topsoil and compact chalk at the 100–300mm depth layer can work a stake loose over a summer of growth, and the autumn loosening of the soil by rainfall after a dry summer does not always re-firm a stake that has already shifted.

Check every tree tie: rubber tree ties stretch and need to be firm but not cutting into the stem; the test is that you can slide two fingers between the tie and the stem, but no more. Twine, horticultural tape or garden wire left over from the original planting should be replaced with proper rubber tree ties now, if this has not already been done. Wire or thin twine that cuts into bark over winter causes a constriction point that shows up as a raised ring on the stem the following spring, and in a bad case can girdle the stem and kill the new plant.

Step 8: Note any conservation-area tree or hedge work that needs notification before winter.

This step applies if your property falls inside Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA or Reading Street CA, which between them cover large parts of the central town, the clifftop approach above Viking Bay, and Reading Street village. In these areas, any work on a tree with a stem diameter over 75mm at 1.5m height (roughly the thickness of a large wrist) requires a Section 211 notification to Thanet District Council. The statutory minimum waiting period is six weeks from receipt of the notification before work can begin, unless TDC responds sooner.

Work planned for November or December needs the notification submitted by mid-October at the latest. Work needed in January still benefits from October notification: the six-week clock starts from TDC's receipt, not your intended work date, and council offices close over Christmas. If work involves a significant hedge reduction or removal in a conservation area and you have not sent notification, October is the last realistic month to start the clock for a November completion.

Routine hedge cutting does not trigger s.211, provided the hedge is genuinely a hedge and not a row of trees that reads as a hedge. If you are uncertain whether a multi-stem section qualifies as a hedge or as individual trees under TDC's interpretation, the safer approach is to call Thanet Planning before cutting, not after. The £20,000-per-tree fine that applies in conservation areas is the penalty for work carried out without proper notification where notification was required.

Evergreens and deciduous: different objectives in October

The October checklist serves two different purposes depending on what type of hedge you are looking at. For evergreens, October is mostly about completing the season's maintenance: confirming the formal profile is correct before the hedge stops responding to cutting, dealing with any structural issues before winter weather makes access difficult, and getting the mulch down before the ground becomes too hard or wet to work comfortably. For deciduous hedges, October is the start of the best maintenance window: the leaves are falling, the stems are going dormant, and the next three months are actually the preferred cutting period for native hedgerows under most management guidance.

Beech and hornbeam deserve a specific note here because they are technically deciduous but retain their dead leaves through winter, a characteristic called marcescence. A beech or hornbeam hedge cut in November will lose its green leaves but keep the dead copper-brown ones through to April, maintaining its screening function all winter. This is not a problem: it is how these species behave, and a properly shaped beech hedge provides better winter screening than most people expect from a deciduous plant.

Frost-tender coastal species: notes for a hard CT10 winter

The CT10 coastal hedge palette includes several species that are rated as borderline frost-hardy in UK horticultural references. Pittosporum tenuifolium, Choisya ternata, Hebe spp. and Griselinia littoralis are all listed with hardiness ratings that leave them potentially vulnerable in hard winters where temperatures drop below −5°C for extended periods.

In practice, most Broadstairs town gardens (set back from the cliff edge, protected by buildings) sit in a warm microclimate that has not seen −5°C sustained for more than two or three nights in the last decade. These species are well established in the area and the risk of total loss is low in an average winter. The gardens at higher risk are those directly on the cliff edge at Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate and the outer North Foreland properties, where the wind-chill factor from northeast gales lowers the effective temperature well below the stated air temperature, and where the combination of frost and salt-laden wind is more damaging to evergreen foliage than either factor alone.

For borderline species on exposed clifftop plots, the practical precautions are: do not cut them after mid-October (fresh wounds are vulnerable to wind scorch and frost), and consider a light horticultural fleece wrap on the most exposed faces of any Pittosporum or Choisya that has been recently planted. Established specimens of five years or more that have already proved themselves through previous winters on the same site do not generally need protection.

Why October mulching matters more in Broadstairs than it does inland

The combination of low rainfall and fast-draining chalk substrate makes moisture management significantly more important in CT10 than in most other parts of Kent. Manston records 612.6mm of rainfall annually, placing Thanet among the driest zones in England. The Margate Chalk Member, which underlies most of Broadstairs, St Peter's and the North Foreland area, is a free-draining Upper Cretaceous chalk: water passes through the upper 200mm of topsoil and into the chalk rapidly, with very little lateral moisture retention.

The practical consequence is that hedge roots on Thanet chalk need the moisture retained in the topsoil organic layer in a way that roots on clay loam in, say, the Weald do not. A hedge on deep Weald clay has access to moisture held in the clay matrix at depth; a hedge on Margate chalk has access only to the moisture in the thin topsoil above the chalk. In autumn and winter, that topsoil dries out faster than the UK average because Thanet is drier and the chalk does not buffer the drying. A 75mm mulch layer applied in October significantly delays that drying process and means roots that are doing their repair and establishment work through winter have adequate moisture to do so.

The effect is measurable in new plantings. A bare-root hedge planted in November on unmulched chalk on a north-facing boundary in Broadstairs will often fail in its second summer even if it made it through the first year, because the second summer dries the root zone below the point the root system can recover from. The same planting with a 75mm mulch layer applied in October of the first year survives the second summer consistently. This is the reason I mulch every new planting I put in as part of the planting job, and why I recommend the autumn mulch to established hedges as well.
