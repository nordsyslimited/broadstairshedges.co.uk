Hedgerow Regulations: how to check whether you need permission before removing a countryside hedge in Broadstairs.

Most CT10 landowners know about the section 211 notice for conservation areas and the section 154 notice for highway overhangs. Far fewer realise that a separate set of regulations, the Hedgerow Regulations 1997, specifically protects countryside hedgerows on agricultural and rural land from removal, and that since May 2024 a second set of management rules adds seasonal cutting restrictions for important hedgerows on farms and equestrian land. Neither applies to garden hedges. Both carry unlimited-fine penalties if ignored.

Steps

Step 1: Establish whether your hedge is on qualifying land

The Hedgerow Regulations 1997 (SI 1997/1160) apply to hedgerows growing in, or adjacent to, land used for agriculture, forestry, the breeding or keeping of horses, ponies or donkeys, or common land. They do not apply to any hedgerow within the curtilage of a dwelling house or marking the boundary of that curtilage. This is the first and most important filter.

In practice this means that the garden privet along the front of a Broadstairs terrace, and the escallonia running along the back of a holiday-let garden in St Peter's, are outside the Regulations entirely. The hawthorn hedge along the boundary of a CT10 equestrian paddock near Reading Street, or the blackthorn line running along the edge of an arable field on the Thanet lanes west of North Foreland, almost certainly falls within them.

The boundary between "garden" and "qualifying land" can be blurred, particularly for older CT10 properties where a paddock or vegetable garden grades gradually into a farm field. If you are uncertain, check the Land Registry title plan to establish what land is included in the curtilage of the dwelling, and what is registered separately as agricultural land. The boundary ownership guide covers how to order and read the title plan.

Step 2: Check the minimum length threshold

Not every qualifying-land hedgerow is covered. The Regulations apply if the hedgerow is at least 20 metres in length, or if it is shorter than 20 metres but its ends both connect to another hedgerow. In practice, isolated fragments under 20 metres that are not part of a wider hedgerow network fall outside the Regulations.

For most CT10 boundary hedgerows, length is not the limiting factor: the old hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple lines that run along the farm lane edges in Reading Street and around the equestrian land east of Margate Road are substantially longer than 20 metres. The 20-metre rule mainly matters for short ornamental runs that are within garden curtilage anyway.

Measure the hedgerow from end to end along its length, not across it. Where the hedgerow follows a curved boundary, measure along the curve. If it connects to another hedgerow at both ends, it is covered regardless of its individual length.

Step 3: Assess whether the hedge might be classified as "important"

Not every qualifying hedgerow is equally protected, but the distinction matters for what the Local Planning Authority (LPA) can do with your Removal Notice. A hedgerow is classified as "important" under Schedule 2 of the Regulations if it has existed for at least 30 years and meets at least one criterion from the Schedule's wildlife, historical or archaeological lists. The LPA makes the final determination, not the landowner, but understanding whether your hedge is a likely candidate changes your planning timescales considerably.

The age threshold is 30 years, which means a hedgerow planted before 1996 qualifies. For most of the countryside hedgerows in CT10, age is rarely the barrier: the old boundary lines around Reading Street, the farm hedges along the rural lanes west of Broadstairs, and the blackthorn thickets at the edge of the North Foreland estate land are almost all substantially older than 30 years. The historical Ordnance Survey maps of Thanet, available through the National Library of Scotland map viewer and through the Thanet History Archive, give a good indication of when a hedge was established.

Schedule 2 importance criteria (a hedge needs to be 30+ years old and meet at least one):

  • Archaeological: the hedge marks the boundary of a pre-1850 parish, estate or farm; it incorporates an archaeological feature (bank, ditch, earthwork); it forms part of a field system pre-dating 1600.
  • Historical: it marks the boundary between two or more historic counties, parishes, hundreds or other administrative units; it is referred to in an historical document older than 1600.
  • Wildlife species: it contains 7 or more native woody species in any consecutive 30-metre section; or it contains 3 or more of a list of woodland indicator plant species (e.g. dog's mercury, yellow archangel, bluebell, ransoms).
  • Ecological: it provides a habitat for a Schedule 5 or Schedule 8 species under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; it is adjacent to an SSSI or a National Nature Reserve; it is a species-rich hedgerow in the terms of the Regulations.

For Broadstairs CT10, the SSSI adjacency criterion is particularly relevant. The Thanet Coast SSSI/SPA/Ramsar designation covers the clifftop strip from North Foreland to Joss Bay and beyond. Any hedge growing adjacent to or near the boundary of this designation that also meets an age or species criterion is likely to qualify as important. That includes some of the older hedgerows along the garden boundaries of North Foreland properties and along the cliff-edge lanes near Kingsgate.

Step 4: Submit a Hedgerow Removal Notice to Thanet District Council

If you have a qualifying hedgerow on qualifying land (Steps 1-2) and wish to remove it, you must submit a Hedgerow Removal Notice to Thanet District Council before any work begins. TDC is the Local Planning Authority for all of CT10. Submitting late, after removal has started, is not an option; starting removal without submitting the notice is a criminal offence.

The notice is submitted through the Planning Portal (planningportal.co.uk) using the standard Hedgerow Removal Notice form. There is no application fee. You will need to provide:

  • Your name and contact details as the owner or occupant of the land.
  • A description of the works proposed (what length is to be removed, and why).
  • A plan at a scale of 1:2500 or larger showing the hedgerow's location, its full length, and the section to be removed clearly marked.
  • The reason for removal: agricultural improvement, development, highway works, or another stated reason.

TDC will acknowledge receipt and begin the 42-day determination period. Keep a copy of the acknowledgement and the submitted plan. Do not begin any removal work until you have received written confirmation from TDC that the hedgerow is not important, or until the 42 days have elapsed without a response.

Step 5: Wait for TDC's response and act on it

Within 42 days of submitting the notice, TDC will either issue a written notification that the hedgerow is not important and work can proceed, or issue a Retention Notice confirming that the hedgerow is important and must not be removed. If 42 days pass with no response from TDC, you are legally entitled to proceed with the removal.

If TDC issues a Retention Notice, you have two options. You can abandon the removal entirely, leaving the hedgerow in place. Or you can appeal to the Planning Inspectorate within 28 days of the Retention Notice being issued. The appeal is made in writing to the Planning Inspectorate (planningportal.co.uk) and must include the original notice, TDC's retention notice, and your grounds for appeal. The Inspectorate appoints an inspector to decide the case; it can uphold TDC's decision, overturn it, or allow removal with conditions (such as replacement planting).

If TDC grants permission for removal, that permission lasts for two years from the date of the written notification (or from the end of the 42-day period if no notice was issued). If the work is not completed within that time, a fresh Removal Notice is required.

Step 6: Check whether the 2024 Management Rules add seasonal cutting restrictions

Since 1 May 2024, an additional layer of protection applies to hedgerows on agricultural land in England. The Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/680) make it illegal to cut or trim an important hedgerow on agricultural land during the period 1 March to 31 August each year. This is enforced by the Rural Payments Agency (RPA), not by TDC.

The 2024 Regulations apply more broadly than the 1997 Regulations: they treat any hedgerow on agricultural land as important for the purposes of the seasonal cutting restriction, without requiring the Schedule 2 importance test. If your land is used for agriculture, any hedgerow on it falls under the 2024 seasonal restriction, whether or not it would qualify as "important" under the 1997 Schedule 2 criteria.

The 1 March cutoff: two laws, one window

The Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations 2024 ban cutting important hedgerows on agricultural land from 1 March to 31 August. That window is not a coincidence: it aligns with the nesting-season guidance under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a Schedule 1 species at or near its nest. On summary conviction, the WCA 1981 penalty is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine per nest. The 2024 Regulations add a second, separate criminal offence for cutting on agricultural land during the same period. On rural CT10 properties, both laws apply simultaneously from March onwards.

What "important" means in practice for CT10 hedgerows

The Schedule 2 wildlife species criterion is the one most likely to be triggered by CT10 boundary hedgerows, and it is worth understanding before you assume a hedge will not be classified as important. The 7-species-in-30-metres test is a count of native woody species within a 30-metre stretch of hedge. For a typical mixed Reading Street or St Peter's boundary hedge containing hawthorn, blackthorn, elder, dog rose, field maple, hazel and bramble, that threshold is already met. Add a spindle (Euonymus europaeus), guelder rose (Viburnum opulus) or crab apple (Malus sylvestris), all native species found in older Thanet hedgerows, and the case is strengthened further.

The woodland indicator plant criterion can also be triggered by species that are common in the understorey of old Thanet hedgerows. Dog's mercury, three-cornered leek and lesser celandine are regularly found at the base of old farm hedges along the rural lanes in CT10 and in the sheltered hedgerows running through the Reading Street conservation area. Three woodland indicator species in the stretch is enough to meet the Schedule 2 test, combined with the age requirement.

If the hedge you are proposing to remove runs along a historic parish boundary, an ancient trackway or the edge of a known scheduled monument, the archaeological and historical criteria are also relevant. Much of the CT10 rural fringe sits within or adjacent to nationally important archaeological zones: the lanes around Reading Street and the land behind Kingsgate Bay have known prehistoric and Roman-period archaeological significance, and an old hedge on that landscape has a reasonable chance of meeting a historical boundary criterion.

CT10 properties most likely to be affected

The Hedgerow Regulations and the 2024 Management Rules affect a specific subset of CT10 properties. If you are removing a hedge on any of the following, a Removal Notice is almost certainly required:

  • Equestrian paddocks and smallholdings anywhere in CT10, including the small-holding fringe south-west of Broadstairs and around Westgate-on-Sea.
  • Agricultural land in the Reading Street area, the Margate Road corridor, and the lanes west of North Foreland where arable, horticultural and pastoral fields persist.
  • Land adjacent to the Thanet Coast SSSI, particularly the clifftop and near-cliff positions around Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate and North Foreland, where SSSI-adjacency triggers the ecological importance criterion.
  • Any farm, holding or rural property where the hedgerow bounds a public right of way shown on the Thanet definitive map.

Garden hedgerows are excluded, so the standard Broadstairs terrace privet, the escallonia along a St Peter's holiday-let boundary, and the Griselinia hedge in a CT10 suburban back garden are all outside the Regulations. If you are in any doubt about whether your hedge is on garden land or qualifying land, the Land Registry title documents and the planning boundary maps at TDC are the starting point.

Penalty for removal without complying

Removing a qualifying hedgerow without submitting a Removal Notice, or removing a hedgerow after TDC has issued a Retention Notice (and before the appeal period has elapsed or an appeal has been decided in your favour), is a criminal offence under the 1997 Regulations. The penalty on summary conviction is an unlimited fine. TDC also has powers to require replacement planting as a condition of any subsequent retrospective consent, so the practical cost of non-compliance can substantially exceed the original removal cost.

Similarly, cutting a hedgerow on agricultural land between 1 March and 31 August in breach of the 2024 Management Regulations is a separate criminal offence, enforceable by the RPA. The RPA has conducted site visits and acted on public referrals in 2025 for suspected breaches of the seasonal cutting restriction. Both laws are actively enforced; they are not theoretical risks.
