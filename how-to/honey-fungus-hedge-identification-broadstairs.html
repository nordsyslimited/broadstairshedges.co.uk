How to identify and manage honey fungus in a garden hedge: diagnosis, removal and replanting for Broadstairs CT10.

Honey fungus (Armillaria spp.) is, according to the RHS, the most destructive fungal disease in UK gardens, and older gardens with established boundary hedges and old fruit trees or stumps in the soil are the likeliest places for it. The fungus spreads underground and can be well advanced before a plant shows any visible sign. This guide sets out exactly what to look for, how to confirm the diagnosis beyond doubt, what to do about infected plants, and which species to plant on the affected site when you are ready to replant.

Steps

Step 1: Check for honey-coloured toadstools in late September and October

The most visually obvious sign of honey fungus arrives in autumn. From late summer through autumn, clusters of toadstools appear at the base of infected plants or from stumps and buried root material nearby. The caps are 5-15cm across, honey-yellow to pale brown (occasionally darker towards the centre), with a ring or skirt on the upper part of the stem. The gills underneath the cap are cream-coloured and run slightly down the stem.

The toadstools are not always present: Armillaria does not fruit every year. Do not assume an absence of toadstools means the site is clean. But when they do appear, their presence around a failing or dead plant is a strong first signal. Photograph them before they deteriorate: the cluster arrangement, the ring on the stem and the honey-yellow colour are the three features to confirm. Do not confuse them with the sulphur tuft (Hypholoma fasciculare), which also grows in clusters on stumps but has greenish-yellow gills and a more bitter smell.

Step 2: Look year-round for the slower signs of decline

Honey fungus does not announce itself with a sudden collapse in most cases. The more typical pattern is a progressive decline over one to three growing seasons. Watch for these in any established hedge plant:

  • A plant that leafs out noticeably later in spring than its neighbours of the same species
  • Thinner, sparser growth than the sections on either side, especially noticeable in formal hedges of privet, box or Leylandii
  • Yellow or pale green foliage that does not respond to feeding or watering
  • Premature autumn colouring on deciduous plants (a beech or field maple section going orange in August rather than October)
  • Partial dieback of one side of a plant while the other side still looks healthy
  • Complete sudden death of a plant in an otherwise healthy-looking hedge, especially in summer

Any of these alone could indicate drought stress, waterlogging, root damage or another disease. The next two steps confirm or rule out honey fungus specifically.

Step 3: Do the bark peel test at the root collar

The bark peel test is the most reliable in-garden confirmation method, and it works on any woody plant. Take a sharp penknife or chisel. At the junction where the plant's main stem meets the soil surface (the root collar), scrape away the soil to expose a small area of bark and then carefully lever off a section of the outer bark, about 50mm square. Look at the wood surface and the inner face of the bark you have removed.

If honey fungus is present, you will see a white or cream-coloured, fan-shaped sheet of fungal tissue growing between the bark and the wood. It has a flat, pressed appearance (unlike the three-dimensional structure of other moulds) and gives off a very distinctive mushroom-like smell: clean, earthy, strongly fungal. In a healthy plant, the tissue between bark and wood is green, yellow or cream but has no fungal structure and no mushroom smell.

The RHS describes white fungal growth between bark and wood at ground level as the most characteristic symptom for confirming honey fungus. On some plants you may also notice the bark itself bleeding a dark gum-like resin, or the wood beneath showing dark staining. These are secondary symptoms of the infection stress, not the confirmation: the white mycelium and its smell are the confirmation.

Step 4: Check for black bootlace rhizomorphs in the soil

Dig carefully into the soil beside the root zone of the affected plant to a depth of about 200mm. The RHS notes that rhizomorphs develop mostly 2.5-20cm below the soil surface in moist soil, though they can be deeper in dry soil. Use a hand trowel rather than a spade to avoid slicing and spreading root pieces.

Look for black or very dark brown, wiry, cord-like structures running through the soil. These are rhizomorphs: the spreading threads that Armillaria uses to move laterally from infected root material to healthy plant roots. They are known as bootlaces for good reason: they look like black or brown cords and are quite unlike ordinary roots.

Finding black rhizomorphs alongside the white mycelium from Step 3 is a very strong indication of Armillaria infection. If you are unsure, the RHS Gardening Advice service can help confirm a diagnosis.

Step 5: Remove infected plants and root material safely

Nesting season: the check before removing hedge plants

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest. Even a dying or dead hedge plant can harbour an active nest from 1 March to 31 August. Before removing any section of hedge, walk it slowly and check for adult birds flying repeatedly into the base or interior. If you find an active nest, leave that plant in position until the brood has fledged (typically two to three weeks from hatching), even if this delays your management work. The autumn window (September to February) avoids this constraint for most situations. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest.

Once you have confirmed no active nesting and are ready to proceed, removal must be thorough. Cutting the plant at ground level and grinding the stump is not enough: the infected root system remains in the soil as a food source for the fungus, which will continue to spread via rhizomorphs to neighbouring healthy plants. The roots need to come out.

Dig out the stump and as much of the root system as the site allows. Trace the larger roots outward and lever them free, working carefully to avoid snapping them: broken root pieces left in the soil can continue feeding the fungus.

Dispose of all infected plant and root material in household waste. Do not compost it, and do not leave it on site. The RHS says the only effective remedy is to dig out and destroy all infected root and stump material. Clean spades, forks and hand tools thoroughly before using them elsewhere in the garden, as fungal material can transfer on tools.

If you are using a stump grinder for a large infected stump, have the chippings removed from site rather than incorporated into the soil or used as mulch: infected wood chippings spread the fungus.

Step 6: Install a physical root barrier around the infection zone

Once the infected material is out, a vertical physical barrier between the cleared area and the adjacent healthy hedge plants slows the lateral spread of any rhizomorphs you were unable to remove. The RHS suggests a vertical strip of butyl rubber (pond lining) or heavy-duty plastic sheet buried about 45cm deep, protruding 2-3cm above soil level, to block the rhizomorphs. Regular deep cultivation also breaks up rhizomorphs and limits spread.

Install the barrier vertically in a trench dug between the cleared zone and the first healthy plants on each side, burying it as deep as the soil allows. Leave the top edge a few centimetres proud of the soil surface, then mulch up to it once the barrier is stable.

A physical barrier contains but does not cure the infection in the cleared zone. The affected soil can remain infected for many years, so replant only with species the RHS lists as rarely affected (see Step 7).

Step 7: Replant with species that tolerate honey fungus on CT10 chalk

Once the infected material is out and the barrier is in, you can replant the cleared section. Species choice matters here: Armillaria residue can persist in the soil for years, and replanting with a susceptible species will likely result in a repeat infection. The RHS honey fungus plant list (November 2024) puts the following hedging plants in its "rarely affected" category, and several of them also suit Thanet chalk and coastal conditions:

  • Griselinia (Griselinia littoralis): rarely affected and a salt-tolerant coastal choice, though it needs a sheltered position in hard winters.
  • Pittosporum and Olearia: both are on the RHS rarely-affected hedging list and cope well with coastal wind.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): rarely affected and long-lived. Slower to establish than privet or laurel. All parts are toxic to people and livestock, so do not plant it where animals can reach the foliage.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): rarely affected by honey fungus, but note the ongoing risk of box blight and box tree moth (see the dedicated guide on this site). Worth considering for small formal sections rather than long runs.
  • Tamarix, Lonicera nitida, Hypericum and lavender: also on the RHS rarely-affected hedging list, for informal or low hedges.
  • Beech, hornbeam, field maple and holly: the RHS puts these in the "sometimes affected" group, so they are a reasonable choice for a native hedge but not as safe as the rarely-affected plants above.
  • Rosa: also in the RHS "sometimes affected" group; the same caution applies.

Avoid replanting the infected zone with privet, Leylandii, Thuja, cotoneaster, viburnum, photinia, pyracantha, escallonia, elaeagnus, hawthorn, forsythia or lilac. The RHS lists these among the plants most frequently attacked by honey fungus, with privet, Leylandii, Thuja, cotoneaster and viburnum as top hosts. Where privet runs along a boundary, a honey fungus outbreak is a reason to diversify the species in the replanted section rather than replace like for like.

Why older gardens are at particular risk

Two characteristics of older gardens increase the likelihood of honey fungus being present in the soil:

Old orchard and garden history: Gardens built on former orchard or farmland, or where trees were felled in the past, can have buried root systems that are a long-lived food source for Armillaria. The fungus can colonise a site before the first hedge is ever planted.

Long-established boundary hedges: The longer a hedge has been growing, the greater the chance that part of its root system eventually meets an infected root or rhizomorph. Privet and laurel boundary hedges in older terraces are a typical example.

None of this means honey fungus is inevitable in CT10 gardens: the majority of established hedges here have never been affected. But when a hedge dies in a section for no obvious reason, and the usual suspects (drought stress, root damage from nearby works, laurel dieback from cold) have been ruled out, Armillaria is the most likely cause and should be investigated before replanting.

What does not work

A few interventions are sometimes suggested that do not, in practice, solve the problem in a garden context:

Fungicide treatment: There are no chemical controls for honey fungus, as the RHS confirms. Products labelled as fungicides for other diseases will not control Armillaria in the soil, and any product claiming to cure honey fungus in a garden planting is not backed by evidence.

Stump grinding without root removal: As described in Step 5, grinding a stump without removing the root system leaves the food source in place. The fungus continues from the root material and uses it to extend its rhizomorph network. Grinding alone gives no benefit over simply cutting the plant down.

Adding lime to the soil: Most garden soils on Thanet chalk are already alkaline, and raising the pH further is not a recognised control for honey fungus. Liming a chalk garden to address it is not useful and risks nutrient lock-up.

Waiting for it to clear on its own: Armillaria can remain in the soil for many years, slowly consuming infected root material and sending rhizomorphs outward. It does not clear spontaneously. The management steps above, imperfect as they are, are the only realistic interventions available to a garden owner.
