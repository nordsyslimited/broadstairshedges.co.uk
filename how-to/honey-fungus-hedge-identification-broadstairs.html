How to identify and manage honey fungus in a garden hedge: diagnosis, removal and replanting for Broadstairs CT10.

Honey fungus (Armillaria spp.) is the most common cause of unexplained, progressive hedge death in British gardens, and old CT10 gardens with decades-old boundary hedges and previous fruit trees in the soil are among the most exposed sites in Kent. The fungus advances quietly through the chalk for two to five years before a plant collapses; by the time you notice the first visible signs, it has already set itself a run. This guide sets out exactly what to look for, how to confirm the diagnosis beyond doubt, what to do about infected plants, and which species to plant on the affected site when you are ready to replant.

Steps

Step 1: Check for honey-coloured toadstools in late September and October

The most visually obvious sign of honey fungus arrives in autumn. From late September through November, clusters of toadstools appear at the base of infected plants or from stumps and buried root material nearby. The caps are 5-15cm across, honey-yellow to pale brown (occasionally darker towards the centre), with a ring or skirt on the upper part of the stem. The gills underneath the cap are cream-coloured and run slightly down the stem.

The toadstools are not always present: Armillaria does not fruit every year, and in some gardens they appear only once in every two or three seasons. Do not assume an absence of toadstools means the site is clean. But when they do appear, their presence around a failing or dead plant is a strong first signal. Photograph them before they deteriorate: the cluster arrangement, the ring on the stem and the honey-yellow colour are the three features to confirm. Do not confuse them with the sulphur tuft (Hypholoma fasciculare), which also grows in clusters on stumps but has greenish-yellow gills and a more bitter smell.

Step 2: Look year-round for the slower signs of decline

Honey fungus does not announce itself with a sudden collapse in most cases. The more typical pattern is a progressive decline over one to three growing seasons. Watch for these in any established hedge plant:

  • A plant that leafs out noticeably later in spring than its neighbours of the same species
  • Thinner, sparser growth than the sections on either side, especially noticeable in formal hedges of privet, box or Leylandii
  • Yellow or pale green foliage that does not respond to feeding or watering
  • Premature autumn colouring on deciduous plants (a beech or field maple section going orange in August rather than October)
  • Partial dieback of one side of a plant while the other side still looks healthy
  • Complete sudden death of a plant in an otherwise healthy-looking hedge, especially in summer

Any of these alone could indicate drought stress, waterlogging, root damage or another disease. The next two steps confirm or rule out honey fungus specifically.

Step 3: Do the bark peel test at the root collar

The bark peel test is the most reliable in-garden confirmation method, and it works on any woody plant. Take a sharp penknife or chisel. At the junction where the plant's main stem meets the soil surface (the root collar), scrape away the soil to expose a small area of bark and then carefully lever off a section of the outer bark, about 50mm square. Look at the wood surface and the inner face of the bark you have removed.

If honey fungus is present, you will see a white or cream-coloured, fan-shaped sheet of fungal tissue growing between the bark and the wood. It has a flat, pressed appearance (unlike the three-dimensional structure of other moulds) and gives off a very distinctive mushroom-like smell: clean, earthy, strongly fungal. In a healthy plant, the tissue between bark and wood is green, yellow or cream but has no fungal structure and no mushroom smell.

This mycelium sheet is definitive. No other garden fungus produces the same fan-shaped white growth under the bark of a woody plant at ground level. On some plants you may also notice the bark itself bleeding a dark gum-like resin, or the wood beneath showing dark staining. These are secondary symptoms of the infection stress, not the confirmation: the white mycelium and its smell are the confirmation.

Step 4: Check for black bootlace rhizomorphs in the soil

Dig carefully into the soil beside the root zone of the affected plant to a depth of 200-300mm. On Thanet chalk, 200mm often reaches solid chalk: the target depth here is the full depth of the topsoil layer. Use a hand trowel rather than a spade to avoid slicing and spreading root pieces.

Look for black or very dark brown, wiry, cord-like structures running through the soil. These are rhizomorphs: the spreading threads that Armillaria uses to move laterally from infected root material to healthy plant roots. They range from about 2mm to 5mm in diameter, feel firm and rubbery when fresh, and have a dark outer skin with white or paler interior if you slice one. Fresh rhizomorphs are often slightly flattened rather than perfectly round. They do not taper to a fine point the way roots do: the ends are blunt or rounded.

Finding black rhizomorphs alongside the white mycelium from Step 3 is a definitive diagnosis of Armillaria infection. Either sign alone is a strong indicator, but both together leaves no room for doubt.

Step 5: Remove infected plants and root material safely

Nesting season: the check before removing hedge plants

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a bird on an active nest. Even a dying or dead hedge plant can harbour an active nest from 1 March to 31 August. Before removing any section of hedge, walk it slowly and check for adult birds flying repeatedly into the base or interior. If you find an active nest, leave that plant in position until the brood has fledged (typically two to three weeks from hatching), even if this delays your management work. The autumn window (September to February) avoids this constraint for most situations. Penalty: an unlimited fine per nest, with up to six months in prison for aggravated cases.

Once you have confirmed no active nesting and are ready to proceed, removal must be thorough. Cutting the plant at ground level and grinding the stump is not enough: the infected root system remains in the soil as a food source for the fungus, which will continue to spread via rhizomorphs to neighbouring healthy plants. The roots need to come out.

Dig out the stump and as much of the root system as the site allows. On the thin Margate Chalk that underlies most CT10 gardens, the main roots typically run within the top 300mm of topsoil before meeting the chalk surface. Trace the larger roots outward and lever them free, working carefully to avoid snapping them: broken root pieces left in the soil can continue feeding the fungus. The chalk surface itself acts as a natural barrier to deep root penetration, which is one point in CT10's favour when managing this disease.

Dispose of all infected plant and root material in household waste. Do not compost it, do not leave it on site, and do not take it to a green-waste site or community composting facility. Bin bags are fine for smaller amounts. Wash spades, forks and hand tools with a 10% bleach solution (one part household bleach to nine parts water) before using them elsewhere in the garden. The fungal rhizomorphs can transfer on tools.

If you are using a stump grinder for a large infected stump, have the chippings removed from site rather than incorporated into the soil or used as mulch: infected wood chippings spread the fungus.

Step 6: Install a physical root barrier around the infection zone

Once the infected material is out, a vertical physical barrier between the cleared area and the adjacent healthy hedge plants slows the lateral spread of any rhizomorphs you were unable to remove. The barrier should be an impermeable membrane: a butyl liner (as used for garden ponds), a thick gauge polythene sheet, or a proprietary root-barrier membrane sold for this purpose. Terram-type permeable membranes do not work for this application: the rhizomorphs can penetrate them.

Install the barrier vertically in a trench dug between the cleared zone and the first healthy plants on each side. On standard UK soils the recommended depth is 500mm; on CT10 chalk soils, the chalk bedrock sits at 200-300mm in many gardens, which limits the practical installation depth. Bury the liner as deep as the chalk allows and turn the top edge slightly outward (away from the healthy plants) so that any rhizomphs tracking upward at the chalk surface are deflected away from the hedge root zone rather than being guided over the top of the barrier. Leave the top edge a few centimetres proud of the soil surface initially, then mulch over once the barrier is stable.

A physical barrier contains but does not cure the infection in the cleared zone. The affected soil can remain infected for many years. Do not replant the cleared area for at least twelve months after the barrier is installed, and then plant only with resistant species (see Step 7).

Step 7: Replant with species that tolerate honey fungus on CT10 chalk

After the twelve-month wait, you can replant the cleared section. Species choice matters here: Armillaria residue can persist in the soil for years, and replanting with a susceptible species will likely result in a repeat infection. The following species combine documented honey-fungus tolerance with good performance on Thanet chalk and coastal conditions:

  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica): one of the most tolerant common hedging species, and highly suited to chalk. On a formal boundary hedge, beech is my first recommendation when replacing a failed privet on a CT10 site.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): good honey-fungus tolerance, chalk-tolerant, and a closer visual match to formal privet hedging than beech in terms of density and clipping response.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): tolerant and long-lived on chalk. Slower to establish than privet or laurel but genuinely resistant once rooted. Do not plant near livestock or horses: all parts are toxic to most animals except birds.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): tolerant of honey fungus but note the ongoing risk of box blight and box tree moth in CT10 (see the dedicated guide on this site). Worth considering for small formal sections rather than long runs.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): good tolerance, good on chalk, useful for a native or mixed hedge context. Deciduous.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): tolerant, native, chalk-friendly and provides excellent wildlife value. Thorny: not suited to formal clipped boundaries near pavements.
  • Rosa rugosa: tolerant and very tough on chalk and salt wind. Dense, prickly, and provides late-season hips. Useful in the barrier strip itself.

Avoid replanting the infected zone with privet, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, Leylandii, cotoneaster, forsythia or viburnum. These are among the most susceptible species in UK garden conditions. In Broadstairs specifically, where Victorian-era privet runs the entire length of many terrace boundaries, a honey fungus outbreak is a reason to diversify species for the replanted section rather than replace like for like.

Why CT10 gardens are at particular risk

Three characteristics of the typical Broadstairs and St Peter's garden increase the likelihood of honey fungus being present in the soil:

Old orchard and garden history: Large parts of what is now residential St Peter's, Reading Street and inland Broadstairs were market garden, orchard and farmland until the mid-twentieth century. Old apple, pear and plum root systems buried during development are a long-lived food source for Armillaria. The fungus can colonise a site before the first hedge is ever planted.

Slow decomposition on chalk: Organic material decomposes more slowly in the alkaline, freely draining Margate Chalk than it would in clay or loam. Old root systems buried in chalk can persist as viable fungal food for many more years than the same roots would on a heavier, more acidic soil. This extends the window during which Armillaria can remain active at a site after the original infected plant is removed.

Long-established boundary hedges: The Edwardian and Victorian terraces that characterise much of Albion Road, Pierremont Avenue, St Peter's Park Road and the streets running back from Viking Bay often have privet or laurel boundary hedges that have been in continuous growth for sixty to a hundred years. These long-established hedges have developed deep root networks that over time encounter buried root material from previous garden features. The longer a hedge runs, the greater the chance that part of its root system eventually meets an infected root or rhizomorph.

None of this means honey fungus is inevitable in CT10 gardens: the majority of established hedges here have never been affected. But when a hedge dies in a section for no obvious reason, and the usual suspects (drought stress, root damage from nearby works, laurel dieback from cold) have been ruled out, Armillaria is the most likely cause and should be investigated before replanting.

What does not work

A few interventions are sometimes suggested that do not, in practice, solve the problem in a garden context:

Fungicide treatment: No fungicide is registered for amateur use against honey fungus in the UK. The RHS confirms this unambiguously. Products labelled as "systemic fungicides" for other diseases will not control Armillaria in the soil. Any product claiming to cure honey fungus in a garden planting is not backed by evidence.

Stump grinding without root removal: As described in Step 5, grinding a stump without removing the root system leaves the food source in place. The fungus continues from the root material and uses it to extend its rhizomorph network. Grinding alone gives no benefit over simply cutting the plant down.

Adding lime to the soil: The Margate Chalk underlying CT10 means that most garden soils here are already at pH 7.5 or above. Raising pH further does not inhibit Armillaria growth. Liming a chalk garden to address honey fungus is not useful and risks compounding nutrient-lockup problems from the already-alkaline soil.

Waiting for it to clear on its own: Armillaria can remain viable in soil for over twenty years while slowly consuming infected root material and sending rhizomorphs outward. It does not clear spontaneously. The management steps above, imperfect as they are, are the only realistic interventions available to a garden owner.
