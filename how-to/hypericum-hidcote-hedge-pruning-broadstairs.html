How to prune Hypericum 'Hidcote': spring timing, the annual cut and renovation for Broadstairs CT10.

Hypericum 'Hidcote' (St John's Wort) is one of the most reliably low-maintenance semi-evergreen shrubs in Broadstairs front gardens. It tolerates the thin alkaline chalk that sits under most of CT10, shrugs off moderate salt-wind exposure without the severe browning that catches Griselinia or Pittosporum in hard winters, and produces large, clear-yellow flowers from June through to October on this season's new growth. Pruning it is straightforward, but the timing matters: cut in spring before the new growth hardens and you get a full season of flowers and a compact shape; miss the spring window, let it run for a couple of years and you end up with a leggy, open plant sitting well above its intended height. Unlike lavender or Brachyglottis, Hypericum can regenerate from old wood, so hard renovation is an option when the annual cut has been skipped.

Steps

Step 1: Confirm the pruning window

The standard annual pruning window for Hypericum 'Hidcote' in CT10 is March to mid-April, once sustained frost has passed but before the new season's growth has extended beyond 50-80mm. The plant flowers on the current year's new wood, so a spring cut does not cost you flowers: within four to six weeks of the cut, new shoots emerge and go on to carry the summer flower buds. Cutting after mid-April is not harmful, but each week you delay, the new shoots lengthen and the cut line moves higher, leaving the plant slightly taller than intended by the end of the season.

The practical test for CT10 timing: once night temperatures have held consistently above 4°C for two weeks and there is no frost forecast in the following ten days, the window is open. In a typical Broadstairs season, mid-March fits this criterion. In a cold easterly spring, hold until early April. The plant will not benefit from waiting longer than mid-April under normal conditions.

Step 2: Walk the plant and check for active nests

The spring pruning window runs squarely within the bird nesting season that extends from 1 March to 31 August. Hypericum 'Hidcote' has a low, spreading habit with dense outer growth, and dunnocks and sparrows regularly nest in garden shrubs at this height. Before any work starts, walk slowly around the full perimeter, watching for adult birds repeatedly entering or leaving the same point in the canopy, and listening for quiet alarm calls or nest noise from inside the plant. Any section with an active nest must be left uncut and marked. Most garden species complete a brood within three to five weeks of hatching, after which the section can be pruned in a follow-up visit.

Hypericum pruning and the nesting window

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy a nest while it is in use or being built. The standard guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. The annual Hypericum spring cut falls within that window for any work from mid-March onward. The nest check is not optional: carry it out before every cut, however quick the job appears. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed per nest.

Step 3: Assess how hard to cut

Unlike lavender, rosemary or Brachyglottis, Hypericum 'Hidcote' can break new growth from old wood, including quite thick, woody stems. This gives you two realistic options:

  • Annual maintenance cut: take back by one-third to one-half of the current above-ground height. For a plant sitting at 1.2m, cutting back to 600-800mm is correct. This keeps the plant compact and within the typical 1 to 1.5m range the RHS describes for 'Hidcote'. The cut can be made across the top of the plant by eye with a hedge trimmer, leaving a horizontal surface with a slightly inward taper on the sides.
  • Harder renovation cut: for a plant that has been left untouched for two or more seasons and is now sitting at 1.8-2m or more, cut back to within 80-150mm of the base, to a point where thick, woody stems still carry a few visible buds or stubby lateral shoots. The plant will push strong new growth within four to six weeks and reach a presentable size again within one growing season.

For a hedge-line situation, where you want a consistent, level top at a predetermined height, the annual maintenance cut is the right approach. For a single specimen or informal grouping that has become ungainly, the harder renovation cut is worth considering.

Step 4: Choose your tool

For a shaped hedge line, a powered or cordless hedge trimmer is appropriate. The stems at the cut height on a Hypericum that receives an annual prune are typically 5-15mm in diameter, within comfortable range for a standard trimmer blade. Use a horizontal sweep across the top first to set the height, then a slightly inward-angled pass down each side.

For a single specimen, an informal grouping, or the harder renovation cut, secateurs and loppers are more practical. Secateurs handle the typical outer growth; loppers deal with older stems at the base in the 15-30mm diameter range. A pruning saw is rarely needed unless the plant has been genuinely neglected for five or more years.

Whatever tool you use: clean and sharpen blades before the job. Blunt blades tear the stems rather than cutting cleanly, leaving a bruised, browning wound face that heals more slowly and is more susceptible to die-back.

Step 5: Make the cuts

For hedge-trimmer work on a shaped hedge: set your intended height with a taut string line or a straight board if precision matters, then work the trimmer across the top in overlapping passes, keeping the blade level. Finish with the two side faces, cutting them slightly inward (the bottom of each face slightly wider than the top) so that light reaches the lower canopy. This prevents the lower stems becoming shaded-out and bare over successive years.

For secateur work on a specimen: cut each stem back to a point where it still carries a live bud or lateral shoot, 10-20mm above a bud. Cut at a slight angle to drain rainwater away from the bud. Remove any dead or hollow stems at their point of origin at the base. Where you are making the harder renovation cut, the final cut height will be close to the base and may feel severe; the plant will recover.

For the whole plant, aim to leave an even, slightly domed or flat-topped profile. Hypericum 'Hidcote' has a naturally arching habit and tends to look better given a domed outline than a hard-squared box form.

Step 6: Clear up and feed

Remove all clippings from the base of the plant. Hypericum clippings compost readily and can go on the heap or in the kerbside green bin. After clearing, spread a layer of well-rotted organic compost or bark mulch 50-80mm deep around the base of the plant, keeping it clear of the stems by 100mm to avoid collar rot. On CT10's free-draining chalk soil, mulch is the main mechanism for retaining moisture through the late-spring and summer dry periods.

Apply a balanced granular fertiliser at the manufacturer's recommended rate in April, once the new growth is visibly underway. Hypericum is not a heavy feeder, but on the lean Margate Chalk, a single spring feed makes a noticeable difference to the density and vigour of the new growth and therefore the size of the summer flower display. Chicken-pellet-based fertilisers work well on chalk; avoid high-nitrogen formulations that drive soft vegetative growth at the expense of flowering.

Why Hypericum 'Hidcote' suits Broadstairs gardens

Three characteristics make it a reliable choice for the CT10 soil and climate combination:

  • Chalk tolerance. Hypericum 'Hidcote' performs well on the thin, free-draining, alkaline Margate Chalk that underlies most of Broadstairs and St Peter's. It does not need acidic soil, resents waterlogging more than drought, and establishes without soil amendment on a typical CT10 front garden. At the other end of the pH scale, it also does well on sandy or loamy soils, so it adapts across the range of CT10 garden conditions.
  • Moderate salt-wind tolerance. 'Hidcote' is not a first-line coastal species in the way that Escallonia, Olearia or Tamarisk are, but it handles the indirect salt-wind exposure that affects gardens within 200-300m of the cliff edge reasonably well. The semi-evergreen habit means it sheds its oldest leaves in winter and re-covers in spring, limiting the cumulative scorch damage that builds up on fully evergreen species in exposed positions. Gardens directly above Stone Bay or Joss Bay are better served by one of the front-line coastal species; gardens set back from the cliff edge in Broadstairs and St Peter's are good candidates for Hypericum.
  • Drought performance. Thanet receives around 615mm of rain per year (Met Office, Manston station, 1991-2020 average), one of the lower figures in England. Established Hypericum 'Hidcote' handles this well: it is drought-tolerant once its root system has developed through the first two summers. The spring mulch described in Step 6 is particularly useful in the first two seasons before the roots have deepened into the chalk subsoil.

The RHS rates 'Hidcote' as hardiness H5 (tolerant to -15°C), which is a generous margin above a typical Broadstairs winter minimum. Even the occasional hard east-Kent frost that damages Griselinia or Pittosporum leaves Hypericum unaffected.

Pruning after flowering: the August alternative

Some UK gardeners prefer to prune Hypericum 'Hidcote' immediately after the main flush of flowering finishes in late August or early September, rather than in spring. The logic: the plant has flowered on the current season's growth, that growth is now spent, and cutting it back allows a secondary flush of growth to harden before winter while also clearing the spent flower heads.

In a Broadstairs garden, an August cut carries two complications. First, the nesting window does not close until 31 August, so a cut in the last week of August still requires a thorough nest check and may need to wait until early September. Second, cutting in late summer leaves the pruned stems exposed to the first autumn frosts without the full recovery period that a spring cut provides. On the sheltered side of a garden in St Peter's or the inland edges of Broadstairs, this risk is minor; for gardens directly exposed to easterly gales from the North Sea in October and November, the spring cut is a safer default.

Either timing works for the plant. The spring cut is a better default for Broadstairs: it aligns with the period of maximum regrowth vigour, avoids the late-nesting complications, and sets the plant up for a full summer of flowers rather than leaving the pruned stems through the winter months.

Renovation for a neglected or overgrown specimen

An unpruned Hypericum 'Hidcote' grows steadily outward and upward, reaching 1.8-2m in height and spreading well beyond its intended footprint within a few seasons. The stems become increasingly woody at the base, and the flowering zone moves progressively to the outer tips as the lower stems stop producing new buds. The plant looks open, lanky and thinly flowered.

Unlike lavender, rosemary or Brachyglottis, Hypericum will regenerate from hard cuts into old, bare wood. This makes renovation straightforward: in March, cut all stems back to within 80-150mm of the base. The cuts will look severe. Within four to six weeks, new shoots emerge from the woody base and from the soil around it. By mid-summer, the plant has a full canopy of new growth and carries its usual summer flowering display on a compact, low framework. By the following season, it is back to a presentable shape and can be managed on the annual maintenance cycle.

After a renovation cut, the spring feed and mulch described in Step 6 matter more than usual: the plant is drawing hard on its root reserves to push the recovery flush, and a balanced fertiliser in April, combined with consistent moisture through the first growing season, makes a visible difference to how quickly the new growth fills in. On the lean Margate Chalk, do not skip the mulch or the feed in the renovation year.

Common problems with Hypericum 'Hidcote' in CT10

Rust disease (orange pustules on the undersides of leaves). Hypericum rust is caused by the fungus Melampsora hypericorum and appears as small orange or yellow pustules on leaf undersides in summer and autumn. It weakens the plant and reduces flowering. The best management is a tidy annual prune that removes the affected growth and improves airflow through the canopy. Avoid overhead watering. Remove severely infected stems at the base and dispose of them in the bin rather than the compost heap.

Sparse flowering after several years without pruning. Hypericum flowers on the current season's new growth. A plant that has not been pruned progressively shifts its flowering zone outward to the new tips on longer and longer stems, while the lower sections produce fewer and fewer buds. Regular annual pruning restores the balance. A single renovation cut brings a neglected specimen back to flowering well within one growing season.

Bare or woody stems at the base. On a well-maintained plant with an annual spring cut, the base should carry reasonably dense growth. Bare stems at the base indicate either that the annual cut has been missed, that the plant has been cut too lightly at the top (failing to stimulate new basal growth), or that it is being shaded by an overhanging canopy above. If shading is the cause, reduce the competing plant; if the annual cut has been inadequate, switch to the harder maintenance cut or the full renovation approach.

Winter leaf scorch or leaf loss. 'Hidcote' is semi-evergreen. In a mild winter it holds most of its leaves; in a cold or windy winter it may shed a significant proportion of them. This is normal, not a sign of disease. The plant re-covers in spring; the spring prune removes any remaining tattered leaves and sets the new growth underway. Do not cut in response to winter leaf loss: wait for the spring window.
