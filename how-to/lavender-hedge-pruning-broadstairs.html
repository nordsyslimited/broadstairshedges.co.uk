How to prune a lavender hedge in Broadstairs: the old-wood rule, timing and chalk aftercare for CT10.

Lavender hedges look deceptively simple until the first season you cut them wrong. In Broadstairs CT10, where the Margate Chalk drains fast and the summers are the driest on the south coast of England, a lavender hedge cut too hard into old wood at the wrong time of year is gone permanently: lavender does not regenerate from brown woody stems. This guide covers the exact timing for CT10's coastal climate, the one rule you cannot break, and the aftercare that keeps a chalk-garden lavender hedge in good condition for 8 to 10 years before replanting.

Steps

Step 1: Time the cut for after peak flowering, late July to mid-August in Broadstairs CT10

English lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) in Broadstairs typically peaks from late June into July, with flowers holding until mid to late August depending on the variety and aspect. The standard UK advice is to cut after flowering finishes. In CT10, the reliable window is late July to mid-August for most established plants in a south-facing position: at that point the main flowering is visibly over, the flower spikes are browning and drying, and the plant has enough warmth in the growing season left to harden new growth before the October frosts arrive on the Thanet coast.

Do not leave the cut until September or October. Lavender cut late in a CT10 autumn has insufficient warm growing days to harden the fresh growth before the first frosts. Open cut surfaces on soft growth in October can die back, and on a plant already on thin chalk with restricted root depth, that die-back can be permanent. The late-July to mid-August window is the one to commit to: the flowers tell you when it opens, and the autumn calendar tells you when it closes.

A lighter spring tidy in April, described later in this guide, can complement but does not replace the main late-summer cut. The main cut does the structural work; the April tidy removes any winter-damaged tips and refreshes the outline.

Step 2: Check for active nesting before picking up any tools

Stand still beside the lavender for 30 to 60 seconds and watch. Lavender in a sheltered Broadstairs garden in July does attract nesting birds, particularly where the planting is dense and borders a low wall, a gravel path or a sheltered south-facing bed. Dunnocks and whitethroats are the most common species using dense lavender for ground-level or low nesting in CT10 gardens. Both are protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.

Active nest in the lavender? The legal position.

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest, or to disturb a Schedule 1 species at the nest. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest and up to six months in prison for aggravated offences. The practical caution window for CT10 is 1 March to 31 August: if you see an adult bird entering or leaving a point in the lavender repeatedly, or hear young calling, leave the plant entirely alone until you are confident the brood has fledged. Most garden bird broods fledge within 14 to 21 days of hatching. As lavender's main pruning window (late July to mid-August) falls inside the nesting season, a brief visual check before every cutting session is not optional: it takes 60 seconds and avoids a criminal penalty.

Step 3: Identify the green-grey zone, the boundary you must not cross

Crouch down beside the plant and look into the base. You will see two distinct zones. The upper zone is the current season's growth: soft, green to grey-green stems with visible leaves and the dead flower spikes from this year's flowering. Below that, the stems thicken and turn brown, grey-brown or woody: this is the old wood. This boundary is the most important feature of the whole operation.

Lavandula angustifolia does not regenerate from old wood. If you cut below the lowest leaves into the brown woody base, the portion of the stem below the cut will not produce new growth. The plant will develop a permanent bare patch or die back from that point outward. There is no recovery from a cut into the brown zone: you are removing material the plant cannot replace.

With a gloved hand, find the lowest green leaf on each stem. That point, approximately 3 to 5 cm above the last leaf pair, is the floor of your working zone. Everything above it is available to cut; everything below it is not. On a well-maintained lavender the green zone is 15 to 20 cm deep. On a plant that has been cut correctly for several years it may be 25 cm or more. On a plant that has been neglected or cut too hard in previous seasons, the green zone may be thin and patchy: the renovation question is addressed at the end of this guide.

Step 4: Make the post-flowering cut, shaping each plant to a dome

Use a pair of sharp, single-action hedge shears for the main shaping work. A pair of clean bypass secateurs handles any individual stem that the shears cannot finish cleanly, or any dead-wood removal inside the canopy. Electric hedge trimmers on a compact lavender hedge apply too much speed and tend to tear the softer stems rather than shear them; hand shears on a domestic lavender hedge give a cleaner finish and let you feel when you are approaching the old-wood boundary.

Working along the hedge, cut back the current season's flowering growth and 5 to 10 cm of the soft leafy stems below the spent flowers, shaping the top of each plant to a rounded dome. The dome form is not decorative: it sheds water away from the centre of the plant and prevents moisture pooling in the crown, which on a chalk soil in wet autumn spells (Broadstairs CT10 receives about 612 mm of rain annually at Manston, concentrated in October to February) causes crown rot in compact varieties.

Aim to remove no more than one-third of the total plant height in a single cut. For a typical mature 'Hidcote' plant at 40 to 50 cm in height, that means removing 12 to 15 cm from the top. The objective is a compact, well-clothed dome, not a severe reduction. You are maintaining shape and removing tired growth, not renovating a neglected specimen.

Step back every few passes and check the profile. A lavender hedge looks best with a consistent dome height along its length. Mark a guide height if you find it difficult to judge by eye: a cane at the target height at each end of the row works well.

Step 5: Remove dead flower spikes from the sides and base

After the main top cut, work along the sides of each plant with the shears, removing dead flower spikes that are growing horizontally or downward from the sides of the plant. These are the remnants of the current season's lateral flowering growth. Left in place over winter, they hold moisture against the stems and can encourage fungal issues in the relatively damp CT10 autumn and early winter.

Check the base of each plant at this point. If there are any entirely dead or brown stems at ground level, cut them out with secateurs flush with the base. Dead stems in the centre of a lavender plant are a normal part of the ageing process and should be removed each year; leaving them encourages the plant to develop bare patches in the centre of the dome.

Do not attempt to feed or thin the interior of a well-maintained lavender. Unlike box or yew, lavender does not need interior air-circulation work. The whole crown is effectively one growing zone: thin it and you create gaps that the plant cannot fill from old wood.

Step 6: Aftercare on Margate Chalk, water, feed sparingly and mulch correctly

Water each plant in immediately after pruning: 5 to 8 litres per compact plant, 10 litres for a larger established specimen. The Margate Chalk formation under most Broadstairs and St Peter's gardens is fast-draining by nature: thin topsoil over pure chalk means the effective water-holding layer is only 15 to 30 cm deep on most CT10 sites. A recently pruned lavender on that substrate needs an immediate water-in to support the recovery flush, even in a year with reasonable rainfall.

Feeding lavender on chalk requires restraint. Lavender evolved on the thin, nutrient-poor soils of the Mediterranean coast and performs best on low fertility. A high-nitrogen feed produces lush, soft growth that is more prone to frost damage and less aromatic than growth produced on lean soil. On the alkaline Margate Chalk, which naturally provides the high-pH, free-draining conditions lavender prefers, the most useful post-pruning feed is a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser at half the label rate, applied once in late spring. Do not feed after the summer cut: feeding in August or September encourages soft late-season growth that does not harden before the October frosts.

Mulching lavender on chalk: use a 40 to 50 mm layer of fine horticultural grit or gravel around each plant rather than bark mulch. Bark mulch retains moisture against the crown and stems in a way that suits the heavier soils for which it was designed; on a CT10 chalk site, a damp bark collar in October is an invitation to crown rot. Grit or gravel mulch suppresses weeds, reduces splash-back of soil onto the lower foliage, and allows the sharp drainage that lavender needs. In practice, many Broadstairs gardens with lavender edging already have a gravel or pea shingle path surface, which performs the same function naturally.

Why lavender performs well in Broadstairs CT10

Three conditions in CT10 align closely with lavender's native habitat: a Mediterranean subshrub that evolved on thin, alkaline, fast-draining soils in full sun, with dry summers and moderate winters.

First, the Margate Chalk. The Upper Chalk formation that underlies most of Broadstairs, the clifftop terraces above Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate, and the Reading Street plateau is essentially what lavender is adapted to: alkaline pH (typically 7.5 to 8.0 at 10 cm depth on a chalk site), near-zero waterlogging risk in normal seasons, and very low organic-matter content in the upper layers. Most lavender failures in UK gardens happen on acidic, clay-heavy or badly-drained soils. CT10's chalk removes those risks entirely.

Second, rainfall. Manston station records approximately 612 mm of annual rainfall, making Thanet one of the driest areas in England. That compares to 700 to 800 mm in much of the Southeast Midlands and over 1000 mm on the South Devon coast. Lavender performs and lives longest where annual rainfall is low: the main disease risk (Phytophthora crown rot and grey mould / botrytis on wet stems) tracks closely with wet winters and high humidity, neither of which characterises a typical CT10 year.

Third, sun exposure. Broadstairs south-facing slopes, the chalk escarpments above Viking Bay, and the walled front gardens of the Broadstairs conservation areas receive strong direct sun from late March through October. Lavender needs full sun for maximum flower production and oil concentration. Shaded plants flower poorly, lose their compact form and become prone to the grey mould that shortens a plant's effective life from 8 to 10 years to 3 to 4.

Varieties suited to Broadstairs CT10

Lavandula angustifolia 'Hidcote' is the most widely planted lavender in CT10 gardens and the most reliable choice for a formal low hedge or path edging. It holds an RHS Award of Garden Merit, reaches 40 to 60 cm in a maintained hedge, produces deep violet-blue flowers from late June, and is frost-hardy to approximately −15°C: well within range for Thanet winters, which rarely drop below −5°C at coastal-plain level even in a hard winter. 'Hidcote' responds well to the annual post-flowering cut and with correct pruning maintains its compact dome form for 8 to 10 years.

'Munstead' is a slightly more open-growing variety with pale purple-blue flowers, also AGM-rated, and fractionally more compact at 30 to 50 cm in height. It flowers a few days earlier than 'Hidcote' in the same conditions. Both are equally suitable for CT10 conditions; the choice between them is aesthetic rather than horticultural.

Avoid French lavender (L. stoechas, the 'rabbit ears' form) for an outdoor hedge in Broadstairs. It flowers earlier and more prolifically but is rated frost-hardy only to −10°C under ideal conditions, which means it can fail in a hard winter on an exposed chalk site above the clifftop terraces. Planted as a seasonal display or in a walled front garden with south-west aspect it survives most CT10 winters, but it is a risk on a north-facing or exposed site. L. angustifolia is the dependable hedge choice for this coast.

The spring tidy in April: a complement to the main late-summer cut

Some gardeners run a light second pass in April, before flowering begins. This is optional, not essential. Its purpose is to remove any winter-damaged tips (identifiable as brown, soft or shrivelled growth that appeared after the October frosts), to restore the dome profile if the winter has caused any asymmetric growth, and to give the hedge a neat appearance through the spring before the flower spikes emerge in June.

The April tidy should be light: remove only the visibly damaged or untidy tips, taking no more than 3 to 5 cm. Do not use this pass to compensate for an inadequate main cut the previous August. Making a structural reduction in April, removing hard old wood to try to open up the plant, risks cutting into the old-wood zone with none of the warmth that helps the plant recover. The April tidy is finishing work; the August cut is the structural operation.

When lavender becomes woody: the renovation question

A lavender that has been cut too lightly for several years, or not cut at all, develops into a sprawling, woody specimen with a thin green zone sitting on top of a large brown woody base. The characteristic appearance is: tall, open, largely flowerless stems; bare brown wood visible at the base; a silhouette that bears no resemblance to the compact dome of a well-maintained plant.

The honest answer for most woody lavender in CT10: if more than half the plant's height is brown old wood with no green growth from it, the plant is past renovation. Lavender does not regenerate from old brown stems, no matter how the cut is framed. The correct response is to remove it, improve the drainage if needed (a 10 cm layer of horticultural grit worked into the planting hole is worthwhile on any CT10 chalk site where topsoil depth is less than 20 cm), and replant with young pot-grown or bare-rooted stock from a reliable UK nursery. Young plants establish quickly on chalk in a warm CT10 spring and will be flowering well within one season.

If the woody base still has some green growth emerging directly from old wood at a few points, there is a limited renovation option: cut back to just above those basal green shoots in April, removing the dead and thin stems entirely, and give the remaining plant the best possible chance with a water-in and a balanced slow-release feed. Success is not guaranteed: on a thin chalk soil with restricted root depth, the shock of a hard renovation cut on an already-weakened lavender produces a 50/50 outcome at best. If the plant does not produce vigorous new growth from the cut points by June, replace it.
