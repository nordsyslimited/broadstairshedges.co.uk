How to prune a myrobalan (cherry plum) hedge: the summer-only rule, silver leaf risk and notes for Broadstairs CT10.

Myrobalan is cherry plum: Prunus cerasifera. It is one of the most widely planted hedging species in Kent farmyards, cottage gardens and the semi-rural roads around Reading Street and St Peter's. It makes a fast, dense, bird-rich boundary: white blossom as early as February, small yellow-red fruits in late summer, and a tangle of stems that blackbirds, house sparrows and goldfinches work all year. Its pruning rule is counter-intuitive and important: cut it in summer, not winter. The reason is silver leaf, a fungal disease of Prunus species that enters through wounds, and whose spores disperse most freely from infected wood in the autumn and winter months. Cut your cherry plum hedge in December and you expose every wound to peak spore load for the following three months. Cut it in July and the wounds heal quickly in the growing season, spore levels in the air are at their annual low, and the hedge shrugs it off. This guide gives the step-by-step method, the nesting-season check that must come before any summer cut, and the specific renovation approach for overgrown Prunus cerasifera boundaries.

Steps

Step 1: Confirm the species before you decide on timing

The summer-only rule applies to all Prunus species susceptible to silver leaf: cherry plum, blackthorn, hawthorn and other Crataegus are less affected than cherry and plum species, but the common rule of thumb for the whole genus in a hedge context is to avoid winter pruning if you can help it. For cherry plum specifically, the case for summer pruning is strongest and the guidance most consistent.

Identification features of Prunus cerasifera:

  • Green-leaved type: the standard myrobalan used as hedging in Kent. Mid-green, oval, slightly toothed leaves, 4-6cm long. White blossom, often appearing in February before the leaves, is the strongest identification feature. Small round fruits, 2-3cm, turning from green to yellow, then flushed red when ripe in August or September.
  • Purple-leaved type ('Pissardii', 'Nigra' and similar): widely planted as an ornamental boundary in Broadstairs and St Peter's streets, particularly in Edwardian terrace gardens. Deep burgundy-purple leaves throughout the growing season, pale pink-white blossom. Often mistaken for a different species; it is still Prunus cerasifera and follows the same summer pruning rule.
  • Not blackthorn: blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) has smaller, darker leaves, produces sloe berries rather than cherry plums, and carries sharp thorns. Both are Prunus species; both benefit from summer rather than winter cuts in terms of silver leaf exposure. The blackthorn guide covers its specific management.

If you are genuinely unsure whether the hedge is cherry plum or another species, observe it in February: white blossom on bare branches before leaf break, in a dense hedge, in a Kent or Thanet garden, is almost certainly Prunus cerasifera.

Step 2: Plan the timing window: late May to late July

The sweet spot for annual maintenance of a cherry plum hedge is late June to mid-July. This balances three competing factors:

  • Silver leaf spore risk: the fungal pathogen Chondrostereum purpureum releases spores mainly from autumn through winter, peaking in damp conditions from October to February. By late June the spore load in the air is low and pruning wounds sealed by active growth.
  • Wound healing speed: in full leaf growth (June to August) the plant closes pruning wounds faster than at any other time of year. The Royal Horticultural Society advises summer pruning for established plums specifically for this reason, noting that cuts heal quickly in the growing season and that this is the most important factor in limiting silver leaf infection.
  • Nesting season (see Step 3): the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting guidance window runs 1 March to 31 August. Any cut in June or July falls inside this window and requires a nest check first, every time, without exception.

Autumn cuts (September, October) are a middle ground that many gardeners fall into because hedge-cutting season feels like it starts again in autumn. For cherry plum, September onwards sees spore loads rising again and wound healing slowing. The RHS specifically names mid-summer as the preferred timing for plum pruning. Avoid autumn cuts where you have any choice in the matter.

Winter cuts (November to February) carry the highest risk. This is not theoretical: silver leaf is a notifiable problem on commercial plum orchards in Kent, and it enters overwhelmingly through winter pruning wounds. A hedge cut in January that then shows silver leaf the following spring requires an entire infected branch removed back to live, unstained wood and disposed of off-site. That is a substantially bigger job than keeping to the summer window in the first place.

Summer pruning, nesting season: how the two overlap for cherry plum

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy a nest in use, or to disturb a Schedule 1 species at or near its nest. The standard guidance window is 1 March to 31 August. Cherry plum hedges, with their early blossom and dense, thorny interior, are used by blackbirds, house sparrows, dunnocks and goldfinches as nesting sites. The complication is that the entire recommended pruning window for cherry plum, late May to late July, sits inside the nesting season. The practical answer is not to avoid the cut, but to carry out the nest check described in Step 3 before every cut, every year, however routine the job feels. On summary conviction for disturbing an active nest, the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed per nest.

Step 3: Carry out a full nest check before cutting

Walk slowly along the full length of the hedge. Watch for adult birds flying in and out of the same section repeatedly. Listen for faint chick calls or adult alarm notes from inside the canopy. Cherry plum hedges often carry dense growth from previous seasons that provides multiple concealed nesting sites, and the nesting birds choose the most sheltered sections: south-facing aspects, areas sheltered from the prevailing south-west wind, and sections near a wall or fence.

If you find an active nest with eggs or chicks, mark that section and leave it uncut. Return after the brood has fledged, typically three to five weeks from hatching for most garden species. The annual cut does not need to happen in a single day: it is legitimate and practical to cut all confirmed-clear sections in the first pass, then return to the skipped sections once they are confirmed clear.

A nest check on a typical Broadstairs or St Peter's residential hedge takes five to ten minutes. It cannot be delegated to memory or habit: the check must happen immediately before the cut, because a hedge checked as clear two weeks earlier may have been colonised in the intervening period.

Step 4: Sterilise cutting tools before you start

Silver leaf enters Prunus through wounds. Contaminated tools can carry Chondrostereum purpureum spores from an already-infected plant to a healthy one, or from one section of a hedge that has begun to show infection to sections that have not.

Before cutting a cherry plum hedge, clean all cutting blades with a 10% bleach solution (100ml household bleach diluted in 1 litre of water) or a commercially available horticultural disinfectant such as Jeyes Fluid diluted as directed. Wipe the blades, allow them to dry for 30 seconds, and then cut. If you move from a Prunus section of a hedge to a non-Prunus section in the same run, cleaning between sections is not needed; if you move from a hedge you suspect may already show silver leaf symptoms to a healthy hedge, clean before you start the second hedge.

This is not an excessive precaution: Orchardists and tree surgeons working with cherries and plums in Kent follow the same procedure routinely. The 30 seconds it takes is a small cost compared with losing a hedge section to silver leaf.

Step 5: Prune to shape

Cherry plum grown as a formal or semi-formal boundary hedge responds well to an annual maintenance trim removing the current season's extension growth. The plant breaks freely from younger wood, so there is no need to cut back to old bare stem for a maintenance cut. Take the following approach:

  • Sides first, top last. Cut the side faces before the top, working from the base upward. This keeps cut material falling clear of sections already finished and lets you check the face angle as you work along.
  • Aim to remove about one-third of the current season's new growth. Cherry plum puts on 60-90cm of new growth per year in a well-established hedge. Removing one-third of that growth in an annual summer trim maintains the hedge at a managed size and encourages dense, bushy regrowth rather than the long, whippy leaders that develop on an uncut boundary.
  • Do not cut back into old, heavily barked wood unless you intend a renovation cut. Prunus cerasifera will produce new shoots from old wood when cut hard, but old-wood cuts are larger wounds and carry a higher silver leaf exposure than cuts into younger wood. For an annual maintenance trim, stay in the current and previous season's growth.
  • Top with a string line on formal hedges. Drive a cane at each end of the run, stretch a taut string line at the target height, and cut to it. Without a string line, following the existing uneven profile by eye locks the irregularity in rather than correcting it.
  • Use clean, sharp secateurs for any stem over about 10mm in diameter. Tearing or crushing a large-diameter stem leaves a ragged wound that is slower to seal and provides a larger entry surface for silver leaf than a clean angled cut made with a sharp blade.

Step 6: Dispose of clippings away from other Prunus plants

Cherry plum clippings from a healthy hedge can be composted normally: they carry no particular disease risk if the hedge is healthy. If there is any visible sign of silver leaf infection on some stems (see the section below on recognising silver leaf), those infected stems and their clippings should be bagged in a sealed bag and taken to the household waste site in Margate or Ramsgate rather than composted on-site. Silver leaf persists in dead or dying woody tissue and can infect new wounds from infected debris on the ground.

Do not place infected clippings near other Prunus plants or under cherry, plum, or damson trees. The spores in infected, damp dead wood can disperse and infect nearby planting through natural wound sites such as broken branches or bark damage.

Recognising and responding to silver leaf

Silver leaf shows as a silvery, metallic discolouration on the upper surface of leaves on affected stems. The silver sheen is caused by the fungus separating the leaf's upper cell layers slightly, creating a narrow air gap that reflects light differently. It is not a surface coating that wipes off: the silvering is structural.

To confirm silver leaf rather than the similar-looking condition known as false silver leaf: cut a suspect stem and examine the cross-section of the wood. True silver leaf shows a brown or purple staining in the wood beneath the bark, usually a narrow central zone in the early stages, widening as the infection progresses. False silver leaf, which is a physiological response to stress rather than a fungal infection, produces silvered leaves without any wood staining. If the wood is unstained, the problem is likely stress (drought, nutrient deficiency or root damage) rather than silver leaf fungus.

If true silver leaf is confirmed: cut out all affected wood back to clear, unstained wood, confirmed by cutting progressive sections until the stain disappears. Make the final cut at least 150mm below the last visible stain. Bag and remove all infected material from the site. Sterilise tools immediately after finishing. The RHS notes that lightly infected hedges can recover following prompt removal of infected wood combined with the switch to summer pruning; heavily infected hedges where the entire internal structure is stained may not be recoverable and require removal and replacement.

Renovation cuts for an overgrown cherry plum hedge

An unmanaged cherry plum hedge grows strongly, regularly reaching 3-4m with a base of heavy, barked stems over 50mm in diameter. If the hedge has been ignored for several years or has never been cut since planting, it will need a renovation cut to bring it back to a manageable size. This is a more substantial job than the annual maintenance trim, and the silver leaf risk is proportionally higher because renovation cuts inevitably go into older, larger-diameter wood.

Carry out renovation cuts in the summer window, ideally late June. Have sterilised loppers and a pruning saw available alongside the hedge trimmer. Where possible, cut large stems to a node or fork junction rather than mid-stem: a cut at a junction heals faster and leaves a less exposed wound face than a mid-stem cut.

Unlike yew, which cannot be cut hard and left to regenerate from bare wood, cherry plum breaks freely from old wood after a hard renovation cut. It will produce multiple new shoots from old stem tissue within the same growing season. The hedge will look sparse immediately after a hard renovation cut; by the following spring the regrowth is typically vigorous enough to restore a reasonable appearance.

Renovation need not be done all at once. If the hedge is very wide, reducing one face at a time over two consecutive summers reduces the total wound area in any single season and limits silver leaf exposure. Cut the more heavily infected side, if there is one, first: opening the canopy to light and air generally improves the health of the remaining growth, and the regrowth on the first side provides partial screening while the second side is cut the following summer.

Myrobalan hedges in the Broadstairs and Thanet landscape

Prunus cerasifera arrived in the UK as a garden and farm hedging plant in the 19th century, valued for its fast establishment on calcareous soils and its early-season blossom. It naturalized readily in the warmer, drier parts of the UK, and Kent is one of its strongest strongholds outside cultivation.

In CT10 specifically, the green-leaved myrobalan is most common in the semi-rural hedgerows around Reading Street, the North Foreland roadlines and the larger gardens of St Peter's. The purple-leaved cultivars, 'Pissardii' and 'Nigra', are more common in the Victorian and Edwardian residential streets behind Viking Bay and in the conservation area of Broadstairs itself, where they were widely planted as ornamental boundary hedges in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

On the Margate Chalk formation that underlies much of CT10, the fast-draining, alkaline soil (pH 7.5-8.0, topsoil typically 200-300mm over chalk) suits Prunus cerasifera well. It is more tolerant of dry, alkaline conditions than hawthorn and significantly more tolerant than beech or hornbeam. The annual rainfall of approximately 615mm across Thanet, one of the drier parts of England, is not a constraint for established myrobalan: the deep, vigorous root system accesses soil moisture below the chalk zone. Newly planted bare-root myrobalan on chalk does need watering through the first summer, as the root system is not yet established enough to reach below the drying chalk topsoil.

The conservation area rules (Broadstairs CA, St Peter's CA, Reading Street CA, Kingsgate CA) apply to trees and hedges, but routine pruning of a cherry plum hedge does not normally trigger the section 211 notice requirement under the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The 75mm-at-1.5m-height threshold applies to individual trees; hedge management is not subject to the same notice process unless the hedge contains protected trees with individual preservation orders. The conservation areas guide covers this in more detail.
