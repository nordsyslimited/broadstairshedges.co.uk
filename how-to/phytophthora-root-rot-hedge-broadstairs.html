How to identify and treat Phytophthora root rot in a garden hedge: seven steps for Broadstairs CT10.

Phytophthora root rot is one of the most common reasons a garden hedge dies without obvious cause in CT10. After the exceptionally wet winter of 2023 to 2024, I dealt with more Phytophthora damage in Broadstairs and St Peter's gardens than in any previous season: cherry laurel sections collapsing, Portuguese laurel going brown from the base upward, griselinia wilting despite adequate rainfall. The disease is frequently mistaken for drought stress, bad pruning or simple old age, because the damage that is visible above ground does not look like a root problem. There is no chemical cure available to gardeners or contractors once the disease is established in the soil. The only effective response is correct diagnosis, removal of infected material, improvement of the drainage conditions that caused the problem, and replanting with a resistant species. This guide takes you through each of those steps in order.

Steps

Step 1: Identify the above-ground warning signs before they become obvious

Phytophthora root rot is caused by Phytophthora species, which are water moulds rather than true fungi. Their zoospores travel through waterlogged soil, infect feeder roots, and move into the main root system and stem base. By the time the foliage shows damage, the root system below is already significantly compromised. This is why the disease catches gardeners out: the visual signal arrives late.

The characteristic above-ground sequence is: unexplained wilting during a period of adequate or high rainfall (the plant cannot take up water because its roots are dying), followed by yellowing or pale foliage, then progressive browning from the shoot tips inward, then dieback of whole shoots, and eventually the death of individual plants or whole sections. The wilting-despite-moisture pattern is the most reliable early warning. If a section of your cherry laurel or griselinia hedge is wilting in October or November after a wet month, and the soil is clearly moist, that is a Phytophthora flag worth investigating immediately.

Secondary signs include a general thinning of the canopy over a season or two, a hedge section that simply fails to put on new growth in spring when neighbouring sections grow normally, and a gradual inward die-off where the outer face looks acceptable but the interior is thinning. On cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel, look for yellowing of the lower leaves first: the disease typically moves upward from the root collar.

At this stage, do not water more heavily or apply fertiliser. Additional watering makes the conditions worse and feeds the pathogen; fertiliser on a plant with a failing root system does nothing useful. The next step is to investigate the root collar.

Step 2: Confirm the diagnosis with the collar-scratch test

The collar-scratch test is the simplest way to distinguish Phytophthora root rot from drought stress, waterlogging without disease, or other causes of dieback. You need a sharp penknife or clean secateurs, and thick gloves if the hedge is a spiny species.

At the base of an affected plant, just above and just below the soil line, make a shallow scrape through the outer bark to expose the cambium layer, which is the green or white tissue immediately beneath the bark surface. On a healthy plant, this layer is creamy white, pale green or bright white. On a plant infected with Phytophthora, the cambium shows a distinctive dark brown, orange-brown or rust-coloured discolouration, often with a water-soaked or greasy appearance. This colouration may extend up and down the stem base in streaks. In more advanced cases the bark at the collar comes away easily, is soft, and has a rotted or sour smell.

Compare an affected plant with a healthy plant of the same species in the same hedge. The colour difference between healthy and infected cambium is usually immediately clear. If the collar of a wilting plant shows the dark discolouration described, Phytophthora root rot is the most probable diagnosis. If the collar looks healthy and white, the cause of the dieback is more likely to be waterlogging alone, drought stress, or another cause: consult the honey fungus guide for one important alternative to rule out.

Be consistent with hygiene: wipe the blade of your knife between plants with a cloth dampened with horticultural disinfectant or household bleach solution (1 part bleach to 10 parts water). Phytophthora zoospores travel on tools, soil and footwear. Carry out the scratch test on all plants in an affected section, working outward until you find plants with healthy collars. This tells you the extent of the infection before you decide on removal.

Step 3: Assess how far the infection has spread through the hedge

Work along the hedge methodically, testing every plant in the affected section and two or three plants beyond the last visually symptomatic one. Mark each plant as you test: I use a slip of coloured flagging tape tied to a shoot of each infected plant, removed when the plant is taken out. This is worth doing before removal because the full picture is usually larger than the visible damage suggests.

In a single-species hedge such as a continuous cherry laurel or leylandii run, the disease often spreads along a section where drainage is poor, taking out four or five consecutive plants while plants beyond the drainage problem are unaffected. In a mixed native hedge, the spread pattern follows which species are susceptible: hawthorn and field maple plants in the same run as infected viburnum may remain healthy, because those species have good resistance to most Phytophthora strains common in SE England.

Record roughly which direction the collapse is moving: uphill or downhill along the hedge line, or toward a low-lying corner of the garden. This tells you something about where the drainage problem sits. A run of three plants dying at the lowest point of a slope, with healthy plants above, is a classic drainage hollow problem. A whole section in a flat part of the garden where water stands after rain is a broader drainage issue that will require more substantial treatment before replanting.

Step 4: Remove infected plants, roots and surrounding soil

Once you have identified all infected plants, remove them, their root systems and the soil immediately surrounding the roots. This is not optional: Phytophthora survives in dead root tissue and in the soil for several years. Planting a new hedge into uncleared infected soil will repeat the cycle, often faster, because the new plants are young and their root systems are small.

Remove the whole root ball of each infected plant as completely as practicable. For cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, and leylandii, the main lateral roots can extend 50 to 75 centimetres beyond the stem base in an established hedge. Dig to the depth of the main roots and remove as much as you can. If the root system is completely rotted, the removal is straightforward; if the plant is at an earlier stage of infection with live root still present, work carefully to get the whole system out.

Remove the soil to a depth of around 30 centimetres and to a 50 centimetre radius around each infected plant, replacing it with fresh uninfected topsoil from outside the garden or from a part of the garden with no history of Phytophthora. Do not reuse soil removed from around infected plants elsewhere in the garden. Bag it in heavy-gauge sacks for disposal via Kent County Council bulky waste collection or take it to the recycling centre in Margate (the Dane Valley tip, booking required via the KCC website). Do not add it to a home compost bin.

Dispose of infected plant material (stems, roots, leaves) in the council green waste bin or take it to the tip. Do not compost it. Do not chip it and use it as garden mulch: the pathogen can survive in wood chip from infected material.

After removal, disinfect your spade, fork and any other tools that contacted the infected root zone. A dilute bleach solution (1 part household bleach to 10 parts water) on the blade surfaces is adequate. Leave tools to air-dry rather than rinsing with tap water immediately after disinfection.

Nest check before clearing infected hedge sections

Even a dying or dead hedge section can contain an active bird nest, particularly if the outer foliage is still present and the structural stems are intact. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest. The main nesting guidance window is 1 March to 31 August, though late broods in some species continue through September in sheltered sites. Clearance of an infected section in winter (October to late February) sits outside the main nesting window and is the safest timing: Phytophthora root rot is typically diagnosed and confirmed in autumn and winter when drainage problems are most visible, so this timing usually aligns naturally. If the collapse and removal are happening between March and August, walk the full length of the section slowly before cutting anything, part the foliage and check the structural stems and interior, and look for adult birds repeatedly entering or leaving. The penalty for damaging an active nest is an unlimited fine per nest and up to six months in custody.

Step 5: Improve drainage before you replant

Improving drainage is the most important step, and the one most often skipped. Replanting a resistant species into soil with the same drainage problem that caused the original outbreak is a partial solution at best: Phytophthora will establish more slowly in a resistant species than in a susceptible one, but persistent waterlogging weakens any plant's root defences over time, and new, more virulent strains can be introduced.

For a drainage hollow in a garden in Broadstairs, St Peter's or Reading Street, the first option is to dig the planting trench deeper than usual and backfill the base with 15 to 20 centimetres of coarse gravel or pea gravel before adding topsoil. This creates a drainage layer that moves water through and away from the root zone. On flat sites where the water table itself is high after rain, a sump or French drain running away from the hedge line to a lower point in the garden or to a soakaway will manage the problem more effectively than gravel alone.

On sloped sites where water is running down the slope and pooling along the hedge line, an interceptor drain cut across the slope above the hedge position, running to a soakaway on each side, will divert the flow. This is the most common drainage fix in the sloping back gardens of the terrace houses above Viking Bay and in the lower roads of St Peter's, where water runs off the chalk ridge above and collects along boundary hedge lines.

Allow at least six to eight weeks after improving drainage and replacing the soil before replanting. Where the original infection was severe, waiting a full growing season (plant in late autumn, improve drainage, replant the following autumn) reduces the residual pathogen load in the soil. Phytophthora cannot survive indefinitely without a host plant: a season without susceptible root tissue reduces but does not eliminate the soil population.

Step 6: Choose a resistant species for the replacement planting

Not all hedging species carry equal risk. This matters for Broadstairs and CT10 specifically because cherry laurel is by far the most commonly planted boundary hedge in the area, and it is also one of the most susceptible to Phytophthora. Portuguese laurel, which has been replacing cherry laurel in many gardens over the past decade, is similarly susceptible. Where Phytophthora has killed a cherry laurel or Portuguese laurel section, replanting with the same species is a poor choice even after drainage improvement and soil replacement: the residual pathogen load, the site history, and the ongoing drainage characteristics all favour a recurrence.

For a full-replacement planting after Phytophthora damage in CT10, these are my preferred resistant or tolerant alternatives:

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): the most reliably Phytophthora-resistant formal hedging species available. Holds dead leaves through winter (similar to beech), good on chalk, tolerates the clay-chalk interface soils found in many Broadstairs gardens, and is well suited to both sun and partial shade. The correct choice if you want a close substitute for the density and formality of cherry laurel without the susceptibility.

Field maple (Acer campestre): UK native, good resistance, very well suited to chalk soils, tolerates exposed positions. A softer, more naturalistic hedge than hornbeam. Loses leaves in winter but provides excellent wildlife value and is a traditional hedgerow species across Kent.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium): good resistance, evergreen, appropriate for CT10 chalk soils with adequate organic matter at planting, and excellent for the conservation area gardens of Broadstairs and St Peter's where a dense evergreen boundary is needed. Slower to establish than cherry laurel but far more durable in the long term. See the holly hedge pruning guide for the annual cut timing.

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): very good resistance, UK native, excellent wildlife value, well suited to Margate Chalk, and appropriate for less formal boundaries. Forms a dense impenetrable barrier if trimmed annually. Bare in winter but provides spring blossom and autumn berries for birds.

Escallonia: for the coastal positions on the clifftop bays at Stone Bay, Joss Bay, Kingsgate and North Foreland, escallonia offers moderate Phytophthora resistance alongside genuine salt-wind tolerance. It is not the natural first choice as a Phytophthora replacement in a sheltered inland position, but for the front-line clifftop sites where cherry laurel struggles anyway, it makes sense as a double fix: resistant to both salt wind and root rot.

Species to avoid as replacements in a site with a Phytophthora history: cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, beech (Fagus sylvatica, moderately susceptible), griselinia (susceptible), leylandii and other conifers on wet sites, viburnum tinus. These will perform adequately if drainage has been genuinely fixed, but carry a higher residual risk.

Step 7: Monitor and prevent future outbreaks

After replanting, check the new hedge in autumn of each year for the early warning signs in Step 1: unexplained wilting despite moisture, tip dieback on healthy-looking plants, one plant in a section performing noticeably worse than its neighbours. Early diagnosis and removal of a single infected plant in a Phytophthora outbreak costs little. Finding the problem two seasons later when it has spread to twelve consecutive plants is a much larger job.

Watering discipline matters. Phytophthora zoospores are motile in water and travel through the soil with irrigation water. For a new hedge in CT10, water deeply but infrequently during the first summer to encourage deep root development, and always allow the soil surface to dry out between waterings. Avoid standing water around the stem bases. A bark mulch, 60 to 75 millimetres deep, applied 10 to 15 centimetres clear of each stem, retains moisture without creating the waterlogged collar conditions the pathogen requires.

When buying replacement plants, buy from a reputable UK nursery with Phytophthora-aware handling practices. Phytophthora can be introduced into a garden on the root system of a purchased plant. Signs of an infected nursery plant include yellowing leaves, dieback of shoot tips, or softness or discolouration at the stem base. Inspect the root ball before planting: white, firm roots are healthy; brown, soft or musty roots are not. Return any suspect plant to the supplier before it goes into the ground.

Tool hygiene is worth maintaining as a habit. If a neighbour's hedge along the same boundary has an unexplained collapse, do not share tools without cleaning them first. Phytophthora in a neighbouring garden is a risk to your own hedge once it reaches the soil at the shared boundary.

Which hedge species are most susceptible in CT10 gardens

The RHS publishes a Phytophthora host list that rates UK garden plants by susceptibility. For the species most commonly used in Broadstairs and CT10 boundary hedging, the picture is as follows.

High susceptibility: cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus), Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica), rhododendron, viburnum (most species including V. tinus, the most widely planted ornamental hedging viburnum in CT10), Lawson cypress (Chamaecyparis lawsoniana). Moderate susceptibility: griselinia (Griselinia littoralis, one of the most popular coastal hedging plants in Broadstairs), pittosporum, beech (Fagus sylvatica), leylandii (Cupressocyparis leylandii). Generally resistant: hornbeam, field maple, hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, holly, escallonia.

These categories explain the pattern I see in Broadstairs gardens after a wet winter: a long run of cherry laurel or Portuguese laurel will show a cluster of dying plants in the lowest, wettest section of the hedge, while a hornbeam or hawthorn hedge on the same side of the same road is entirely unaffected. The species choice determines the susceptibility; the drainage conditions determine whether the susceptibility becomes a problem.

Soil and drainage in CT10: why Phytophthora is a greater risk than many gardeners expect

Broadstairs and most of St Peter's sit on the Margate Chalk Formation. Chalk drains freely and would seem to be an unlikely environment for a water-mould disease. The complication is that many CT10 gardens have a chalk subsoil overlain by a layer of clay-rich made ground or head deposits, particularly in the lower-lying roads toward Broadstairs seafront and in the terrace gardens south of Broadstairs town centre. Where this clay layer is at 20 to 40 centimetres depth, it acts as a drainage cap: water from the chalk above drains down through the topsoil and pools on the clay, creating a perched water table in winter. The root zone of a hedge planted in this soil is in permanently wet ground from November through March.

The wet winters of 2022 to 2023 and 2023 to 2024 were significantly wetter than the SE England average: the Met Office records for Thanet show above-average rainfall in both winters, and the 2023 to 2024 season was particularly prolonged. That extended period of wet soil increased the population density of Phytophthora zoospores in the soil and their travel radius from existing sources, which is why the number of Phytophthora cases I saw in CT10 gardens in 2024 was noticeably higher than in previous years.

The gardens least at risk are those on pure chalk at the top of the ridge above Broadstairs town centre and along the clifftops at North Foreland, where the topsoil is thin and drainage is rapid. The gardens at greatest risk are those in the flatter, lower-lying streets where the clay cap is present and where shade from buildings reduces evaporation.

When Phytophthora cannot be treated: accepting full section loss

If the collar-scratch test on every plant in a section shows the dark staining of Phytophthora infection, and the section runs to ten or more consecutive plants of a susceptible species, the practical outcome is full section removal and replanting. This is not a failure: it is the correct response to a disease for which there is no chemical cure and no way to reverse established root damage.

In Broadstairs conservation area gardens, the removal of a substantial length of an established hedge can raise questions about planning and the s.211 notice requirement. The s.211 notice under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 applies to work on individual trees with a trunk diameter over 75 millimetres at 1.5 metres above ground level, in a conservation area. It does not generally apply to the removal of hedge plants, since most hedging plants do not reach the trunk diameter threshold as individual stems. However, if your hedge has single-stemmed plants that have grown to tree scale over many years (a common situation in older cherry laurel hedges in the larger gardens of Broadstairs and St Peter's), check the stem diameters before carrying out removal. If in doubt, contact Thanet District Council's planning and trees team for a pre-application enquiry before starting work. The conservation areas guide covers the full s.211 framework for CT10.
