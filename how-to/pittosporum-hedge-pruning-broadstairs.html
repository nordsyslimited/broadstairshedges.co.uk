How to prune a Pittosporum hedge: timing, technique and the three-cut year for Broadstairs CT10.

Pittosporum is the coastal evergreen that too many mainland Kent gardens pass over in favour of Leylandii or privet. In Broadstairs and along the Thanet clifftop arc, where the maritime climate softens winter minimums and the salt-laden east wind rules out softer screens, Pittosporum tenuifolium is a natural choice: it shrugs off salt spray, holds its small wavy leaves through the coldest months, and gives a dense, formal face that takes annual clipping well. I see it in the older clifftop gardens at Kingsgate and North Foreland, in front-of-terrace positions in the St Peter's conservation area, and in the narrower front gardens along the Viking Bay seafront. The pruning question most owners get wrong is how many times per year to cut, and how far back to go each time. Pittosporum is nothing like yew: cut into bare old wood and it will not come back. This guide explains the three-cut year that keeps it dense and well-faced, the tools to use, the nesting-season check that must come first, and what to do if the hedge has already been allowed to get away.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the hedge and check for active nests

This step comes before any hedge work carried out between 1 March and 31 August, without exception. Pittosporum's dense, small-leaved growth makes it attractive to nesting wrens, dunnocks, robins and goldfinches in particular: the tight branch matrix provides good support for a small cup nest and the evergreen canopy gives year-round concealment. Walk slowly along the full length of the hedge, pausing every metre or so to watch and listen. Look for adult birds flying repeatedly into and out of the same section; listen for faint chick noise or alarm calls from inside the canopy. If the hedge runs along a south-facing wall or fence, check those sections with particular care, as birds favour the warmth. An active nest with eggs or chicks must be left undisturbed. Mark the section, leave a clear gap in the cut at that point, and return once the brood has fledged: most garden passerines complete a brood in three to five weeks from hatching. You can cut the rest of the hedge in the meantime, leaving a half-metre section either side of the nest untouched.

Pittosporum, nesting birds and the 1 March-31 August window

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest containing eggs or young, or to disturb a bird while it is on an active nest. The practical caution window for Broadstairs gardens is 1 March to 31 August. Pittosporum's dense evergreen structure means nests can sit close to the clipping surface and be missed by a quick visual check: a proper, slow walk of the full hedge length before each cut is not optional. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest, with up to six months in prison for aggravated offences. Nests can be present in any of the three annual cut windows described in this guide, so check every time.

Step 2: Understand the three-cut year for Pittosporum in CT10

Pittosporum tenuifolium in a Broadstairs garden needs three cuts in a normal growing season to stay well-faced, dense and within its allotted space. The sequence runs as follows.

  • Late April to mid-May (first cut): a light formative trim once new growth is pushing actively but before it has extended more than 50-80mm from the previous year's wood. This cut removes any winter die-back, corrects any unevenness that appeared over winter, and sets the shape for the season. Do not cut hard at this stage: remove only the current extension growth and any dead or damaged stems. This cut is done with secateurs or hand shears rather than a powered trimmer for most hedges, as the growth at this point is typically not dense enough to justify the powered tool.
  • July (main cut): the principal annual shaping cut, taken once the main flush of new growth has extended and is firming up. This is the cut that defines the hedge's face and top for the summer, and where most of the year's volume reduction happens. The hedge trimmer earns its place here on most Pittosporum hedges because the growth is now dense, uniform and woody enough to cut cleanly without tearing. Aim to remove roughly half of the current season's extension growth: enough to tighten the face and correct any lean or unevenness, but not so much that you expose bare interior wood.
  • Late August (final tidy, no later than 31 August): a light pass over the faces and top to tidy any new growth that has pushed through since July and to bring the hedge into the autumn and winter in a clean, level condition. This cut must be complete by 31 August: cutting in September clips into the bud sites forming on the current season's wood that will push growth the following spring, and it also risks damaging the soft new growth before it has hardened sufficiently to resist the first autumn frost. In a year where the growing season runs warm and wet and July growth is still active in late August, prioritise completing this cut before the end of the month rather than waiting for growth to settle.

The three-cut calendar is not fixed to exact dates: a cold late spring might push the first cut back to late May, a warm season might bring the July cut forward to late June. Read the hedge, not the calendar. What matters is the principle: three light cuts across the growing season rather than one annual hard cut, which stresses the plant and tends to produce coarser, less uniform regrowth.

Step 3: Gather your tools

Pittosporum's small leaves (typically 20-40mm long on most cultivars) respond well to both powered hedge trimmers and hand shears without the brown-edged leaf damage that makes power tools a poor choice on large-leaved species like cherry laurel. The small leaf surface means cut damage is minimal and the hedge greens up within two to three weeks of a powered pass.

For the first cut (late April/May):

  • Hand shears or secateurs for tidying winter die-back and light formative shaping. The growth at this stage is often not uniform enough for a powered trimmer to cut cleanly without leaving obvious gaps.

For the July and late-August cuts:

  • Powered hedge trimmer for the main faces and top on established hedges. A medium-weight cordless trimmer (blade length 500-550mm) works well for a hedge up to 2m in height. Avoid a heavy petrol trimmer for Pittosporum: the vibration can score the small leaves more than a lighter cordless tool.
  • Long-handled hedge shears for finishing edges and corners, particularly where the hedge meets a fence post or wall and a powered trimmer cannot reach cleanly.
  • Secateurs for cutting any individual stems the trimmer has partially cut or left ragged, and for removing dead or diseased wood identified during the cut.

For hedges above 2m, use platform steps with a wide standing platform. Do not lean a standard ladder against Pittosporum growth: the flexible stems compress but do not support the foot of a ladder securely, and the crown, once the top is cut level, provides no lateral grip for the ladder feet.

Step 4: The late-April first cut

Work along the full length of the hedge before setting up equipment, confirming the nesting check is complete and noting any sections that need particular attention: die-back from the previous winter, sections that grew faster than the rest and are ahead of the target profile, or corners that have moved out of vertical. The first cut is a correction cut as much as a growth-removal cut.

Trim the sides first, working from the base upward on each face, then set a string line and trim the top level. Remove only the new growth and dead material at this stage: in most cases this means taking 20-40mm off the faces and a similar amount from the top. On a hedge that wintered well and has not pushed hard growth yet, the first cut may be almost entirely cosmetic: straightening a slight lean on one face, removing a handful of dead stems, levelling a top that sagged under the weight of January wind.

Do not use this cut to try to reduce the overall height or width of the hedge. Height and width reduction on Pittosporum must be done during the period of active growth (the July cut is the better moment for any size reduction), because cutting into old bare wood outside the active growing period is the surest way to produce dead sections that do not recover.

Step 5: The July main cut

This is the cut that defines the hedge for the season. Set a string line between canes at each end of the run, with a support stake at the mid-point on any run over 6m, at the target cut height. Pittosporum does not grow with the same absolute uniformity as a formally clipped yew or box, and without a string line it is easy to follow the existing slightly undulating top and lock in the irregularity rather than correcting it.

Cut the sides first, maintaining the batter angle: the hedge should be slightly wider at the base than at the top, typically 60-80mm difference in width across a 1.5m face. This matters on a Broadstairs clifftop hedge particularly: the salt-laden wind suppresses the upper and seaward growth progressively, and a hedge cut with a vertical or outward-leaning profile loses light to its base over successive years, producing sparse lower growth and an increasingly top-heavy shape. The batter keeps the base dense and the windbreak effective at ground level where it is most needed.

Remove approximately half of the current season's extension growth on the sides. On a hedge trimmed annually, this will typically mean cutting 80-120mm back from the current tips. The target cut line should fall into wood that still carries live leafy laterals: if you are cutting into wood that is bare for more than 20-30mm behind the cut, you are going too hard. Move the cut line outward until you are back in leafy growth and accept a slightly less tight face than you aimed for: a slightly open face recovers within one growing season; bare wood exposed on Pittosporum does not.

Once the sides are done, run the trimmer along the string line for the top, then check the whole run for individual stems that need finishing with secateurs. Pittosporum, with its slightly wavy and irregular stem habit, tends to leave a small number of individual shoots standing proud after a powered pass: these are best removed with a hand tool rather than going back over the face with the trimmer, which risks removing more than intended from sections that were already cut to the right line.

Step 6: The late-August final tidy

By late August, the hedge will have pushed 40-80mm of new growth over the July cut. The final tidy removes this growth, leaving the hedge clean and level going into autumn and winter. This is a light cut: run the trimmer once over each face and across the top, taking the new growth back to just above where the July cut left the surface. There is no need to reset a string line for the top on a hedge that was levelled correctly in July, as the new growth should be broadly uniform and the existing cut surface is still visible as a reference.

Finish by the end of August without exception. In a typical Broadstairs growing season, September growth on Pittosporum is softer and less mature than August growth, and cutting into it removes the bud sites that will push the following spring's extension. It also leaves a soft cut surface that is more vulnerable to damage from the first Thanet autumn frosts, which arrive on still clear nights from mid-October onward in most years. A hedge tidied by 31 August will have three to four weeks to begin callusing the cut surface before the first risk of frost damage.

When renovation is not straightforward: the bare-old-wood problem

Pittosporum's one significant maintenance constraint is its response to hard cutting: it does not regenerate from old bare wood the way yew, cherry laurel or even Leylandii does. If you cut into wood that has lost its leaves entirely, the stems below the cut point will not push new growth to fill the gap. This means that a Pittosporum hedge that has been cut too hard in the past, or that has been neglected and then cut back severely in a single intervention, can be left with permanent bare sections that do not recover.

The practical rule is this: never cut beyond the point where leafy lateral growth is present on the stems you are cutting. If the hedge has grown so wide or tall that the leafy growth zone has moved far out from the desired face position, you cannot simply cut it back to where you want it: you must work the face back gradually over two or three growing seasons, removing no more than 25-30% of the leafy face width in any single year. This is slower than the approach you would take with yew or laurel, but it is the only method that does not risk creating permanent bare patches.

For a Pittosporum hedge in the Broadstairs area that has been left uncut for three or more years and has grown substantially beyond its original position, the honest assessment is that returning it to a tight formal face may not be possible without removing the hedge entirely and replanting. If that is the position you are in, I can advise at a site visit whether the hedge has enough leafy growth close to the target face to work with, or whether replanting makes more sense for the specific site.

One exception to the bare-wood constraint applies to individual branches that have died back due to frost or physical damage: these can be cut back to live wood at any point, even if the cut falls well into the main stem structure, as long as you are cutting to a point where live wood is confirmed by a visible green cambium layer under the bark. Dead sections do not regenerate regardless of where you cut, so removing them back to confirmed live wood is always the correct action.

Pittosporum varieties in Broadstairs gardens and on the Thanet coast

The choice of variety affects both the hedge's appearance and its resilience in a Broadstairs position. The key split is between the green-leaved and variegated forms: the variegated cultivars are generally slightly less salt-hardy and slightly more frost-sensitive than the plain green forms, and on a directly exposed clifftop position a green-leaved variety is the safer choice.

Pittosporum tenuifolium (the straight species, plain green form) is the most reliably hardy option for the Thanet coast, rated RHS H4 (down to approximately -10C), and the most tolerant of salt wind. Its small, apple-green wavy-edged leaves give a fine texture in a clipped hedge that reads well from a distance. I recommend this form for any front-line position: above Stone Bay or Joss Bay, along the North Foreland headland, or on the seaward face of a property where the hedge takes the full force of an easterly. Growth rate is moderate (30-40cm per year once established), reaching 2-3m at maturity with annual pruning.

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Silver Queen' is the most widely planted variegated form in CT10 gardens: grey-green leaves with a cream-white margin, a slightly more open habit than the plain green form, and a slightly higher visual profile that suits a garden wanting more presence than a plain green hedge provides. It performs well in sheltered Broadstairs positions: the older St Peter's conservation area gardens, south-facing front gardens with wall-shelter, and the more enclosed terraced gardens inland from the town centre. In a fully exposed front-line clifftop position, Silver Queen can suffer more winter browning than the plain green form and will need assessment in any year that combines a hard frost below -6C with sustained easterly wind.

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Tom Thumb' is a compact, slow-growing form with deep purple-bronze foliage, reaching 0.5-1m with minimal pruning. It suits a low formal boundary hedge in a sheltered front garden or a repeat planting used structurally in a border. Its low growth rate (typically 10-15cm per year) means the annual cut is very light: it is more a matter of keeping the shape than actually removing significant volume. I see it most in the newer front-garden redesigns in the St Peter's area and along the roads inland from Viking Bay, where a small formal hedge with good winter colour is more useful than a full privacy screen.

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Trim's Hedger' is a named cultivar bred specifically for formal hedging, listed by the RHS specifically for this use. It has a naturally upright, dense growth habit that makes it easier to maintain as a tight formal face with less annual clipping than the straight species. Where a very neat formal hedge is the priority and the position is reasonably sheltered, this is the variety to specify at planting. It is less widely available from retail nurseries than Silver Queen or the plain species, but is offered by several UK hedging specialists and is worth seeking out for new plantings where long-term tidiness and low maintenance effort are the main criteria.

For a detailed comparison of front-line and second-line coastal species across the Broadstairs and Thanet area, the coastal species guide covers Pittosporum alongside Escallonia, Griselinia and Elaeagnus, with notes on which species performs best at different distances from the cliff edge.

Common problems with Pittosporum hedges in CT10

Bare patches that fail to recover. As described above, these result from cutting into old bare wood. The only resolution, short of hedge removal, is to allow the surrounding leafy growth to extend and gradually cover the gap over successive seasons, being careful not to clip back the adjacent growth hard enough to expose further bare sections. This can take two to four seasons depending on the size of the gap and the vigour of the surrounding growth. On a front-line coastal hedge, wind-suppressed growth often means the recovery takes closer to the longer end of that range.

Winter die-back on exposed stems. Following a hard winter with sustained frost below -8C combined with easterly wind, Pittosporum can show die-back on individual exposed stems, particularly on the seaward or north-facing face. Do not cut this material until late April: as with Escallonia and Griselinia, new growth breaking from live wood below the dead section through spring will clarify how far back the die-back actually extends, and cutting too early can remove wood that would otherwise have recovered. Once the recovery line is clear, cut back to confirmed live wood. In most Thanet winters, which are moderated by the maritime influence, die-back is limited to individual shoot tips rather than main structural stems.

Sparse base on an older hedge. This is almost always the result of cutting the hedge vertically or with an outward lean at the top, which progressively shades the lower growth. Restore the correct batter angle (wider at base than top) and, if the base is already bare, carry out a light renovation cut on the lower face to expose it to more light. Pittosporum will push new growth from the base if light is restored to it, though more slowly than species like yew or cherry laurel: allow at least one full growing season before judging the result.

Yellowing foliage on young plants. On newly planted Pittosporum in Broadstairs chalk soil, chlorosis (yellowing between the leaf veins while veins remain green) can indicate iron or manganese deficiency caused by the alkaline free-draining chalk restricting nutrient uptake. Apply a chelated seaweed or ericaceous-formulated foliar feed in April and May. If yellowing persists into a second season, dig in acidifying matter (leaf mould, composted bark) around the root zone in autumn and apply a balanced slow-release fertiliser in early spring. The free-draining chalk that makes Pittosporum prone to nutrient lock-up is the same characteristic that helps it establish quickly on the thin Margate Chalk soils of the clifftop belt, so the trade-off is manageable with the right feeding regime.
