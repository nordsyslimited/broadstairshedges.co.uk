How to plant and train a pleached hornbeam screen in Broadstairs: privacy and windbreak on a compact coastal plot.

A solid hedge blocks everything: the neighbour's line of sight and the view over the cliff. In the narrow plots that sit above Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate, that is often not what people want. A pleached hornbeam screen gives you a flat, clipped panel of foliage from about 1.5 metres up, on clear bare stems below that level, which means you get privacy at head height while keeping the view under it. The screen is also far less prone to wind toppling than a solid hedge acting as a sail on an exposed clifftop position. This guide covers the full sequence: framework, planting on Margate Chalk, first-year training, and the twice-yearly maintenance clip.

Steps

Step 1: Source bare-root hornbeam whips in autumn and plan your spacing

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is the standard choice for pleached screens in the UK because it is genuinely tough on exposed sites, trains readily onto a flat framework, holds its russet marcescent leaves through winter (which matters for year-round screening) and tolerates the alkaline Margate Chalk beneath most CT10 gardens without the soil-amendment work that acid-loving species would need.

Buy bare-root 1-year-old whips, 60-90cm, from a UK nursery. October to late November is the right window: bare-root stock is lifted once dormant and planted before the ground hardens. Bare-root whips are substantially cheaper than containerised stock (roughly £1-2 per whip versus £8-15 for a potted equivalent), establish faster on free-draining Thanet chalk, and produce more vigorous growth in the first season. Hopes Grove Nurseries in Tenterden or Ashridge Trees carry good bare-root hornbeam stock each autumn. If you miss the bare-root window, containerised stock planted in February or early March works, though it costs more and benefits from more attentive first-year watering.

Space trees at 1.5 metres centre to centre along the line of the screen. This allows each tree to develop laterals that just touch those of its neighbours by year 3, producing a continuous flat panel without overcrowding. For a typical Broadstairs terrace side return or garden boundary, a run of 4 to 6 trees (6m to 9m of fence line) is the common ask. Anything longer is fine: you just need a proportional increase in posts and framework materials.

Step 2: Install the support framework before the trees arrive

A pleached screen needs a rigid framework for the first 3-4 years: without it, young hornbeam stems flex in the coastal wind and lose their flat-panel shape. The framework can be removed once the screen is self-supporting, but most people leave it in permanently as it becomes invisible inside the canopy.

Set 75mm x 75mm pressure-treated timber posts at 1.5m intervals (one post between each pair of trees, plus one at each end). Each post should stand 2.4m above finished ground level; with a 600mm setting depth in chalk, that means cutting or ordering 3m posts. On particularly hard chalk, you may need a post-hole borer or post-mix concrete rather than just driving posts in with a maul.

Run galvanised 3.5mm straining wire between the posts at 300mm vertical intervals from 1.5m height to 2.2m height. That gives you 3 wires defining the panel zone. Strain each wire tight using eye bolts and straining screws at one end post: slack wire allows the trained shoots to sag and ruins the flat-panel look. Alternatively, screw horizontal 50mm x 25mm pressure-treated timber battens across the posts at the same intervals: battens are more rigid than wire and easier to tie young shoots onto, which is useful in a windy Thanet plot where wire vibration can work ties loose.

The clear stem section below 1.5m is left entirely bare. Any shoots emerging below that level are rubbed off as they appear in the first two seasons.

Step 3: Plant the hornbeam whips on Margate Chalk

Position each whip directly in front of a post, centred on the 1.5m spacing. Dig a 450-500mm-deep hole for each one. On the thin chalk topsoil that underlies most of CT10, widen the planting hole to 450mm diameter and work good garden compost or well-rotted organic matter into the backfill at about 30% by volume: the chalk drains fast, and the added organic content around the roots makes a real difference to moisture retention through the first dry spring and summer.

Plant to the nursery soil mark (the line on the stem where the soil level stood in the nursery field). Do not plant deeper: hornbeam resents being buried at the base of the stem. Backfill carefully, firm with your boot in stages to eliminate air pockets, and water in with 10 litres per tree immediately after planting.

Stake each whip with a 600mm stake angled at 45 degrees away from the prevailing wind (south-westerly for most Thanet plots: angle the stake to the north-east). Fix with a rubber tree tie just below the lowest framework wire. The stake holds the lower stem steady while the framework above will eventually support the canopy. Apply 75mm of composted bark mulch in a 400mm radius around each tree, keeping a 50mm clearance from the stem itself.

Tie the leading shoot loosely to the nearest post as soon as you plant: young hornbeam whips are flexible enough to bend in the coastal wind and if the tip bends repeatedly it sets that way. A loose tie with soft garden twine is enough; do not constrict the stem.

Step 4: First growing season: selecting and tying lateral shoots

From April, new shoots emerge from the stem. Most will grow radially in all directions. Your job from April to July is to select and tie in the shoots you want, and remove the ones you do not.

On each side of the stem (left and right, parallel to the screen plane), select 3 or 4 laterals that are growing in the plane of the screen. Tie each selected lateral to the framework wire or batten with a single loop of soft garden twine: not so tight that it constricts, but firm enough that the shoot lies flat against the frame. These tied laterals become the primary lateral structure of the panel, building up year by year.

Remove any shoots growing directly forward or directly backward (perpendicular to the screen plane) by rubbing them off while they are still soft, or cutting flush with a sharp pair of secateurs once they have hardened slightly. Do this promptly: a shoot left for a full season becomes a side branch that takes time and a visible cut to remove. Also rub off any shoots emerging below the 1.5m clear-stem line: this is what gives the screen its distinctive "on stilts" look and what allows the view under it.

When the leading shoot reaches your target overall height (typically 2.0-2.2m above ground, which places the top of the panel at the top wire), pinch or cut the growing tip. This redirects the tree's energy into the side laterals and starts the process of building a dense, wide panel rather than a tall, thin one. On CT10 chalk, first-season extension growth is usually 40-60cm on the leader and 20-35cm on the laterals: do not expect to form a complete panel in year one.

Nesting season: check before any pruning or tie-in work

Once a hornbeam screen starts to develop real leaf cover, typically from year 2, songbirds readily nest in the canopy layer. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest, carry a penalty of an unlimited fine per nest and up to six months in prison for aggravated offences. The practical safe window for all cutting and training work on an established screen is 1 September to the end of February. If you need to tie in or prune between 1 March and 31 August, walk the full length of the screen first and watch for adult birds entering or leaving repeatedly: if a bird is visiting a particular point on the screen more than once in a few minutes, there is almost certainly a nest there. Leave that section completely alone until you are confident the brood has fledged.

Step 5: First dormant-season formative prune (November to February)

Once the leaves have dropped in November, the framework structure is fully visible and you can see exactly what you have. This first dormant prune is the most important single operation in establishing a good pleached screen, and it is straightforward because the wood is still thin and manageable.

Work along the screen with a pair of sharp bypass secateurs. For each lateral you tied in during the summer, cut it back to within 3-4 buds of the point where it meets the main stem (or, from year 2 onwards, to within 3-4 buds of the previous year's hardened lateral spur). This is the classic spur-pruning technique: it forces the tree to respond by breaking out 3 or 4 new shoots at each cut point next spring rather than just extending the existing shoot. After 3 or 4 years of this, each lateral becomes a dense, many-branched spur system that produces a tight, opaque panel from bud-break in April through leaf-drop in November.

Remove any shoots growing out of the screen plane: cut flush to their origin point, leaving no stub. Stubs callus slowly and are a potential entry point for fungal problems. Remove any crossing shoots within the panel that would create obvious lumps in the flat face. Cut cleanly with sharp blades: hornbeam is a hard, dense wood and a blunt secateur blade tears rather than cuts, leaving ragged tissue that heals slowly.

If the leading stem has not yet reached the top wire, leave it and let it extend through the following season before cutting it back to a bud just above the top wire in the subsequent winter. Do not rush this: a screen with a ragged, uneven top line is always slightly untidy. Wait until the leader clears the top wire by 150-200mm before cutting back, and cut to a bud pointing in the same plane as the screen rather than one pointing forward or back.

Step 6: Ongoing maintenance: the two-clip year

Once the screen is established (typically from year 3), you settle into a twice-yearly routine that takes 30-60 minutes for a typical 6-8 tree run with a good pair of powered or battery hedge shears.

Late summer cut (August-September): this is the main shaping cut, and the one that does most of the cosmetic work. By late July or early August, the spring flush of growth has hardened enough to cut cleanly without tips browning back. Work along each face of the screen with sharp shears, cutting back flush to the flat plane of the previous year's panel face. Clip the top edge straight with a line stretched taut between the two end posts if you want a clean horizontal top. Cut the end faces straight across (the screen will have a "stepped" front view at each end post that is part of the structural appearance). Be conservative: you cannot put growth back. Take off the new season's extension growth plus a centimetre or two into the hardened wood. After this cut, the screen looks tight, flat and formal.

Winter cut (December-January): a lighter, structural cut done with secateurs or hand shears. The leaves have dropped so you can see the framework again. Remove anything that has escaped the summer cut and grown out of the plane. Tidy any leading shoots that over-extended after the summer cut. Check ties and replace any that have tightened or degraded. This cut is done in full dormancy and carries almost no risk of browning or die-back on the cut ends.

Do not cut in the heart of summer (June or early July). At that point the new growth is still soft and actively elongating: cut ends brown visibly and the subsequent growth is weaker than if you wait a further 3-5 weeks for the flush to harden. The late-August window, after the main growing flush but before any autumn extension, produces the cleanest result.

Why hornbeam works on the Thanet coast

The combination of the Thanet Coast SPA, the Thanet Coast SSSI and the direct south-westerly and north-easterly exposure above the chalk bays creates conditions that eliminate most formally trained tree species within 5 years. Salt-wind desiccation, rapid chalk drainage and occasional hard frost in north-easterly February conditions (when temperatures on the North Foreland headland can run 2-3 degrees below those inland at Westgate) all narrow the field considerably.

Hornbeam handles all three. It is native to the south-east of England, which means it has evolved with the same climatic range as Thanet, not been imported from a more benign continental setting. Its tolerance of thin, alkaline chalk soils is well documented: it grows naturally on chalk downland in Kent and is a constituent species of ancient coppice on the chalky ground south of Canterbury. The RHS has awarded Carpinus betulus a full Award of Garden Merit. The leaf retention through winter (marcescence) is a practical advantage specific to pleached applications: unlike lime (Tilia spp.), which is beautiful in summer but completely open and screenless from November to April, hornbeam provides year-round privacy even on a cold February morning in CT10. The russet-brown retained leaves are also less visually dead than beech's tan-grey: in the low winter light above Viking Bay, the warm-toned retained hornbeam canopy reads almost as a colour feature in its own right.

The open base of a pleached screen is a specific coastal advantage. A solid hedge on an exposed clifftop position acts as a rigid sail from ground level up: the total wind force on it is proportional to its area. A pleached screen presents a gap below 1.5m, which allows a significant proportion of a strong south-westerly or north-easterly to pass underneath. In practice this reduces the loading on both the trees and the framework posts. A solid 2m hornbeam hedge on a thin chalk site with 600mm of topsoil above the chalk may uproot in an exceptional gale; a pleached screen on the same site, with its reduced total surface area and wind-permeable base, is substantially less at risk.

Framework sizing for exposed clifftop positions

The standard 75mm x 75mm posts and 3.5mm straining wire described in Step 2 are adequate for most sheltered or semi-exposed CT10 garden plots. For positions directly above the chalk cliffs, facing the North Sea or the Channel with no intermediate shelter (the gardens immediately above Stone Bay, Kingsgate Bay and the North Foreland headland are the clearest examples), upsize to 100mm x 100mm posts set 750mm deep in post-mix concrete, and use 4mm galvanised straining wire in place of 3.5mm. The extra cost per post is modest and the additional rigidity in a strong gale is significant.

For fully exposed clifftop positions, consider fixing a short diagonal brace at each end post: a 50mm x 50mm timber at 45 degrees from the top of the end post to a stake 600mm back from the screen line. This prevents the whole framework racking longitudinally in a gale. The braces can be removed once the screen is well established (year 5 or later) or left in and the hornbeam allowed to grow over them.

The wire tensions need checking each autumn before the leaf-drop. Coastal salt air accelerates galvanised wire corrosion slightly: inspect each strainer for rust at the thread of the straining screw and replace any showing deep surface corrosion. The wire itself typically lasts 8-10 years before the galvanising becomes visibly degraded on a clifftop position; coastal wire sold specifically for fence use (heavier galvanising) is worth specifying from the outset.

First-year watering on Margate Chalk

This is the single most common reason a pleached hornbeam establishment fails in CT10 gardens. The Margate Chalk formation is porous, free-draining limestone: in the thin topsoil above it, a week without rain in May or June can mean the top 200mm is dry while the chalk itself retains moisture 400mm below the root ball of a newly planted whip. The roots have not yet extended down to reach it.

From planting until the end of the first summer, water each tree at 5 litres per week during any dry spell longer than 5 consecutive days without significant rain. "Significant rain" at Broadstairs means at least 8-10mm at the site: light showers that barely wet the surface are not meaningful on chalk. Manston station (the nearest recording station, 8km south-west of Broadstairs) records around 612mm of annual rainfall, but the distribution is uneven and April-June can be notably dry in the south-east. Do not assume summer rain will save you: in a dry spring the establishment of a first-year screen depends almost entirely on supplementary watering.

The mulch layer applied at planting (Step 3) reduces surface moisture loss significantly, but it does not replace watering. Apply water slowly at the base of each tree using a low-flow hose or watering can rather than a sprinkler: fast application on chalk runs off the surface before it has time to percolate down to the root zone. The aim is to wet the top 250-300mm of soil at the root ball: slow watering achieves this, fast sprinkler watering mostly evaporates or runs off.
