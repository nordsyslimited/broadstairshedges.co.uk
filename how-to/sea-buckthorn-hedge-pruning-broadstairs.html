How to prune and manage a sea buckthorn hedge in Broadstairs: timing, suckers and CT10 coastal notes.

Sea buckthorn is one of the most capable hedging plants available for front-line positions on the Thanet coast. It grows wild on the chalk cliffs and shingle banks between Broadstairs and North Foreland, takes full salt wind without losing vigour, and thrives on the fast-draining Margate Chalk that defeats many other species. The dense, thorned grey stems create an impenetrable barrier, and the orange berries that ripen in October draw in fieldfares and redwings arriving from Scandinavia. The pruning regime is straightforward, but two things need understanding before you start: the correct cutting window and how to stop the plant colonising the rest of the garden by suckering.

Steps

Step 1: Check for nesting birds before picking up any tool

Walk the full length of the sea buckthorn hedge at close range before doing anything else. The dense, thorned structure makes sea buckthorn a preferred nesting site for whitethroat, linnet and dunnock in gardens close to the Thanet Coast SSSI, and for house sparrow and starling in more sheltered positions. The physical difficulty of inspecting the interior of a mature sea buckthorn hedge is not an exemption from the legal requirement to check: if you cannot see into the hedge clearly, watch for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section before concluding that no active nest is present. If you find a nest with eggs or dependent young, stop work at that section and return once the brood has fledged, typically two to three weeks after hatching for small passerines.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting birds in a thorny hedge

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly take, damage or destroy an active nest. The accepted guidance window for caution is 1 March to 31 August, although the Act itself sets no dates and early nesters can be active before March on sheltered south-facing sites. The correct pruning window for sea buckthorn (late February to mid-March) sits right at the edge of this period: a check is not optional, it is the difference between working legally and potentially committing an offence. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest found.

Step 2: Choose your cutting window and set your height and width targets

The right window for the annual pruning of sea buckthorn is late February to mid-March, before the new season's buds begin to break and while the plant is still in late dormancy. Cutting in this window removes the previous year's long, arching growth before it sets another season's extension, and it avoids the period when the orange berries, if present, are of most value to wintering birds. By late February most of the berry harvest has been stripped by fieldfares, redwings and starlings, so cutting at this point does not deprive birds of food.

Decide before cutting what height and width you are maintaining. A garden windbreak hedge at 2.0-2.5 metres is manageable with a long-handled hedge trimmer from ground level and provides effective shelter across a generous CT10 garden. Sea buckthorn left unmanaged can reach 4-6 metres over a decade, but cutting annually in February maintains any target height comfortably. Do not attempt to reduce a sea buckthorn hedge by more than one third of its total height in a single season: like most woody shrubs, it responds better to repeated modest cutting than to an abrupt drastic reduction.

Step 3: Remove dead, diseased and crossing stems before shaping

Sea buckthorn is a tough, healthy species and rarely shows significant disease in a CT10 coastal garden, but dead stems do accumulate in the interior of mature hedges, particularly where the growth is dense enough to shade out lower sections. Before making any shaping cuts, spend time removing dead or clearly dying stems from the hedge body. Use loppers or a pruning saw for stems thicker than 25mm: the thorns on sea buckthorn are stiff and sharp enough to puncture heavy gloves, so leather gauntlets are necessary for this work, not just a precaution.

Remove any stems that are growing back through the centre of the hedge rather than outward, as these will eventually create a congested tangle that reduces the hedge's ability to regenerate from the base. Cut crossing stems back to a clean outward-facing lateral rather than leaving a short stub. Leave the overall shape assessment until the dead and crossing material is gone: a sea buckthorn hedge often looks smaller and better once the dead wood is cleared.

Step 4: Cut back the previous season's long growth to shape, maintaining a batter

Sea buckthorn extends by 300-600mm in a growing season on well-drained chalk soil in CT10, and the new season's growth is typically long, arching and unbranched at the tips. The shaping cut removes this extension to your target width and height, leaving the branched structural growth intact beneath.

Use a powered hedge trimmer for a hedge of any significant length: the thorned stems are too numerous for hand-shears work to be practical on anything beyond a single short specimen. Work the sides first, trimming from bottom to top with the blade at a slight inward batter (wider at the base, narrower at the top). A batter of about 50-75mm narrowing per metre of height is enough to keep light reaching the lower stems: a sea buckthorn hedge cut with perfectly vertical sides will eventually go bare at the base, just as a beech or hornbeam hedge does. Work the top last, using the string line or cane guides if the hedge line is long or the level requires checking.

Do not attempt to cut into the hard, woody interior growth on a routinely managed hedge: sea buckthorn does not regenerate reliably from bare brown wood, and cutting into old wood on a healthy section removes growth that cannot be replaced. The annual trim works on soft new extension only. If the hedge has been left unmanaged for several years and the new growth is now deep into the structure, staged renovation over two winters is safer than a single hard cut (see renovation notes below).

Step 5: Cut suckers to the root through the growing season

Suckering is the main management challenge with sea buckthorn in a garden setting. The plant spreads by sending up new stems from the lateral roots, which can extend 2-4 metres from the main hedge body in any direction including into borders, lawns and neighbouring ground. On the sandy, free-draining soils over Margate Chalk, the lateral roots travel fast and suckers appear from April through to October.

The only effective long-term control is to cut suckers off at or below ground level as soon as they appear, before they thicken and become a management task in their own right. Use a sharp spade or mattock to cut each sucker at the root rather than at ground level: a sucker pruned at the surface will often branch and send up two or three replacement shoots. Dig down 150-200mm and cut the lateral root itself, then remove the sucker and its small root section completely. A sucker missed for a full season and allowed to develop a woody base will regrow from the cut more readily than a young sucker removed promptly.

If the hedge is planted along a boundary with a lawn, a physical root barrier (high-density polyethylene sheet, 500mm depth, buried vertically along the lawn side of the hedge) installed at planting time eliminates the sucker problem in the lawn completely. Retrofit installation of a root barrier once the hedge is established is possible but requires careful excavation and will cut some existing lateral roots, which typically prompts a short-term flush of new suckering before the plant adjusts. In borders, regular inspection and prompt removal is the practical solution rather than a barrier.

Sea buckthorn on the Thanet coast

Hippophae rhamnoides (sea buckthorn) is native to coastal sites on the east coast of England, where it grows on shingle, sand dunes and chalk cliff edges. The East Kent coast between Sandwich Bay and Margate includes several natural populations: the species appears on the chalk headland at North Foreland, on the exposed shingle at Sandwich Bay and Shellness, and on dune scrub at Minnis Bay. In CT10 gardens, it turns up most often in the clifftop properties above Kingsgate Bay, Stone Bay and Joss Bay, where it was planted as a windbreak from the 1970s and 1980s onward and has since naturalised beyond its original hedge lines in some locations.

The Thanet Coast SSSI designation covers the foreshore and cliff face from Minnis Bay to Ramsgate, including the coastal chalk grassland habitat on the clifftop at North Foreland. Sea buckthorn growing within or immediately adjacent to the SSSI boundary is part of a designated coastal dune thicket habitat (National Vegetation Classification type H7), and its management in that zone may engage Natural England. A garden hedge of sea buckthorn set back from the cliff edge and clearly within the domestic boundary is not subject to SSSI consent requirements, but if you are uncertain about the SSSI boundary in relation to your property, the Natural England MAGIC map (magic.defra.gov.uk) shows the exact designation boundary for the Thanet Coast SSSI.

The species is described by the JNCC as characteristic of exposed coastal sites on sand and chalk, and is noted for its capacity to form dense thickets through suckering, which is exactly the behaviour that makes it an effective windbreak hedge and exactly the behaviour that requires management in a garden context. It is nitrogen-fixing, which is unusual for a woody shrub and means it can establish and grow vigorously on the very low-nutrient soils over chalk without supplementary fertiliser.

Male and female plants: the berry question

Sea buckthorn is dioecious: there are separate male and female plants, and only female plants produce the orange berries. Male plants produce pollen but no fruit. Most bare-root sea buckthorn sold for hedging in the UK is supplied as a mixed planting with the intention of including both sexes, but the ratio varies by supplier. If the hedge you are managing produces no berries, it may be entirely or predominantly male, which is fine for windbreak purposes but means the wildlife value from the autumn berry crop is absent.

If you want berries, plant at roughly one male plant to six females along the hedge row. Single-sex male cultivars such as H. rhamnoides 'Hikul' are available for situations where you specifically want no berries (the fruit can stain paving and hard surfaces). Female plants ripen their berries from September to October in CT10 and the harvest is taken by fieldfares, redwings, starlings and blackbirds. The berries that remain after the first hard frost soften and become more palatable to a wider range of species. Leaving the berries unharvested through October and November and timing the annual pruning for late February means the full wildlife benefit of the fruit is available before the cut disturbs anything.

Renovating an overgrown or neglected sea buckthorn hedge

A sea buckthorn hedge that has not been cut for several years will typically have extended its outer growth by 1-2 metres per side and may have suckered substantially into surrounding ground. The renovation approach depends on how much of the hedge body is still green wood.

If the hedge has green, leafy growth from the base to the current outer extremities, a two-winter staged renovation works: cut one side hard in February of year one, reducing the hedge width by half, and leave the other side untouched. In year two, once the cut side has put on one season's regrowth, cut the other side to match. This staged approach avoids removing all the foliage from the hedge in one operation, which on a heavily overgrown sea buckthorn can leave a bare, unattractive structure for a full growing season.

If there are large dead sections within the hedge body, identified in Step 3, these need to be removed entirely and the gaps replanted. Sea buckthorn does not regenerate from dead wood, and a bare dead section within an otherwise living hedge will not fill in from the sides quickly: the plant extends by tip growth and suckering rather than by filling laterally. Plant bare-root replacements (available November to March) directly into the gap at the same spacing as the original planting, approximately 300-400mm apart, and water in well. On Margate Chalk, the first summer is the critical establishment period: water twice weekly through any dry spell between May and September in the first growing season.
