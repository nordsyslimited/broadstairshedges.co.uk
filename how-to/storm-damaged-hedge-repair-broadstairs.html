Recovering a storm-damaged hedge in Broadstairs: what to check and when to cut.

Hedges on the Thanet coast take the wind and the salt, and autumn and winter gales, and named storms such as Ciárán in November 2023, can leave a hedge scorched, leaning or broken. Most of the time the right response is slower than people expect. The important step is telling apart wind scorch, where you wait, from a structural break or a lifted root plate, where you act. This guide sets out what to check, what the RHS advises, the legal points that still apply after a storm, and what recovery usually involves.

Steps

  1. Stay away from the hedge until the wind has dropped.

    Do not work on a hedge during a storm or while gusts are still strong. Branches under strain can release suddenly, and a leaning hedge can bring a fence or wall with it. Look first from a distance along the full length of the hedge, upwards as well as along the face. Check for large branches hanging by a strip of bark, root plates lifting against a wall or fence, and any contact with overhead cables. If a cable is involved, keep clear and contact the network operator.

  2. Sort the damage into four types.
    • Root lifting (windthrow): the root plate has been pushed partly out of the ground, usually on the leeward side, and the hedge leans. Tall evergreens with a large surface area are the most exposed. Young or smaller plants can often be firmed back in and staked. A large established plant with a heavily lifted root plate may not recover.
    • Structural breaks: stems or branches have snapped or split. These are the cuts that are worth making promptly, to remove the hazard and leave a clean surface.
    • Wind scorch: leaves on the exposed face are brown and shrivelled, but the stems behind them may still be alive. The RHS describes this as damage caused by cold, drying winds combined with difficult soil conditions, and says to cut out scorched foliage on broadleaved evergreens in spring as growth resumes, not in winter.
    • Cosmetic lean: the hedge is pushed over but the roots are firm and nothing is broken. Check the base is still firmly in the ground and give it time.
  3. Check for nests before you cut.

    Storm damage and nesting birds

    Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built. The RSPB advises avoiding hedge and tree cutting between 1 March and 31 August to protect breeding birds. A storm does not suspend that. If a hazard is genuinely immediate, keep the work to the minimum needed to make it safe, look for nests first, and leave any active nest alone. Penalties can include an unlimited fine and up to six months in prison.

  4. Make hazards safe with the right tool.

    Use a handsaw or loppers for branches that are hanging under load, not a hedge trimmer, which can bind or kick. Stand clear of the line the branch will fall. For a broken stem, cut back cleanly to a bud, side shoot or the branch collar rather than leaving a ragged stub. Heavy limbs, anything near buildings or cables, and any chainsaw work are better left to a trained and insured contractor.

  5. Test whether a stem is alive.

    The RHS suggests scraping the bark lower down. If there is green tissue underneath, that part of the plant is alive and there is hope of regrowth, which can be encouraged by cutting back to live growth once the risk of frost has passed. If there is no green anywhere, the stem is dead. The RHS also notes that most damaged plants grow out of it, and that watering and feeding afterwards matter more than cutting hard straight away.

  6. Be patient with wind-scorched evergreens.

    For broadleaved evergreens such as laurel, privet, Escallonia and Griselinia, leave the browned foliage through the winter, then check for green under the bark in spring and cut out scorched growth as new shoots start. Conifers behave differently. The RHS says scorched conifers will often fail to regrow from badly damaged shoots and should not be pruned. Leyland cypress and most other conifers (yew is the main exception) do not reshoot from old brown wood, so a section with no green left is unlikely to come back.

  7. Firm and stake a leaning plant promptly.

    If a young or small plant has lifted but is still alive, firm the soil back around the root plate and stake it so it cannot rock. Use a proper tree tie that will not cut into the bark, and check it regularly. Leave the support in place until the plant is holding itself, usually through at least one growing season. Where the root plate has separated completely, removal and replanting is usually the better answer.

  8. Feed, mulch and water through the next season.

    The RHS advice after a damaging weather event is to focus on watering and feeding. In spring, feed with a general-purpose fertiliser and mulch the root zone, keeping the mulch clear of the stems. Water in dry spells during the first summer, because a damaged hedge has less leaf area to draw on and copes less well with drought than an undamaged one.

Species recovery notes

Yew is the one common conifer that regrows from old wood, so a damaged yew hedge is usually worth keeping, though it recovers slowly. Hawthorn and blackthorn are native deciduous hedging plants that regrow well from hard cutting, and wind scorch is less of an issue because they are bare in winter. Cherry laurel and privet reshoot readily from old wood. Escallonia and Griselinia are popular on the coast for their salt tolerance and can recover from scorch, but check for live buds before cutting back, because old leafless wood does not always regrow. Leylandii and other conifers do not regenerate from old brown wood, so a badly damaged or windthrown Leylandii hedge is often better replaced than restored. Legal points that still apply after a storm

If your property is in a conservation area, trees there are protected by section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990, which requires six weeks' notice to the council before certain work. GOV.UK guidance says notice is not needed for a tree with a trunk diameter of 75mm or less, measured at 1.5m above ground, and that protection orders would not normally cover shrubs, although a tree growing within a hedge can be covered. For a dead tree, five working days' written notice is normally needed before the work, with evidence that it is dead. Where a tree presents an immediate risk of serious harm and the work is urgent, you can do what is needed and notify the council as soon as practicable afterwards. Routine hedge cutting is normally not affected, but a larger tree growing inside a hedge may be. If you are unsure, ask Thanet District Council before cutting.
