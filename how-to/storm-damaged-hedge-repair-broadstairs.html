How to repair a storm-damaged hedge in Broadstairs: assessment and recovery steps for CT10 coastal gardens.

Thanet hedges take a battering. Storm Ciárán landed 1–2 November 2023 and caused structural damage across CT10 gardens: lifted roots, snapped branches and entire sections of Escallonia and Griselinia pushed over onto walls. Storm Eówyn in January 2025 did the same. The northeast exposure of Broadstairs, Stone Bay and the North Foreland cliffs means the prevailing storm track hits these hedges at their most vulnerable angle, from a direction that combines full wind load with salt-laden air. The approach that actually works after a storm is not "cut everything back and start again": it is a careful triage that separates wind scorch (where you wait) from structural breaks (where you cut now), followed by a deliberate recovery sequence. These are the steps I follow after every named storm on the Thanet coast.

Steps

  1. Wait for safe conditions before you go near the hedge.

    Do not enter the garden during a named storm or while gusts are still above gale force. The danger is not the hedge itself: it is overhead hazards created by the storm. Snapped branches under bending load can release suddenly and travel a long distance. On CT10 properties above the cliff edge, a lifted root ball can destabilise a section of boundary fencing or a neighbouring tree, and that secondary risk is harder to read from a distance than the hedge damage itself. Wait until the Met Office gust speed has dropped below 25 mph before carrying out any assessment. In practice this usually means the morning after the storm, not the same evening.

    Before starting work, walk from a safe distance along the full length of the hedge and look upward as well as along the face. Check for: large hanging branches still attached to stems by a strip of bark; uprooted root balls that are leaning against a fence or wall and could fall if the support shifts; and any entanglement with overhead cables (rare in most CT10 gardens, more common near the cliff-top properties on the North Foreland approach).

  2. Walk the hedge and categorise every damaged section by type.

    Damage to CT10 hedges falls into four distinct categories, and the correct response differs for each. Identifying which type you are looking at before picking up a tool is the step most often skipped, and skipping it leads to unnecessary removal of sections that would have recovered, or to leaving wind-scorched growth in place for months waiting for it to come back when it never will.

    • Root lifting (windthrow): the entire root ball, or a section of it, has been pushed partially out of the ground on the leeward side. The stem is still attached and still alive but the root plate has lifted. This is most common in shallow Margate chalk soils where roots cannot go deep, particularly in Leylandii and tall evergreen hedges that present a large surface area to northeast winds. The recovery window for windthrow sections is short: re-firming and staking must happen within 24–48 hours of the storm to give the best chance of root contact being restored.
    • Structural breaks: stems or branches have snapped. Some are still attached by a strip of bark and hanging at an angle; others have broken cleanly. Structural breaks need prompt clearance to remove the hazard, but the cut placement matters: a cut in the wrong place on a hedge species that reshoot from old wood can be wasteful, and a cut in the wrong place on one that does not (Leylandii, Griselinia) can remove more live material than necessary.
    • Wind scorch: the leaves on the exposed face are brown, crispy and collapsed, but the stems behind them are still green and intact. This is desiccation damage, not physical breakage. The cold northeast wind removes moisture from the leaf surface faster than the root zone can supply it, particularly when the ground is partially frozen. Wind scorch looks alarming from a distance and is often reported to me as "the hedge is dead", but in most cases the stems behind the brown leaves are still fully alive. The most important rule for wind scorch: do not cut it back in winter. Wait until late March or April.
    • Cosmetic lean: the hedge has been pushed by the wind and is leaning noticeably away from vertical, but the root system is intact and there are no broken stems. This is primarily a visual issue and often self-corrects within one growing season as the hedge resumes vertical growth. It rarely needs intervention beyond checking that the base is still firmly in the ground.
  3. Assess nesting activity before cutting, if the storm fell inside the March-to-August window.

    Storm damage in nesting season: the legal check before you cut

    Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest containing eggs or young, or to disturb a nesting bird protected under Schedule 1. The caution window for Broadstairs gardens is 1 March to 31 August. Storm Ciárán and Eówyn both fell outside this window, but storms do hit in spring and early summer: a severe May gale or a late-August event is not unusual on the Thanet coast. If the storm falls inside the nesting window, the emergency clearance of a genuinely hazardous hanging branch (one that could fall on a person, a vehicle or a structure) is unlikely to be prosecuted where no reasonable alternative exists, but elective clearance of structural breaks and renovation cuts requires a nest check first. Walk the damaged section slowly, watch for adult birds entering and leaving, listen for alarm calls, and if you find an active nest in a damaged section, mark it and leave that section until the brood has fledged. The penalty for an offence is an unlimited fine per nest.

  4. Clear immediate hazards first, using the right tool for each cut.

    Hazardous hanging branches: those still attached to the stem by a strip of bark and under load are the priority. Use a handsaw or loppers for these cuts, not a hedge trimmer. A trimmer blade is not designed to take the lateral force of a branch under bending load; it will bind, kick, or stall, and the release of the branch when it finally cuts free is unpredictable. Work from the safe side (not standing beneath the hanging section), cut the branch free at a point that removes the hanging material and leaves the parent stem intact, and stand clear as it drops.

    For broken stems: cut cleanly just above a bud node or back to the branch collar at the junction with the parent stem. Never leave a ragged stub: a stub more than 25mm long above the nearest bud node dries out and dies back to that node anyway, but the dying stub creates a point of entry for fungal pathogens over the months it takes to die. A clean cut at the right position heals faster and leaves a smaller wound surface.

    Do not attempt to use a chainsaw on a hedge plant without appropriate PPE and training. For large structural damage involving sections thicker than 50mm, call in a professional rather than attempting the cut with domestic equipment.

  5. Make the salvage-or-remove decision section by section.

    Once the immediate hazards are cleared, walk the hedge again and make a deliberate decision on each damaged section before cutting further. The questions to ask:

    • Is there live growth visible on or above the damaged point? If yes, there is a case for cutting back to that live growth and waiting for recovery. If no, the section is likely dead and removal makes more sense than a recovery attempt.
    • Is this species one that reshoot reliably from old wood? (See the species notes below.) For Leylandii or Griselinia, if the damage has reached the brown zone with no live growth visible, there is no recovery. For yew, hawthorn, box or privet, old wood that still has green cambium under the bark will reshoot freely.
    • Is the root system still intact? A section with fully uprooted roots that cannot be re-firmed is a removal candidate even if the above-ground growth looks alive, because it will not recover once the root contact is lost.
    • What is the scale of the gap a removal would create? Removing a 1.5m section of a 10m hedge will leave a visible gap for two to three seasons. That is a manageable outcome, and can be addressed via gapping-up with new plants. Removing sections piecemeal without considering the cumulative gap tends to leave a hedge that looks worse after the remedial work than before.
  6. For wind-scorched evergreens, resist cutting until late March or April.

    This is the step that goes most often wrong in CT10 gardens, and the one I spend the most time explaining after January and February storms. A Griselinia or Escallonia hedge with a fully brown face in February looks dead. In most cases it is not. The brown leaves are desiccated but the stems behind them are still alive, and if you cut back into the green zone in February, you expose fresh cut surfaces to further cold and drying. The plant survives this but the recovery is slower than if you had waited.

    The correct approach: leave the brown material in place through winter. By late March, scratch the bark of the apparently dead stems at several points with a thumbnail. Green tissue under the bark means the stem is alive. New growth buds will begin to swell in April, and by May you can see exactly where the live-dead boundary is. Cut back to just above the first live bud on each affected stem. Do not cut back below that point. In most years, an Escallonia or Griselinia hedge with wind-scorch damage is back to a presentable density by the following September, provided the stems themselves were intact and the root zone was mulched to retain moisture through the following summer.

    Conifers, and Leylandii in particular, behave differently: unlike most broadleaved hedges, Leylandii does not reshoot from old brown wood. If the stem behind the scorched needles shows no green tissue under the bark, that section is dead and will not recover regardless of how long you wait. This is covered in more detail in the Leylandii reduction guide.

  7. Re-firm and stake any partially lifted root sections within 48 hours.

    On the shallow Margate chalk that underlies most of Broadstairs, St Peter's and the North Foreland area, hedge roots cannot penetrate more than 300–600mm before hitting bedrock or the compact chalk subsoil layer. This means a tall hedge on a CT10 site has a relatively small root plate compared to the same species on deep loam. In a strong northeast gale the wind load on that root plate is considerable, and partial windthrow is a common result: the root ball lifts 50–150mm on the leeward side without the stem snapping, leaving the hedge leaning at an angle with visible root plate exposure at the base.

    If you can physically lever or push the root section back into contact with the soil while the stem is still alive, do it. Use a timber lever under the root ball if needed, not the stem itself. Once the root plate is back in contact with the soil, ram the disturbed material firmly back in around it by foot, and then stake the stem immediately. A hardwood stake, 40–50mm diameter, driven at a 45-degree angle away from the hedge on the windward side and tied to the stem with a proper tree tie (rubber collar, not wire or twine that cuts into the bark), holds the stem in position while the roots re-establish contact over the following growing season. Leave the stake in place for a minimum of one full year, two if the windthrow was severe.

    If the root ball cannot be re-firmed because the stem has already dried beyond recovery, or because the lift is so severe that the root plate has separated completely, the section should be removed and the gap addressed by replanting in the autumn.

  8. Feed and mulch the entire root zone after clearance work is complete.

    Storm damage is stressful to the plant beyond the visible physical breaks. Root disturbance, water loss through damaged foliage and the general shock of sudden defoliation on one face all depress the plant's recovery rate. After the clearance cuts are made and any staking is in place, give the full root zone a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser at the label rate for an established hedge. Broadcast it evenly from the stem base out to the drip-line; do not apply it in winter if the ground is frozen, but in late March or April when growth is resuming it makes a measurable difference to the speed of the recovery.

    Apply a 75mm layer of garden compost or composted bark mulch over the root zone, keeping it clear of the stem bases by 50mm. On the free-draining chalk soils typical of CT10, the mulch layer is particularly important in the following summer: Thanet records approximately 612.6mm of annual rainfall at Manston, and summer months can be dry enough that a hedge already stressed by storm damage will struggle to produce new growth without moisture retention at the root zone. The mulch pays for itself over the first growing season after a storm event.

Species recovery notes for Thanet hedges

Not all hedge species respond the same way to storm damage, and the expectation of recovery should be set accordingly when making the salvage-or-remove decision in Step 5.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is among the most resilient species in a CT10 garden for storm damage. It will reshoot reliably from old brown wood provided the stems are still alive underneath (check with the thumbnail test). Even severe structural damage that removes two-thirds of the canopy rarely kills a yew hedge outright. The recovery is slow: full density typically takes three to four years after major damage, but the plant survives and returns. The single exception is a fully uprooted root ball: yew roots do not re-establish from a complete windthrow.

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) and blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) are native species with outstanding storm resilience. Structural breaks can be cut back hard, to near-ground level if necessary, and hawthorn will reshoot vigorously from the stump. This is the basis of the traditional coppice cycle for native hedgerows. Wind scorch is not a significant issue for deciduous species because the leaves are not present during the worst of the northeast winter gales.

Escallonia recovers well from structural breaks and has moderate tolerance for being cut back into old wood, better than Griselinia but worse than yew. Wind scorch is common on the exposed face after a hard February gale, and the waiting-until-April rule applies. A severely scorched Escallonia that still has live stems behind the brown face will be largely recovered by late summer.

Griselinia littoralis is widely planted on the Thanet coast for its salt tolerance, and it does perform well as a windbreak. However, it has limited ability to regenerate from old wood. If structural damage cuts into the zone where there is no live growth visible on the remaining stems, that section will not recover, and the gap should be replanted rather than left. Wind scorch on Griselinia produces brown, papery leaves across the exposed face; wait until April before assessing what can be cut and what must be removed.

Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii) presents the most difficult recovery picture of any common CT10 hedge species. It does not reshoot from old brown wood: once a section shows no green tissue under the bark, it is dead. Wind scorch that browns the outer needles is sometimes temporary, but structural damage that exposes the dead inner zone of a Leylandii is generally irreversible in the affected section. The shallow-chalk root zone also makes Leylandii the species most vulnerable to full windthrow in Thanet gardens: the root system cannot anchor a tall hedge against a sustained northeast gale in the way it could on deeper soil. A Leylandii hedge with multiple windthrown or structurally broken sections after a severe storm is often better replaced than restored.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) both reshoot freely from old wood and are among the easiest hedge species to recover after storm damage. Hard cuts back to a framework are well tolerated. The main risk on laurel is leaving ragged cuts from loppers on the large leaves: browning at cut leaf edges is visible for several months, but the plant itself is not harmed.

The wind-scorch and structural break distinction: why it matters for evergreens

Wind scorch and structural break look similar from the road and are often conflated, but they require opposite responses. Wind scorch is a condition of the leaves, not the stems: the cold northeast wind removes moisture faster than the root system can replace it, the leaves collapse and brown, but the woody growth behind them is still alive and will produce new foliage once conditions improve and the plant is cut back at the right moment. Cutting back in winter removes the dead leaf material but also exposes the live stems behind it to further desiccation and cold, and it does nothing to accelerate the recovery timeline because the new growth cannot start until spring regardless.

Structural breakage is different: a stem that has snapped or split is already dead above the break point, and leaving it in place achieves nothing other than a continued visual problem and a potential entry point for pathogens. These cuts should happen promptly, to the correct position (collar or bud node, not a mid-internode stub).

The practical tell between the two: push your hand through the brown outer face of the hedge and look at the stem you find inside. If it flexes without snapping and shows green tissue when you scratch the bark with a thumbnail, it is alive and wind-scorched only. If it snaps cleanly with no bending, or shows no green tissue under the bark, it is dead and should be cut. This five-second stem check is more informative than the appearance of the outer face.

Margate chalk and shallow roots: why Thanet hedges are more storm-vulnerable than most

The chalk that underlies the majority of CT10 gardens is thin: typically 100–400mm of topsoil over compact or fissured chalk, with bedrock at 400–900mm depending on the specific location. Roots of large hedge plants, particularly Leylandii, tall laurel and mature hornbeam screens, cannot penetrate beyond the chalk layer in the way they could on deep clay or loam soils. The result is a relatively wide, shallow root plate that provides good anchorage in still conditions but is vulnerable to the lateral loading of a sustained gale.

This is why the CT10 pattern of storm damage is different from inland Kent gardens: windthrow (partial or complete root lifting) is more common here than in a garden on Weald clay, and structural breakage at mid-stem is less common. If you are planting a replacement hedge after storm damage, and the replacement species is a large-canopy evergreen, keep the eventual height in mind: a hedge over 2.5 metres on shallow chalk will have a progressively larger windthrow risk as it matures. Species like yew, privet and beech, which have a smaller wind profile for a given height than Leylandii or tall laurel, are better suited to sites that have a history of windthrow.
