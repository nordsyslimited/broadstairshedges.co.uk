Vine weevil in Broadstairs hedges: how to identify grubs and treat with nematodes in CT10.

A hedge that wilts and dies in sections despite adequate watering usually turns out to have one of two causes: honey fungus working through the root zone, or vine weevil larvae eating the roots from the inside out. The two are easy to tell apart: honey fungus produces the characteristic white bootlace mycelium under bark at the base, whereas lifting a wilting vine-weevil-affected plant reveals the C-shaped white grubs, each roughly 1 cm long with a brown head, curled around the remaining root stubs in the soil. Vine weevil is not primarily a hedge pest in the way that aphids and scale insects are, but on the CT10 coastal strip it causes significant damage to the Euonymus, Griselinia, Viburnum tinus and Escallonia hedging that dominates Broadstairs gardens. This guide covers how to identify an infestation, how to time the treatment, and how to apply nematodes correctly in CT10 November conditions.

Steps

Step 1: Look for the indirect signs before searching for grubs

The two life stages of vine weevil cause distinct and recognisable damage, and you are likely to notice one before the other.

Adult notching on leaf margins. Adult vine weevils feed at night from late April through to September, cutting irregular U-shaped notches into the margins of leaves. The notching is not through the leaf but inward from the edge, and the shape is characteristically round-bottomed rather than the angular tear of wind or mechanical damage. On Euonymus japonicus and E. fortunei it is usually the first visible sign of a vine weevil problem; on Griselinia and Escallonia the notching is easier to miss because the leaves are larger and a few notches do not attract immediate attention. The notching itself rarely causes serious harm to an established hedge, but it confirms adult weevils are present and that larvae will follow in the soil.

Wilting, yellowing sections despite adequate moisture. This is the sign of larval root damage and it appears from September onward, as larvae hatched from July and August eggs grow large enough to cause significant root loss. A section of hedge that visibly wilts during a week when neighbouring plants are not under moisture stress, that cannot be explained by drought or waterlogging, should be investigated below the soil surface without delay.

Step 2: Confirm by inspecting the root zone

The quickest way to confirm vine weevil larvae is to take a hand trowel and carefully excavate the top 10 cm of soil at the base of a wilting or suspect plant, working a few centimetres out from the stem. Vine weevil larvae in CT10 soil are normally found within the top 15 cm in autumn and early winter, moving deeper into the chalk subsoil only during harder frosts.

The larvae are unmistakable: plump, legless, creamy white, slightly curved into a C-shape, with a distinctive brown or orange-brown head capsule. Full-grown larvae are 10–12 mm long. Even one or two larvae per plant confirm the pest is present; ten or more per plant represents a heavy infestation that is likely already causing root loss.

To confirm adults, go out after dark on a warm evening in May or June with a torch and check the soil surface and lower stems of the hedging plants. Adult vine weevils are 8–10 mm long, dull black with patches of pale yellowish-brown scales on the wing cases, with a characteristic long, elbowed snout. They have no hind wings and cannot fly. Adults walk, so an infestation spreads along a hedge run from an initial entry point: check the adjacent plants as well as the worst-affected section.

Step 3: Identify which hedge plants are most at risk in CT10

Vine weevil attacks a wide range of garden plants but shows a consistent preference for certain genera. The hedging species used most widely along the Broadstairs seafront, the terraced streets behind Viking Bay, and the clifftop gardens above Stone Bay and Joss Bay include several of the vine weevil's preferred hosts.

  • Euonymus japonicus and E. fortunei. The most susceptible of the common CT10 hedging species. Vine weevil larvae feeding on the fibrous root system can destroy it faster than the plant can replace roots, particularly in the free-draining chalk soil which offers no buffer of organic matter to compensate. A heavy infestation in a first-year Euonymus hedge can kill plants outright by their second winter.
  • Griselinia littoralis. Adult vine weevils do feed on Griselinia leaves, though the large glossy leaf area absorbs notching better than smaller-leafed species. Larval damage is a more significant risk on younger Griselinia than on well-established plants with an extensive root system.
  • Viburnum tinus. Both adult feeding damage and larval root damage occur on Viburnum tinus in Broadstairs gardens, particularly in contained and raised planters where the warmer rooting medium suits vine weevil egg-laying.
  • Escallonia. Adult notching on Escallonia leaves is common. Larval root damage in established Escallonia hedges is less frequent than on Euonymus, but new plantings in their first two years are genuinely vulnerable.
  • Taxus (yew). Vine weevil larvae attack yew roots, particularly in young yew hedges. Established mature yew with deep root systems tolerates larval pressure better than recently planted stock, but a bare-root yew hedge in its first three years should be treated as a risk plant.

Container-grown or pot-staged hedge plants are significantly more susceptible than the same species planted in open ground, because the confined root ball provides dense, warm, moisture-retentive conditions that suit egg-laying and larval development. If you have recently planted hedge specimens that were grown in containers, check their root zones first.

Step 4: Choose the right treatment window for CT10 conditions

The two treatment windows for vine weevil nematodes are autumn (August to November) and spring (March to May). In CT10 Broadstairs conditions, autumn is the stronger of the two.

Autumn treatment: late August to November. Vine weevil larvae in CT10 are at their youngest and most susceptible stage from August to October, when eggs laid in July and August have hatched and the new larvae are small and concentrated in the top few centimetres of soil. Nematodes applied to moist soil during this window intercept the larvae before they cause substantial root damage. In Thanet, soil temperatures at 10 cm depth typically remain above 8°C through most of November on south-facing aspects, making the treatment window longer than it would be on heavier inland soils that cool faster.

Spring treatment: March to May. Larvae that have survived winter are larger, more deeply positioned and closer to pupating by spring. Nematode treatment is less effective at this stage but is still worth applying if an autumn treatment was missed, particularly in a year where vine weevil pressure is high.

Nematode species and temperature: two species are available from UK biological control suppliers for vine weevil. Steinernema kraussei (sold as "Vine Weevil Killer Autumn" by Nemasys and Andermatt) is the correct choice for CT10 November treatment: it is effective at soil temperatures from 5°C, which covers the typical November range in Thanet. Heterorhabditis bacteriophora (or megidis) is better suited to spring/summer treatment when soils are above 12°C. Confirm the soil temperature in your hedge border before ordering: a soil thermometer pushed to 10 cm depth in the morning gives a reliable reading. If the reading is below 5°C, hold the treatment until a mild spell.

Step 5: Prepare the site and apply nematodes correctly

Vine weevil nematodes are live organisms and die quickly if mishandled. Their effectiveness depends entirely on application technique.

  1. Order fresh stock. Nematodes have a short shelf life, typically four to six weeks from production. Order from a UK biological control supplier with reliable cold-chain distribution: Nemasys (Becker Underwood), Andermatt (andermattgarden.co.uk), and Dragonfli (dragonfli.co.uk) are established UK suppliers. Store the pack in the fridge at 2–8°C until use. Do not freeze.
  2. Water the soil thoroughly the day before application. Nematodes move through moist soil; dry chalk soil provides no medium for them to disperse into the root zone. If the hedge border has not received rain for more than a week, water it generously 24 hours before treatment.
  3. Mix and apply in the evening. Nematodes are killed by UV light. Mix the product in a watering can or a knapsack sprayer with the nozzle set to a coarse spray (fine nozzles break up nematodes). Apply as a drench to the base of each plant, covering a radius of 30–40 cm around the stem, working in a systematic run along the hedge. For a 10-metre run of hedge, a 50 million nematode pack (the standard small-garden size) is approximately the right quantity.
  4. Water in after application. Follow the nematode drench with a further thorough watering using plain water to push the nematodes down into the top 10 cm of soil. The target zone for the nematodes to reach is the same layer where the larvae are feeding.
  5. Keep the treated soil moist for two weeks. Nematodes die in drying soil. If November brings a dry period after treatment, water the base of the hedge every three to four days. Mulching over the treated zone immediately after application reduces moisture loss and extends the nematodes' active period in the soil.

Step 6: Inspect and assess plants that show collapse

If one or more hedge plants have already wilted badly by the time the infestation is discovered, dig them up carefully to assess root damage before deciding whether to treat in situ or replace.

Lift the plant gently using a fork, taking as much of the root ball as possible. Shake the soil from the roots over a tray or sheet and look at both the root ball and the extracted soil for larvae. Remove any larvae you find by hand and dispose of them. Inspect the remaining root structure: if two-thirds or more of the fibrous feeder roots are intact, the plant can usually recover if replanted immediately with slow-release fertiliser granules worked into the backfill, then kept well-watered through the following spring. If the root system is largely destroyed, with only the main structural roots remaining and no visible fibrous roots, the plant is unlikely to establish well and replacement is the more reliable option. Apply a nematode drench to the replanting hole before inserting the new plant.

Lifting hedge plants between 1 March and 31 August: the nesting check applies

Vine weevil root damage is sometimes discovered in spring when a hedge plant fails to put on new growth in April or May. If lifting a collapsed or suspect plant between 1 March and 31 August, check carefully for active bird nests at the base and in the lower canopy of the affected section before disturbing the roots or the adjacent plants. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a Schedule 1 bird. Watch the hedge for adult bird activity for a few minutes before you begin, and part the hedge carefully at the collapse point to check the interior before lifting. If you find an active nest, leave the plant and treat the surrounding soil with nematodes from the surface without lifting. The penalty on summary conviction is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, assessed per nest.

Step 7: Reduce the conditions that favour adult vine weevil

Nematodes address the larval population in the soil, but do nothing to prevent adults already present in the garden from laying a new generation of eggs the following summer. Combining the nematode treatment with measures against the adult population improves the outcome over successive seasons.

  • Corrugated cardboard traps on the soil surface. Adults hide under loose material during daylight. Lay corrugated cardboard or small pieces of damp hessian sacking along the base of the hedge. Check under them in the morning from May onward and collect and dispose of any adults you find. This is not a complete control, but it reduces the laying population significantly on a hedge run where adults are established.
  • Sticky banding on container plants. For potted or container-staged hedge plants that are particularly at risk, apply a band of sticky barrier gel (Vaseline applied to a tape band is a reasonable substitute for proprietary products) around the pot rim. Adults walk from pot to pot and from soil into containers; a sticky band stops them climbing in.
  • Avoid planting Euonymus and Griselinia directly against existing infested plants. New hedge plants installed alongside an established vine weevil source will be colonised rapidly. If replacing a dead plant in an infested run, drench the new plant's root ball and the surrounding soil before and after planting.
  • Autumn nematode treatment as a routine on susceptible species. On a hedge run with Euonymus, Griselinia or Viburnum tinus, treating with S. kraussei nematodes every autumn from September to early November is cheaper and more effective than waiting for visible collapse to trigger treatment. A 50 million nematode pack costs around £15–£20 from UK suppliers, far less than replacing dead hedge plants or repairing a collapsed section.

Vine weevil and the CT10 chalk soil

Margate Chalk is the bedrock under the whole of the Thanet peninsula, and the light, free-draining soil it produces above it is a significant factor in vine weevil behaviour in Broadstairs gardens. Chalk-based topsoil warms quickly in spring and drains fast after rain, so soil temperatures in CT10 hedge borders in April and May reach the range that triggers adult weevil activity earlier than they would in the heavier clay-loam soils of inland Kent. This means the egg-laying season starts earlier in Thanet, the larvae reach a treatable size earlier in autumn, and the November treatment window is better than it would be on a colder, wetter site.

The same free-draining soil that makes CT10 gardens relatively good for vine weevil nematode treatment makes the soil dry out fast in summer, which is a stress factor for the hedging plants most susceptible to vine weevil. A Euonymus hedge on chalk that runs short of moisture in June and July grows more slowly and produces less new root material per season, which leaves it less able to recover from the root loss that larval feeding causes. The practical implication is the same as it is for scale insect management on chalk: adequate mulching and watering in the growing season is not just good practice for plant health, it is also part of the pest management approach.
