Salt-wind scorch on coastal hedges in Broadstairs: how to protect, treat and assess in November.

The worst salt-wind damage to CT10 hedges does not happen in August. It happens in January and February, when north-easterly gales off the North Sea drive spray up over the cliff edge and the air temperature drops below zero at the same time. Cold desiccating wind draws moisture out of evergreen foliage faster than the roots can replace it from frozen or near-frozen chalk soil, and the airborne salt compounds the injury by drawing more moisture from the leaf surface and depositing a film that blocks photosynthesis. By January, there is nothing useful left to do. November is the window: before the main battering season, while soil temperatures still allow a mulch layer to settle properly, while anti-desiccant spray can adhere to dry foliage, and while there is still time to put up a temporary windbreak screen on the exposed face. This guide covers the steps I carry out on clifftop Broadstairs hedges before winter begins in earnest.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the hedge and identify the most exposed plants

Start with a full walk of your hedge in daylight, noting which sections face north or north-east and which plants sit within 150 metres of the cliff edge. Those two factors, aspect and proximity to the sea, are the main predictors of winter salt-wind damage in Broadstairs CT10.

On the clifftop above Joss Bay and Stone Bay, the prevailing winter wind is from the NE and N, and salt spray carried on a 40-knot gust can reach 1–2 km inland from the cliff line. The hedges most at risk are on the seaward side of North Foreland Road, along the cliff paths above Stone Bay, on the exposed boundaries behind Viking Bay, and on the north-facing garden boundaries in the streets running down towards Kingsgate. Gardens in central Broadstairs or St Peter's, sheltered by the town or by existing mature hedging, are significantly less exposed, though no CT10 garden is entirely free of salt wind risk in a severe winter.

As you walk, make a mental or written note of three things for each hedge run: the species, the aspect (which cardinal direction the hedge faces), and whether the plants were established more than three growing seasons ago or are more recently planted. Newly planted hedges in their first two winters are always higher priority for intervention than established ones, since their root systems have less reserves to draw on.

Step 2: Check foliage now for early signs of salt-wind browning

Some CT10 hedges will already show the early signs of salt deposition and desiccation stress by mid-November, particularly after any easterly gales in October. Look for three things on the exposed face of each hedge.

Marginal browning on individual leaves. Salt-wind scorch most often starts at the leaf margin and tip, working inward. The brown edge is dry and papery rather than soft and wet, which distinguishes it from frost damage. On Pittosporum and Photinia 'Red Robin', the marginal browning typically appears first on younger leaves at the branch tips; on cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel it appears on the outer surface of the leaf and can cover large areas of the wind-facing side. Griselinia, with its waxy leaf surface, usually shows salt deposition as a dull or slightly sticky film before browning begins. Run your finger across a Griselinia leaf: if the surface feels gritty or slightly sticky on the exposed side, salt has been deposited.

Die-back at branch tips on the wind face. More advanced desiccation shows as wilting or blackening shoot tips, particularly on Pittosporum tenuifolium and on newly planted Escallonia. This is not the same as normal tip-browning from a hard frost; it appears on the wind-facing side of the hedge and is not uniform across the whole plant.

A clearly wind-burned face compared to the sheltered face. Step around to the sheltered side of the hedge and compare the two faces. A healthy, unexposed CT10 hedge has similar foliage colour on both sides in November. A hedge that is already under salt-wind stress has noticeably more browning, dullness or shoot-tip die-back on the exposed face. That difference tells you which sections need priority treatment at Steps 3 and 4.

Step 3: Apply anti-desiccant spray to vulnerable evergreen foliage

An anti-desiccant is a wax-based product that you spray onto the foliage surface to form a thin film, reducing the rate at which cold, dry or salt-laden wind draws moisture from the leaves. It does not prevent all scorch in a severe winter, but it reduces the rate of moisture loss from the foliage and gives the plant more time before the deficit becomes damaging. It is most useful on the hedging species that suffer most from salt-wind scorch: Pittosporum tenuifolium, Photinia 'Red Robin', Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica), Viburnum tinus and Hebe species.

Products are sold by UK garden centres under various trade names, including formulations marketed for use on Christmas trees and newly planted conifers. Look for a "horticultural anti-transpirant" or "anti-desiccant spray" at a farm or garden supplies merchant rather than a supermarket. Avoid products primarily designed for frost protection that work by freezing rather than coating the leaf surface.

Application notes:

  • Apply on a dry, still day when the air temperature is at least 5°C. Spray will not adhere properly to wet foliage, and application in a wind makes even coverage impossible.
  • Spray the exposed face of the hedge first, working from the base upward. The wind-facing surface of the outer shoots is the priority; getting the product onto the undersides of leaves is a secondary aim.
  • Griselinia, Escallonia and Olearia have naturally waxy or coarse foliage that offers more inherent resistance to salt deposition and do not need anti-desiccant treatment routinely. Focus the product on the more tender and large-leafed species listed above.
  • Reapply after any prolonged rain that runs visibly off the foliage, as this will remove the film. One application before December and one in January, in a mild window, is a reasonable programme for a hedge in an exposed clifftop position.

Step 4: Install a permeable windbreak screen on the exposed face of newly planted hedges

For hedge runs planted in the last two growing seasons, a temporary windbreak screen on the exposed (NE or N-facing) side reduces the wind speed at the foliage surface and the salt loading on new leaves. Use 50–70% permeable woven polypropylene windbreak netting, not solid polythene sheeting or close-board fence panels. A solid barrier deflects wind over its top and creates turbulence on the leeward side that can be more damaging than the unbroken wind; a permeable screen reduces wind speed by passing some air through it.

Fix the netting to sturdy stakes (1.5–1.8 m wooden posts or steel angle-iron) driven into the ground on the windward side of the hedge at 2–3 m intervals. Leave a 20–30 cm gap between the netting face and the hedge foliage to prevent the netting abrading the leaves or ice-loading against them directly. The screen should be at least as tall as the hedge.

Remove the screen in April or May once the main winter wind season has passed and the hedge has begun to put on new spring growth. Leaving it in place through summer traps heat, reduces airflow, and can encourage fungal problems inside the hedge.

Established hedges with three or more seasons of growth generally do not need a screen. Their root systems are deep enough to supply leaves through all but the most extreme NE gales, and in CT10 the established coastal hedging species (Griselinia, Escallonia, Olearia, hornbeam, hawthorn) have adapted to the salt exposure. Use the screen where the return on effort is highest: on newly planted Pittosporum, Photinia or Portuguese laurel in a exposed clifftop position.

Step 5: Mulch the root zone to insulate against frost and desiccation

Salt-wind scorch is partly a foliage problem and partly a root problem. If the root zone in a free-draining chalk-based soil freezes, or simply runs dry during a prolonged cold and windy period, the roots cannot replace the moisture that the leaves are losing. The mulch layer at this step addresses both risks.

Lay a 7–10 cm depth of bark mulch or composted wood chip along the full length of the hedge, covering a 40–50 cm band on each side of the stem line. Keep the mulch at least 10 cm clear of each stem at its base to prevent rotting of the bark at ground level. If the soil under the existing hedge is dry to the touch at 10 cm depth (run a hand trowel in to check), water the full length of the hedge border first and allow it to drain for a day before laying the mulch, so that you are trapping moisture into the root zone rather than trapping a dry layer.

On Margate Chalk, the natural topsoil is typically 15–25 cm deep above the chalk bedrock, draining fast in both summer drought and winter freeze-thaw cycles. The mulch layer insulates the topsoil temperature, reducing the chance that a sharp ground frost will freeze the top few centimetres of chalk-based soil and cut off root uptake. It also retains the moisture that the roots need to supply the foliage during the desiccating NE winds of January and February.

Step 6: Mark badly affected plants for spring assessment

If you found sections of hedge at Step 2 that are already showing significant browning or tip die-back, place a garden cane or a piece of brightly coloured tape at each affected plant now. Do not cut the damaged material in autumn or winter.

Leaving the scorched growth in place through winter protects the living tissue underneath it. The dead or browned outer growth acts as a physical buffer for the still-living inner shoots and reduces the rate of moisture loss from those interior sections. Cutting it away in November or December removes that protection and exposes the inner growth to the full force of the winter winds that caused the damage in the first place. The spring cut, done correctly in late March or April, is far more effective and less harmful than cutting in winter.

Mark the plants now so that you do not forget them when the garden is busy again in spring, and so that you can track whether individual plants deteriorate further over winter. If a plant that showed marginal browning in November is showing complete die-back of the wind-facing face by February, that is the evidence that you need to decide between renovation cutting and replacement.

Step 7: Remove damaged growth in late March or April, not before

Once the main frost risk has passed for CT10, which is typically after the spring equinox on 20 March, return to the marked plants and assess each one for the extent of the damage.

To judge whether a shoot is dead or merely dormant, scrape a small section of bark from a twig with a thumbnail or a knife blade. Living tissue shows green under the bark; dead tissue is brown or grey throughout. Work inward from the tip of each shoot until you reach green tissue, then cut back 10 mm beyond that point into definitely living wood. Remove the cut material and dispose of it cleanly.

For a hedge where only the outer face is browned but the interior shoots are green, this is a tidy-up cut that removes a few centimetres from the wind face and is complete in one pass. For a hedge where the browning extends to 20–30 cm depth on the exposed face, treat it as a light renovation cut: cut back to healthy green wood, feed with a slow-release granular fertiliser worked into the mulch at 70 g per square metre, and water during any dry spells in April and May. Most Griselinia, hornbeam, beech and native mixed hedges recover from even a severe salt-wind winter without permanent damage given the spring cut and feeding. Pittosporum and Photinia are more variable: a hedge that has had the same section killed back to the same point for three consecutive winters may not be in the right position and species replacement with a genuinely salt-tolerant alternative is worth considering.

Carrying out the spring cut between 1 March and 31 August: the nesting check

The removal of damaged growth at Step 7 falls within the nesting season for most of its useful window. Before cutting any hedge between 1 March and 31 August, walk the full length of the section at close range and check for active nests. Look for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same part of the hedge and part the outer foliage carefully to look into the interior. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest or disturb a bird of a Schedule 1 species. The penalty on summary conviction is up to six months in prison and an unlimited fine per nest. If you find an active nest in a damaged section, leave that section uncut until the nest is confirmed empty and the young have fledged. This can mean leaving some scorched growth in place until late August, which is not ideal cosmetically but is both legally required and far less damaging to the hedge than prosecuting the cut during active nesting.

Which species are most and least vulnerable in CT10

Knowing the relative vulnerability of your hedging species tells you where to concentrate Steps 3, 4 and 5. This is based on what I observe on CT10 clifftop gardens season to season.

Lower vulnerability (tolerates most CT10 winters without protection): Griselinia littoralis, Escallonia 'Red Hedger' and E. macrantha, Olearia x haastii, hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), field maple (Acer campestre), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus). These species cope with heavy salt loading because their foliage is either naturally waxy, coarse-surfaced or deciduous, and because they have deep root systems able to supply leaves during cold desiccating periods.

Moderate vulnerability (benefit from mulching and monitoring; anti-desiccant worthwhile in exposed positions): Viburnum tinus, Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica), Euonymus japonicus, Hebe species (varies by cultivar), beech (Fagus sylvatica), Choisya ternata. These species can sustain significant browning in a severe winter above Joss Bay or Kingsgate, but they generally break from healthy buds in April and recover cleanly with the spring cut and feed.

Higher vulnerability (priority for all protection steps in exposed positions): Pittosporum tenuifolium, Photinia x fraseri 'Red Robin', cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) in an exposed clifftop position, Griselinia littoralis cultivars with variegated or fine-textured foliage (which are less salt-tolerant than the plain-leafed species). These plants combine large or tender foliage with a moderate root system, and in a severe NE wind winter above the Thanet cliff edge they can suffer enough damage to require significant renovation cutting or replacement if not protected.

The CT10 cliff exposure in context

Broadstairs sits on the east-facing chalk headland at the north-eastern tip of Kent, with the North Sea directly to the east and the Thames Estuary approaches to the north. The sea cliff runs for roughly 3 km from Botany Bay in the north through Kingsgate Bay, Joss Bay, North Foreland and Stone Bay to Viking Bay and the town centre, with Broadstairs Harbour at the foot of the main cliff. The town's conservation area includes the cliff-top streets and the gardens above Viking Bay where some of the site's most exposed hedging stands.

The prevailing winter wind in CT10 is from the south-west, which is relatively mild and salt-free. The damaging winds are the occasional NE gales, typically from November through to March, which blow off the open North Sea without any significant landmass to diminish them before they reach the Thanet coast. These events are not frequent: in an average winter, three to five severe NE gale events occur between November and March. But their combined effect on exposed clifftop hedging is cumulative, and a hedge that survived two seasons without protection can be significantly browned in a single harsh winter.

Gardens on the inland side of the cliff roads, in St Peter's, the Reading Street area, and the Kingsgate hinterland back from the cliff, are substantially less exposed. The hedges on these sites typically receive only the residual salt from spray that has already lost energy crossing the cliff and the coastal buildings. For those gardens, mulching in autumn is a sensible precaution but the full windbreak netting programme is usually unnecessary.
