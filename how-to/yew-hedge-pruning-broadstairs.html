How to prune a yew hedge: timing, technique and renovation for Broadstairs gardens.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is the one conifer you can cut hard into bare brown wood and have it break healthy new growth. I see established yew hedges in Broadstairs gardens, particularly around St Peter's and in the older properties behind Reading Street, that have been standing for eighty or a hundred years and can still tolerate a renovation cut that would kill a Leylandii outright. That resilience is what makes yew the premier formal hedge for CT10: dense dark-green face all year, manageable with a single annual cut, and suited to Thanet's thin chalk soils. This guide covers the step-by-step pruning method, the right timing window, and what to do with a yew hedge that has grown well beyond its intended dimensions.

Steps

Step 1: Walk the hedge and confirm it is healthy before you cut

Yew is resilient but not invincible. Before the annual trim, walk the full length of the hedge and look for any sections showing yellowing foliage, dead patches, or reddish-brown dieback from ground level upward. Phytophthora root rot is the most common serious problem in established yew: it is associated with waterlogged or compacted soils, and while it is less common on CT10's free-draining chalk than on clay sites, yew hedges planted into pockets of imported topsoil can be vulnerable after a wet winter. Any suspect section should be assessed at root level before cutting hard; removing large amounts of foliage from a root-stressed plant accelerates the decline rather than helping the hedge recover.

A healthy yew has dense, dark-green foliage on both the face and the interior of the hedge. Press a gloved hand into the outer face: the foliage should be solid and springy. Sparse interior foliage is usually a light response rather than disease, and will fill back in once the outer face is cut and more light reaches the interior.

Step 2: Choose the right time of year, then check for nesting birds

The traditional pruning window for yew is late July through to the end of September. Burncoose Nurseries, a well-established UK nursery in Cornwall with a long track record on Taxus baccata, recommends September or October as the best single window for the annual cut. Hopes Grove Nurseries, based in the Weald of Kent, identifies August as the traditional month, with trimming possible through into winter. The practical recommendation for CT10 gardens: cut any time from mid-July to the end of September. This window lets the freshly cut surface harden before the frosts of October and November and avoids the main peak of the nesting season, though a nesting check is required whenever you cut between 1 March and 31 August.

A light secondary trim in late May or early June will neaten the spring flush of new growth if you want two tidy cycles per year. Do not cut after October in Kent: a hard frost on freshly cut yew can damage the leading shoots and produce a patchy, dull face the following spring.

Nesting season and yew: the check that must happen before you start

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest, or to disturb a bird on an active nest. Yew provides dense, sheltered nesting habitat: blackbirds, robins, dunnocks and wrens all nest regularly in yew hedges in Broadstairs and St Peter's gardens. Walk the full hedge before cutting any time between 1 March and 31 August, watching for adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section and listening for alarm calls or chick noise from inside the canopy. If you find an active nest, leave that section uncut until the brood has fledged. Penalty for the main offence: an unlimited fine per nest, with up to six months in prison for aggravated cases.

Step 3: Set up safe access and gather tools

Established yew hedges in Broadstairs gardens frequently reach 1.8-2.5m. For hedges up to 2m, sturdy aluminium platform steps or a non-slip stepladder usually provides enough reach to work across the top comfortably from either side. Above 2m, scaffold platform steps with outriggers give a more stable working position and a clearer sightline along the cut than a ladder does. Do not lean a standard ladder directly against a yew: the foliage compresses under the weight but does not hold the ladder base securely.

Tools: a powered hedge trimmer with a 45-55cm blade handles yew well, as the needles are small and the stems are not thick in a regularly maintained hedge. For a formal finish on lower sections or topiary detail, hand shears give better control. Bring a taut string line, two canes or stakes, and a tape measure for setting the top cut level.

Step 4: Set a string line at the target cut height

Measure up from ground level at both ends of the hedge run, drive a cane at each end, and run a taut string line between them at the target cut height. For runs over 8m, add a third stake in the middle to prevent the line sagging: a 5cm sag in the line produces a visible bow in the finished top that is difficult to correct once the cut is made. On sloping ground, run the line parallel to the slope rather than dead level. The string-line method is covered in detail in the cutting technique guide.

Step 5: Trim the sides to a batter angle, working from the base upward

Always cut the sides before the top. The standard approach on a yew hedge is to trim the sides slightly inward as you go up, so that the base is wider than the top by roughly 100-150mm across a typical 1.5-2m face. This batter angle allows full light to reach the lower branches. A yew hedge trimmed with perfectly vertical sides for many seasons will slowly lose its lower foliage as the base is shaded by the widening upper growth, leaving bare lower stems that look poor even though the plant is healthy.

Hold the trimmer blade at a slight inward angle and work upward from the base in a single sweeping pass. Yew responds cleanly to machine trimming without the tearing you get on large-leaf species like cherry laurel: you can move at a steady pace without stopping to clear the blade. After each pass, step back and look along the face for any high or low spots before continuing to the next section.

Step 6: Cut the top

Work from one end of the hedge to the other with the trimmer blade held horizontally just below the string line. Sweep the blade forward along the cut plane in long, even strokes rather than pushing down into the top growth: this produces a cleaner, denser finished face. Yew cut at the top will push new growth from the cut surface within a few weeks in warm conditions. On a well-maintained hedge cut annually, the new growth typically fills in within four to eight weeks and the top presents as a solid face by the following season.

Free-hand trimming of the top on a long hedge run inevitably drifts. Do not skip the string line: the time taken to set it is repaid by a level result that would take much longer to re-cut if the line varies by even 30-40mm along the run.

Step 7: Collect and dispose of all clippings carefully

All parts of yew except the soft red flesh of the berry arils are highly toxic to mammals, including dogs, horses and cattle. Fresh clippings scattered on a lawn or compost heap are a genuine hazard if animals access the garden. Bag all clippings in garden waste sacks immediately and dispose of them via the kerbside green bin collection or the Thanet District Council household waste recycling centre on Poorhole Lane, Broadstairs. Do not compost yew clippings at home.

Also clear clippings from the top of the hedge before they settle and begin to decompose against the freshly cut surface. Wet clippings packed against new tissue can create damp pockets that encourage fungal growth, particularly in the autumn conditions typical of the Thanet coast after the late-season cut.

Why yew is different from other conifers

The most important thing to know about yew is that it shares a key property with deciduous shrubs rather than with Leylandii and most other conifers. Most conifers have a green zone: cut back past the outermost foliage into the brown woody interior and nothing regrows from the exposed wood. Yew is the exception. Taxus baccata carries dormant adventitious buds throughout its woody tissue, and cutting hard back into apparently bare wood will trigger new growth from those buds within one or two growing seasons.

The RHS confirms this directly: yew responds well to hard pruning and will regenerate from old wood. Hopes Grove Nurseries, in Kent, advise that established yews can tolerate cutting back to within a few centimetres of very old woody stems. This is what has made yew the traditional choice for formal topiary and long-lived estate hedging across Kent and the south-east for centuries. A Leylandii hedge that has gone five years without attention may need staged reduction over two or three seasons before it looks presentable; a yew hedge in the same situation can be cut back hard and will return to a presentable green face in a single growing season.

Yew on the Broadstairs chalk coast

Taxus baccata is native to the chalk downs of south-east England and occurs naturally on the North Downs, which feeds into the chalk plateau underlying Broadstairs, St Peter's and the coastal strip from Kingsgate to North Foreland. The Margate Chalk formation that runs under CT10 is chemically well-matched to yew's preferred free-draining, alkaline soil: yew does not enjoy waterlogging and actively benefits from the drainage chalk provides.

Salt wind is the variable that catches people out on the Thanet coast. Front-line sites directly exposed to the prevailing south-westerlies above Stone Bay, Joss Bay and Kingsgate will desiccate yew foliage in a hard winter gale. I do not plant yew as the primary windbreak on a fully exposed clifftop site: Escallonia, Olearia or Griselinia are better suited to the front line. Yew works well in a second row, sheltered from the direct salt blast, where it provides the dense formal backdrop that salt-tolerant front-line species are too loose-growing to give. Properties in St Peter's, behind the clifftop ridge, and in the sheltered gardens of Reading Street and the two conservation areas are well within yew's comfort zone without any additional windbreak. The coastal species guide covers the full matrix of front-line and second-line planting for Thanet sites.

On thin chalk, do not plant yew directly into solid chalk without breaking out the planting pit and backfilling with a minimum 400mm of topsoil and organic matter. The chalk drainage is an advantage; solid chalk with no organic content is not. Established yew hedges in CT10 gardens generally self-manage on the chalk once past the first two or three seasons, and are notably more drought-tolerant once established than beech, which sulks in very thin chalk.

Renovating an overgrown yew hedge

If a yew hedge has been left uncut for five or more years, or has grown significantly beyond its intended dimensions, renovation is still achievable because of the hard-cutting property described above. The staged approach used by professional contractors:

In the first winter (November to February, outside the nesting season and the main growing season): cut one side hard back to the main structural stems, leaving the other side untouched. The cut face will look stark and bare immediately after the cut. By the following spring, dormant buds along the woody stems will begin to break. By mid-summer, a thin but even flush of new green growth will have appeared across the cut face. By the end of the second growing season after the cut, the face will be largely covered in growth again and will accept a normal trim.

In the second winter: cut the other side back in the same way. The plant then has one established recovering face and one newly cut face, and the root system is not put under pressure by simultaneous hard cuts on both sides.

Top reduction can be done at the same time as the first side cut, or staged over two seasons if the height reduction is more than 50% of total height. Unlike the sides, a hard top cut on yew typically produces visible new growth within the same growing season as the cut. Allow 60-90cm of new growth on the top before trimming back toward the target finished height. The renovation approach for a range of hedge species is covered in the overgrown hedge renovation guide.
